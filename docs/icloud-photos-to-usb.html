Save iCloud photos to USB from Android

Your photos in iCloud are files in Apple's cloud, not files on your phone. Download them first — iCloud.com in a browser writes a real file into Downloads — then copy that file to a USB drive, no computer.

November 25, 2026 · 13 min read

TL;DR: A photo in iCloud is not on your phone yet. iCloud Photos is Apple's cloud photo library: you can see the photo, open it, and share it from iCloud.com in a browser — and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you download it. There is no iCloud app for Android — Apple makes none, and its system-requirements page lists browsers for Mac and PC only — so the route is Chrome on your Android phone: open iCloud.com/photos, sign in with your Apple Account, tap Select, tap the photos, tap the More button, choose Download, and the files land in the phone's Downloads folder as ordinary files. That is the whole first half of the job, and it is the half every other guide skips: the generic Android-to-USB guides start from photos already on the phone, and the iCloud guides that do exist stop at the phone — download to the phone, done — or route the whole job through a computer, which is the opposite of what you asked for. From Downloads the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the downloaded iCloud photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: the download is in the most compatible format for your device unless you say otherwise, so a HEIC photo arrives as a JPEG and a video as MP4 unless you pick Unmodified Originals in More Download Options first; a Live Photo arrives as two files, a still and a .MOV, not one; a downloaded photo's taken date survives only if the file's EXIF Date Taken survives the download, and the file's modified date will be the download date either way, so it may copy in download-date order — save one photo and check where it lands in the gallery's date order before you download a thousand; you can select up to 1,000 photos at a time, and there is no download-the-whole-library button on the phone web; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. As of November 25, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated iCloud-photos-to-USB how-to with no computer: the results are download-to-phone guides that stop at the phone, a computer route for the whole library, and generic USB-copy guides that never mention iCloud at all — and one of them, rottenwifi, routes iCloud through a computer explicitly.

The confusion first: iCloud is not Google Photos — and not Google Drive either

iCloud sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is iCloud versus Google Photos. They are not two names for the same cloud. iCloud Photos is a photo library. You back up your iPhone's camera roll there, and the photos live in a library organised by date, album, and face, with editing and search built in. Google Photos is also a photo library — the same kind of thing, run by a different company. They are not two views of the same thing. They are two different libraries, and a photo in one is not in the other unless you put it there too. If you open iCloud.com and cannot find a photo you are sure you have, that is the first thing to check: it may be in Google Photos, not iCloud, and this article's steps do not apply to it. The Google Photos route — download from the Photos app, then copy to USB — is a different job, covered by the live2tech guide and the tecnobits guide, and it is not this article. This article covers photos stored in iCloud Photos only.

The second confusion is iCloud versus Google Drive, and it is subtler, because the names sound like the same kind of thing and they are not. Google Drive is a file store. You put files in folders — documents, PDFs, spreadsheets, and photos as files — and you open them from the Drive app, a browser, or any signed-in device. iCloud Photos is a library, not a file store: your photos live in a library, organised by date and album, and there is no folder of files to browse. The route is a different shape for each, and the guides that confuse them will send you down the wrong menu. Drive says Download from a file's three-dot menu, and the file lands in Downloads. iCloud says Download from the Photos web app's More button, after you Select the photos — and the menu wording is close enough to look the same while the thing behind it is different: a library photo, not a file in a folder. Our own Google Drive article covers the Drive half of it. This article uses iCloud's words, in iCloud's order, so the menu you see matches the menu this article describes.

The third confusion hides inside iCloud itself, between the two ways a photo gets there. One way is the iPhone camera roll: your iPhone backed up the photos automatically, and they live in your iCloud Photos library. The other way is upload: you, or someone, put a file into iCloud Photos from a computer — dragged it into iCloud.com/photos in a browser, or uploaded it from the Photos app on a Mac. Both land in the same library, and the download steps in this article apply to both exactly the same way, because a library photo is a library photo whatever put it there. The difference is only what you will find when you open the library: a photo your iPhone backed up is in the library by date, and a photo someone uploaded from a computer is in the library by its own date too — the library does not keep the two apart. Search by date first — the library is organised by date, and the date is the fastest way to find a photo — and let the library tell you whether the photo is there at all.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still in the cloud. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your iCloud account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into iCloud either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no cloud account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of the cloud and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a download. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

One more thing to settle before the steps: the photos you download are yours in the plainest sense, with one condition. A photo downloaded with Unmodified Originals is the same file that was captured — same pixels, same resolution, same format — because that option downloads the format as captured or imported, Apple's own words. A photo downloaded with the default, most-compatible format is the same picture re-encoded into a format your device handles best — a JPEG instead of a HEIC, an MP4 instead of an H.265 video — same picture, different file. Copy the file and everything worth keeping lives inside the file itself, byte-for-byte, in the format you chose. That is the honest centre of this article: an iCloud photo is an ordinary image file once it is downloaded, and the only traps are the download — the difference between a file on your phone and a photo in your account, the format the download arrives in, and the date that may or may not survive it. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the save-one-and-check rule, because for iCloud the date problem is a format problem, and the format is a choice you make before you tap Download.

The wall: there is no iCloud app for Android — the browser is the app

Apple makes no iCloud app for Android. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. Search the Play Store for iCloud and you will find third-party apps with iCloud in the name, and none of them is Apple's. Apple's own system-requirements page lists the browsers iCloud.com supports, and it lists them per platform — on Mac: Safari 13.1 or later, Firefox 128 or later, Google Chrome 130 or later, Opera, or Microsoft Edge 130 or later; on PC: Firefox 128 or later, Google Chrome 130 or later, or Microsoft Edge 130 or later — and Android is not on the list at all. The page that describes web-only iCloud access says the same thing in shorter form: iCloud.com supports the latest versions of Safari, Firefox, Chrome, Edge, and Opera. No Android app, no Android browser on the list, no native route of any kind.

And yet the browser route works on Android, and it works well enough that the guides that tested it say so plainly. Android Authority put it in one line — "There is no iCloud app for Android. You need to access it from a browser." — and then tested the whole flow on a Google Pixel 8a running Android 15 with Chrome 134: open Chrome, go to iCloud.com, sign in with your Apple ID, tap Photos, and the library is there — view, download, and share photos from iCloud just fine from the beginning, in their words. SlashGear gives the same steps — on any desktop or mobile browser, log in to iCloud.com and tap Photos, then Select, tap the images, tap the three-dot menu, tap Download — and MacPaw gives the same route with the same sign-in, including the two-factor step: if you have not signed in from this device before, tap Allow on your Apple device and enter the code on the Android phone. Apple's own download page is the authority behind all three: "In a web browser on any device, you can find your iCloud Photos at iCloud.com/photos after you sign in with your Apple Account."

So the honest statement is two-sided, and this article states both sides instead of picking one. Officially, Apple does not list Android browsers as supported — the system-requirements page names Mac and PC browsers only. In practice, Chrome on Android opens iCloud.com, signs in, and downloads photos, tested on a Pixel 8a on Android 15. That is a tested route, not a supported one, and the difference matters if something goes wrong: if the sign-in stalls, or a button does not appear, the first fix is the one Android Authority names — tap the three-dot menu and select Desktop site, which switches the page to computer mode and exposes the fuller set of controls. The second fix is the browser itself: use the latest Chrome, because Apple's list starts at Chrome 130, and an old browser is the likeliest reason a supported page misbehaves on an unsupported platform. Do not install a third-party iCloud app to get around a browser hiccup. The gbtimes guide warns about exactly that, in words worth quoting: Apple does not officially endorse any such apps, and providing your Apple ID credentials to unknown apps poses a significant security risk. The browser is the app, and the browser is enough.

There is one more account detail worth settling before the steps, because it decides whether the download works at all. If your Apple Account was created on the web without an Apple device — a web-only iCloud account — Apple caps it at 1 GB of storage, and some features are missing: Apple's own web-only page says so, that web-only iCloud accounts have a 1 GB storage limit, and that to get the full set of iCloud features you sign in on an iPhone, iPad, Mac, or Apple Vision Pro. Most readers of this article have an iPhone, or had one, so their account is a full account and the cap does not apply. If you have never owned an Apple device and your account is web-only, the 1 GB cap is the reason your library is smaller than you expected, and no download step will change it. Check the account before you blame the browser: sign in at iCloud.com, open Settings, and look at the storage bar.

So the rule for the whole article, stated once and used everywhere: the browser is the only Apple route, Downloads is where the files land, and the format is a choice you make before you tap Download. If you are ever unsure whether a download worked, open the Files app and look in Downloads. If the photo is there, you downloaded it. If it is not, you did not — whatever the browser seemed to confirm. And one detail that trips people up before they even reach the menu: sign in first, then tap Photos. The iCloud.com home screen shows shortcuts to Mail, Photos, Drive, Notes, and Find My, and the Photos shortcut is the one this article uses — do not tap Drive looking for photos, because Drive holds files, not your photo library, and your photos are not there.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with Chrome installed — the latest version, because Apple's list starts at Chrome 130 — and signed in to nothing in particular: the sign-in that matters is the Apple Account, on iCloud.com, not a Google account. Your Apple Account credentials, and a trusted Apple device nearby for the two-factor code, because the first sign-in from the Android phone will ask for one. Enough free space on the phone to hold the downloads: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, download in batches and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the download steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a download guide.

One more check, on the iCloud side: open iCloud.com/photos in the browser and confirm the photos are actually there. Search for one by date. If the library shows it, it is an iCloud photo and the steps below apply. If the library does not show it, check the other account — sign out and sign in with the other Apple Account, because iCloud shows you one account's library at a time — and then check Google Photos, because the photo may be a library photo in Google's cloud, not Apple's, and the Photos download is a different menu in a different app. Do not download first and diagnose later: a download of the wrong photo is a waste of the phone's storage and your time, and a download of a photo that was never in iCloud is impossible, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake.

Method 1: Download one photo, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open Chrome on your Android phone. Go to iCloud.com/photos and sign in with your Apple Account — enter the password, and if the phone asks for a two-factor code, tap Allow on your trusted Apple device and type the code in. Tap Photos on the iCloud home screen if it does not open directly. Tap Select, then tap the photo you want. Tap the More button — the three dots — and choose Download [number] Items. Or, before you tap Download, tap More Download Options: that is where the format choice lives, and the format choice is the step that decides what file you get. Then tap Download, and wait for the browser to finish — a photo that is still downloading is a half-written file, and a half-written file is not a backup of anything. Then open the Files app, look in Downloads, and confirm the photo is there: the file name should be plausible, and the size should be plausible for a photo, not a few kilobytes.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the Downloads folder is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo in Downloads is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of Downloads, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where Chrome put it. The default run, described in the next section but one, reads the media index with no folder filter, and Downloads is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the file in iCloud if you ever need to match them up again.

The format choice deserves its own paragraph, because it is the one step in this method that changes what you get, and it is the step most guides skip. Apple's download page lists three options under More Download Options: Unmodified Originals, which is the format as captured or imported; Highest Resolution, which is the best quality for your device and typically HEIC or H.265; and Most Compatible, which is JPEG or MP4/H.264. Apple's user guide adds the default: photos and videos are downloaded in the most compatible format for the device you are currently using. That means if you tap Download without choosing, you get the most-compatible file — a JPEG, not the HEIC your iPhone captured, and an MP4, not the H.265 video. If the format matters — if you want the original file, or the taken date matters, which the date section covers — tap More Download Options and choose Unmodified Originals before you tap Download. The choice is per download, not a setting, so you make it every time you download a batch.

Once the photo is in Downloads, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the downloaded iCloud photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the download batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the album of twenty from a trip, the scans someone sent you. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand three-tap downloads is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For an album full of photos, use Method 2, which empties a whole album in one select-all pass. For a whole iCloud library, use a computer — iCloud.com on a computer, select all, download as a ZIP you copy to the drive — and accept that the whole-library job is the one job in this article that genuinely needs a computer. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

Method 2: A whole album — the select-all workaround (and the 1,000-at-a-time limit)

iCloud.com on the phone has no download-the-whole-library button. That is not a guess: Apple's own download page gives a limit instead of a button — you can select up to 1,000 at a time — and the page describes selecting photos, not selecting a library. There is no menu item that downloads an album in one tap, no setting that downloads a library, and no batch command in the phone web that empties a library at once. The workaround is selection: open the album, or open the Library view and scroll to the date you want, tap Select, tap the photos to add them — tap each one, or tap the first and drag across the rest — then tap the More button and choose Download [number] Items. Every selected photo downloads. It is a handful of taps for a whole album instead of three taps per photo, and it is the difference between a feasible job and a tedious one.

There are two catches, and both are worth knowing before you tap Download on fifty files. The first is the limit itself. Apple caps a selection at 1,000 photos at a time. For an album of fifty, that is no limit at all. For a library of ten thousand, it is ten passes at least — and ten passes of selecting, downloading, waiting, and checking is a job that will not get done on a phone, which is why the honest advice for a whole library is the computer route: on iCloud.com on a computer, select all, download, and it arrives as a ZIP — unzip it first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives. Do not spend an evening tapping through a library to prove a point. The phone route is for albums and handfuls; the computer route is for libraries and archives. Knowing which job you have is the skill, and the skill is worth more than any single workaround.

The second catch is that the download may arrive as one ZIP file instead of separate photos, and Apple does not say which it will be. Apple's phone-flow page says "Choose Download [number] Items" and says nothing about the package: no ZIP, no separate files, no mention at all. One independent guide says the photos will be downloaded as individual files or within a ZIP archive, and if it is a ZIP you will need to extract the contents. Apple's own archive page, for iPhone and iPad, says the multi-photo download arrives as a .zip file — but that is the iOS Files-app flow, where the ZIP lands in iCloud Drive, not the Android browser flow, so it is evidence about the shape, not proof about your phone. This article does not claim it either way, because stating it either way would be a guess. The check is the same either way: after the download, open Downloads and look. If you see separate .jpg or .heic files, the download worked as separate files. If you see one .zip, extract it in Files first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives. The check costs ten seconds, and it is the check that catches the case either way.

One more detail on the multi-select pass, and it is a difference from the Google Drive route that is easy to get wrong if you have read our Drive article first. On Drive, selecting several photos at once may hand you a single ZIP file instead of separate photos, and the article warns you to unzip it before backing up. For iCloud, no source this run says the phone web zips a multi-select download, and this article does not claim it does or does not — the behaviour is unreported, and stating it either way would be a guess. What the sources do say is that you can select up to 1,000 at a time, and that the download button downloads the selection. The check is the same either way: after the download, open Downloads and look. If you see separate files, the download worked as separate files. If you see one .zip, extract it in Files first. The one route that definitely produces a ZIP is the computer route: on iCloud.com on a computer, selecting a large set and choosing Download gives you a ZIP, and the guides say so — so if you ever take the computer route for a library, unzip before you copy to the drive.

There is also a question readers ask at this point that deserves a straight answer: can you download a shared album — a family album, a photographer's delivery — the same way? Yes. A shared album is still an iCloud Photos album, and the download steps still apply: open the album, tap Select, tap the photos, tap More, choose Download, and the files land in Downloads like any other. But they are not your photos, and that matters in two ways. First, the owner can remove your access, and a photo you have not downloaded yet is a photo you may lose — so download shared photos promptly, and do not assume they will be there next month. Second, the download is one-way, as the format note says: changes to the downloaded copy do not flow back to the shared album. The USB copy is yours once it is on the drive, and it stays yours even if the share is revoked — which is, after all, the point of a backup.

Method 3: Download straight to the drive from Chrome's save picker

There is a shorter route than Downloads, and it is worth trying first, because if it works it skips the second half of the job entirely. Chrome on Android can ask you where to save each file: open Chrome, tap the three dots, go to Settings, then Downloads, and turn on Ask where to save files — and with that switch on, every download opens a picker where you choose the folder. A USB drive plugged into the phone is a storage location too, so it may appear in the same picker: plug the drive in before you start the download, turn the switch on, and look for the drive in the picker. If it is there, pick it, and the photo lands on the drive directly — no Downloads, no second copy, no SnapDisk run needed for that photo at all.

The honest limit on this method is that the direct pick is reported for SD cards and untested for USB drives on this phone. The guides that document the setting say Chrome offers a Download location choice between internal storage and a microSD card, and that the Ask-where-to-save toggle is the per-download picker — and no source this run shows a USB drive in that picker. The picker is Android's storage picker, and a mounted USB drive shows up in that picker in the ordinary case — the Files app lists it under Storage devices, and the picker draws from the same mounted volumes — but "in the ordinary case" is not a test, and this article does not claim a test it has not run. So the direct route is stated as a try-first, not as a promise: if the drive appears in the picker, use it; if it does not, download to Downloads and copy from there, which is the route the rest of this article proves. The two routes are not rivals. The direct route is the shortcut, and Downloads is the road that always works.

If the drive does not appear in the picker, check the mount before you blame the picker: open Files by Google and look under Storage devices. If the drive is not listed there, the phone has not mounted it, and no picker will show it — check the adapter, the cable, and the drive format first. If the drive is listed in Files but not in the picker, the picker is only showing internal storage, and the Downloads route is the answer, not a different setting. Do not spend the evening in the picker's settings: one look, and then download to Downloads.

One more thing about the direct route, because it changes how you should think about the batch. Downloading straight to the drive writes one photo at a time, through the browser, with no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-download, and no verification that every file actually arrived. It is the right route for a handful of photos, and it is the wrong route for a hundred, because a hundred one-by-one downloads is the same evening of tapping as Method 1, with less feedback. For an album, download the batch to Downloads first — one select-all pass — and then let SnapDisk copy the batch to the drive in one run, with progress, pause, and a size check on every file. The direct route saves a step for one photo. The Downloads route saves an evening for an album. Use each one for the job it fits.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a downloaded iCloud photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a downloaded photo in the Downloads folder copies with the rest of the library. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or anywhere else in shared storage.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: download the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery — in the Download album, or in the camera roll if you used a save-to-gallery route — it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in Downloads: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered in the uploaded-versus-camera-upload section below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into iCloud. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask iCloud for anything. A photo still in the cloud is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still in the cloud is invisible to the file manager. The download is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at iCloud; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up iCloud photos without downloading them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in Downloads came from iCloud, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the iCloud route, the Drive route, the Dropbox route, the OneDrive route, the WhatsApp route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the download that puts it there.

The date problem: a downloaded photo shows the download date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo downloaded from iCloud may show the download date, not the date the photo was taken — and whether it does depends on the format you picked before you tapped Download. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a downloaded file is the download time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a downloaded photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you downloaded it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

Whether the taken date survives depends on the format, and this is where the format choice from Method 1 comes back. Unmodified Originals downloads the file as captured or imported — Apple's own words — which keeps the file's EXIF data, including Date Taken, intact inside the file. That is the expected case for an originals download: the taken date is inside the file, the media index reads it, and the photo sorts by its taken date. The default download, Most Compatible, re-encodes the photo into a JPEG and the video into MP4, and a re-encode is the step most likely to rewrite the metadata — the new file is written by the download, and its taken date may be the download date, or it may be gone. This article states that as the expected case, not as a certainty, because Apple does not say what the re-encode does to EXIF: the download page lists the three format options and says nothing about metadata, and no source this run tested the taken date after a most-compatible download. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: download one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you download a thousand of them.

There is one way in which iCloud is better than the other clouds on the date, and it is worth stating fairly. No source this run says iCloud rewrites the taken date when the photo is uploaded — iCloud Photos stores the original file, and the upload is not the step that loses the date. That is the opposite of OneDrive, where Microsoft's own support forum says the taken date can be replaced by the upload date, especially by camera upload or a bulk upload, before you ever tap Save. For iCloud the risk is at the download, not at the upload, and the risk is a choice — the format — not a property of the cloud. Pick Unmodified Originals, and the taken date has its best chance. Pick the default, and you are betting the re-encode keeps it.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. iCloud lets two photos in different albums share a name — "IMG_0001.heic" in the 2019 album and "IMG_0001.heic" in the 2021 album — and when you download both to the same Downloads folder, the second download either overwrites the first or arrives as "IMG_0001 (1).heic", depending on the browser and the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the file in iCloud if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to download one album at a time and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next, or to rename the files in Downloads first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to download.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Download the 2019 album, back up Downloads, clear Downloads, download the 2021 album, back up Downloads again: the drive ends up with the albums in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

Live Photos and HEIC: what actually downloads

A Live Photo is not one file. It is a still image and a short video, paired, and the download gives you both — as two separate files. Three independent guides agree on that, and they agree in the same words: the still image and a .MOV video file. TinyGrab states it directly — Live Photos will be downloaded as a still image and a .MOV video file — and again in its FAQ, that they will be downloaded as two separate files, a still JPEG image and a .MOV video file. The Windows Club says the same for the iCloud.com download — it will download and save both JPEG and MOV files on your PC — and Windows Report says the same, that downloading via iCloud will save both JPEG and MOV files. Apple's own download page does not spell this out: it lists the format options and says nothing about Live Photos splitting. So this article states the split with the attribution it has — three guides, no Apple line — and tells you to check Downloads for both files after the download, because the check is what proves it on your phone.

Both parts copy, and both parts are in the code's extension lists. The still is an image — .jpg, .jpeg, or .heic — and the image list keeps .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl. The motion is a video — .mov — and the video list keeps .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi. A Live Photo downloaded as a still and a .mov is two files the default run sees, and both copy to the drive. What does not survive is the pairing: on the drive they are two files with the same name stem and different extensions, and no phone gallery will play them back as one Live Photo, because the Live Photo is an Apple pairing, not a file format. The still is a photo. The .mov is a three-second video. Both are backed up. Neither is a Live Photo any more.

HEIC needs its own paragraph, because it is the format most iPhone photos arrive in, and it is the format the default download quietly changes. An iPhone captures in HEIC unless you changed the setting, and iCloud stores the HEIC. Download with Unmodified Originals and the file stays HEIC — the format as captured. Download with the default, Most Compatible, and the file arrives as a JPEG — the same picture, re-encoded, in a format every Android gallery opens. The HEIC file still copies: .heic is in the image list, the media index holds it, and the default run copies it like any other photo. But a HEIC file on an Android phone is a file some galleries preview and some do not, and this article does not claim a preview it has not tested — the copy is proven by the code, the preview is a property of the gallery, and the two are different claims. If the gallery does not show the HEIC, open the file once in the Files app, or use the From picker route, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all — code-supported, untested on this exact folder, in the same form the series has used since WL-246. And if you want the JPEG instead, do not convert the HEIC on the phone: download again with Most Compatible, and the JPEG is the file you get.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to Downloads, select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest, a middle one, and the most recent download. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against Downloads on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and Downloads holds the photos plus whatever else was already there — but the photo count should match the number you downloaded, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second download overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from Downloads before the backup ever saw it. Download one album at a time and copy each batch before downloading the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the iCloud batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a downloaded photo may show the download date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure — if you downloaded with Unmodified Originals, expect the taken date; if you downloaded with the default format, expect the download date. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the download, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, download that photo again with Unmodified Originals, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in the re-encode, not at upload — and no download route will recover it, because the file iCloud holds is the original, and the file you downloaded is the copy.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Apple Account password to a third-party iCloud app. Apple makes no iCloud app for Android, and no app in the Play Store with iCloud in the name is Apple's. The guides warn about this in plain terms — Apple does not officially endorse any such apps, and giving your Apple ID credentials to an unknown app is a significant security risk — and the warning is worth repeating here because the Play Store search is the first thing a reader tries, and the first result looks official. The browser is the route. Use it.

Do not download the whole iCloud library to the phone. The phone does not have room for it, and the download will fail partway through with a storage error, leaving a half-downloaded set that is worse than nothing, because you will not know which photos arrived and which did not. Download in batches — one album at a time, or fifty photos at a time, inside the 1,000-at-a-time limit — copy each batch to the drive, and delete the batch from Downloads before starting the next. The batch size is set by the phone's free space, not by your patience: leave at least a gigabyte free at all times, because Android itself needs room to work, and a phone with zero free space stops downloading, stops copying, and stops telling you why.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not download with the default format if the taken date matters. The default download is the most compatible format for your device — a JPEG, not the HEIC your iPhone captured — and the re-encode is the step most likely to rewrite the metadata. If the date matters, tap More Download Options and choose Unmodified Originals before you tap Download. It is one extra tap, and it is the tap that keeps the original file.

Do not download straight to the drive for a whole album. The direct route from Method 3 writes one photo at a time with no progress detail and no verification, and a hundred one-by-one downloads is an evening of tapping with nothing to show for it until the last one. Download the batch to Downloads first, then let SnapDisk copy the batch in one run. The direct route is the shortcut for one photo. The Downloads route is the road for an album.

Do not trust the cloud as the only copy. iCloud is a store, not a backup: files in iCloud can be deleted, accounts can be closed, shares can be revoked, and storage quotas can be exceeded, and any one of those turns a photo in iCloud into a photo that is gone. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the cloud is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
