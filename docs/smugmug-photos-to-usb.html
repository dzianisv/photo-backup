Save SmugMug photos to USB from Android

Your photos on SmugMug live on SmugMug's servers, not on your phone. Save each one in the app — it lands in the Pictures folder — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 14 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see on SmugMug is not on your phone yet. SmugMug is a photo library that lives on SmugMug's servers, not a folder on your phone: you can see the photo, open it, bookmark its gallery, and share its link, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you save it. In the SmugMug app on Android, open the photo, tap the three-dot overflow menu, and tap Save to Device, and the photo lands in your phone's Pictures folder — or, from a gallery view, tap Save Gallery to Device and the whole gallery saves at once. That is the whole first half of the job, and it is the half every other guide skips: the generic Android-to-USB guides start from photos already on the phone, and the SmugMug guides that do exist stop at the phone — save to the phone, done — with no next step to a drive. From the Pictures folder the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the saved SmugMug photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: SmugMug saves nothing by itself — no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and the app's automatic feature runs the other way, uploading your phone photos to SmugMug, not downloading galleries to your phone; a bookmark keeps the gallery inside your account, not a file, so if the photographer deletes the gallery your bookmark points at nothing; a saved photo sorts by the day you saved it, not the day it was taken, because SmugMug serves you its own copy and the taken date does not survive it; the saved copy is SmugMug's served copy, and the original lives behind the download-size setting the gallery owner chose; the gallery owner can switch downloads off, and then there is no Save to Device to tap; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The confusion first: SmugMug is a photo library on someone else's server — and Save to Device is not a bookmark

SmugMug sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. SmugMug is a photo library — folders, galleries, a site organised the way a photographer organised it — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like a library, it behaves like a library, and it is not your library. It is a library on SmugMug's servers, and the photos in it are files that live there, not on your phone. A photo library on your phone — the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. SmugMug shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your SmugMug account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into SmugMug either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no social account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of SmugMug and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a save. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion SmugMug's own design invites. SmugMug is where photographers keep their work, and the account feels like an archive — your galleries, your folders, your site, all in the order you put them in, all yours. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy on someone else's server, under someone else's account rules, and it is a paid service — Basic, Power, Portfolio, and Pro plans, no free account — which means the photos sit behind a subscription as well as behind an account. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits in an account you do not control is a photo at risk twice over. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the feed is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

The third confusion hides inside SmugMug itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. Tapping the overflow menu and toggling on Bookmark Gallery adds the gallery to your app's Bookmarks screen, where you can find it later. It is the fastest option, and it is not a download: you are keeping the gallery, not the files. You cannot send a bookmarked photo in a chat as a file, you cannot copy it to a drive, and if the photographer deletes the gallery, the bookmark points at nothing — the photos are gone, and the thing you kept was a pointer to them. Sharing a gallery's link is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the gallery remaining available. Marking a gallery for offline viewing keeps it available in the app when you have no Wi-Fi, but offline viewing is still the app's own storage, not a file in your Gallery: it is SmugMug's cache, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and gone when the app's data is cleared. The only tap that writes a file is the overflow menu's Save to Device — or Save Gallery to Device from a gallery view — and if you have been bookmarking galleries and wondering why none of the photos are on your phone, this is why: you have been keeping galleries, and kept galleries are not files.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on SmugMug's servers. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The save is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the save is a step you have to take yourself, because SmugMug offers no save-them-all setting and no auto-save. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the save-one-and-check rule, because for SmugMug the date problem is a save problem: the file is written when you save it, and the day you saved it is the day it sorts by.

The wall: SmugMug saves nothing by itself — the save is yours to do

SmugMug does not put your gallery photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the save describes a manual step — open the photo, tap the overflow menu, tap Save to Device — because the manual step is the only step there is. SmugMug's settings hold nothing about saving to the phone: the app has no save-location setting beyond the storage-location choice, no auto-download toggle, and no sync-to-device option. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache, which saves data when you scroll back over photos you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is SmugMug's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's cache is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you save it, and you save it one photo at a time — or one gallery at a time, with Save Gallery to Device.

The save itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because SmugMug's own help states it in the same words this article uses. Open the photo in the SmugMug app. Tap the overflow menu — the three vertical dots in the top menu bar — and tap Save to Device. That is SmugMug's own help, first-hand: "To download, navigate to a photo or video you want to save and tap the overflow menu icon. You'll see an option to Save to Device if you're logged in and have download rights." The two conditions in that sentence are the whole gate, and they are worth reading twice: you must be logged in, and you must have download rights. If you are browsing a gallery instead of a single photo, the same menu offers Save Gallery to Device instead — the whole gallery, in one tap, which is the one bulk step the app does offer. The photo lands in your phone's Pictures folder — that is the independent guide's version, PicBackMan: "Photos will save to your device's Pictures folder" — and the Gallery shows it from there. If the overflow menu shows no Save to Device, tap the photo first to open it full-screen, then try the menu again — the save option belongs to the photo, and a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, not the file.

There is one more thing the save does let you choose, and it is worth knowing before you save a hundred photos, because it decides where the files land. SmugMug's app settings hold a download-location choice: tap your user icon in the upper-left corner to enter the settings, tap Storage Location, and then SD Card — if your Android phone supports SD cards. That is SmugMug's own help again, and it is the only location choice the app offers: internal storage or the SD card, not a folder you pick. Treat any choose-a-folder step as unverified: the save lands in the Pictures folder, every time, on the versions the guides describe, or on the SD card if you changed the storage location. That is a limit, and it is also a convenience: every SmugMug photo you save is in the same folder, and the folder is the thing you point a backup at. Do not waste an evening looking for a save-location setting in the SmugMug app. There is not one, on Android, in any guide this run found — only the internal-or-SD-card choice.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the save sometimes silently does nothing. The SmugMug app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the save tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first save, open the Gallery and look for the photo in the Pictures folder. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every save after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission in Settings, Apps, SmugMug, Permissions, Photos and videos, and save the photo again. Do not save fifty photos and check at the end. Save one, check the folder, then save the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the SmugMug app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the SmugMug account, because the photos live in that account's galleries, and the help page is explicit that Save to Device appears only if you are logged in and have download rights. Enough free space on the phone to hold the saves: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, save in batches and copy each batch to the drive before saving the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the save steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a save guide.

One more check, on the SmugMug side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can save. If the photo is in a gallery you can open, it is a photo you can try to save. If it is from an account that is private, or the photo was deleted, or the gallery is protected by a viewing password you do not have, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be saved, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. And if the Save to Device option is missing entirely, that is usually the owner's choice, not a bug: the next section but one covers it. Do not save first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then tap the overflow menu. A save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Save one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the SmugMug app on your Android phone and sign in. Find the photo — in a gallery, in a folder, in your Following list, or in search — and open it full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a save of a thumbnail is a save of a thumbnail. Tap the overflow menu — the three vertical dots in the top menu bar. Tap Save to Device in the menu that appears. Then open the Gallery and look in the Pictures folder: the photo should be there, at full size, and the size should be plausible for a photo, not a few kilobytes. If the overflow menu shows no Save to Device, the gallery owner has likely switched downloads off — the fixes below cover it — and no tap in the app will bring it back.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the Pictures folder is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo in the Pictures folder is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of the Pictures folder, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where SmugMug put it. The default run, described in the next section but one, reads the media index with no folder filter, and the Pictures folder is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo on SmugMug if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is in the Pictures folder, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the saved SmugMug photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the saved batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one gallery, the photos from one photographer. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand overflow-menu-and-save taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole gallery, use Save Gallery to Device in the app, or Method 2, which gets the whole gallery in one ZIP instead of one save at a time. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what the owner enabled — SmugMug offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Save to Device option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the owner disabled downloads: SmugMug's help is explicit that Allow Downloads is a per-gallery setting the owner controls, and if it is off, the download control disappears for everyone except the owner — respect that choice, and ask the photographer for the file if you need it. Second, check that you are logged in: the help page says Save to Device appears only if you are logged in and have download rights, so a logged-out browse shows no save at all. Third, update the SmugMug app: the menu wording varies between app versions, and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Fourth, try the gallery route instead of the photo route: open the gallery, tap the overflow menu, and use Save Gallery to Device, which saves the whole gallery at once. Fifth, open the photo in Chrome instead of the app: open the photo page at smugmug.com in the browser, tap the picture to enlarge it, press and hold it, and choose the download or save-image option the browser offers — the file lands in Downloads, and the gallery shows it from there. Sixth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, SmugMug, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and save again. One of those six fixes the save in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, in the Pictures folder, before you save the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the Pictures folder, select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole gallery — the download-all ZIP route

SmugMug is better than most apps in this series on one point, and it is worth stating plainly: the app does offer a gallery-level save. From a gallery view, the overflow menu offers Save Gallery to Device instead of Save to Device — the whole gallery, in one tap, with no per-photo tapping. That is SmugMug's own help, and it is the first route to try for a gallery you want in full. But the app save is not the only gallery route, and the web route is the one that gives you the originals with the most control, so this section covers both, and says which one fits which job.

For a gallery on the web, the route is SmugMug's own delivery flow, and it is worth following exactly, because the flow has two traps — the link expiry and the ZIP-on-Android wall — that decide whether it works on a phone at all. The gallery owner enables visitor downloads first: in My Site, Site Organizer, open the gallery settings, Photo Protection tab, and turn Allow Downloads on. That adds a download-all button to the gallery — a little downward-facing arrow — and a visitor clicks it and follows the on-screen prompts to verify their email address, so SmugMug can deliver the photos digitally in .zip files. The gallery needs three or more photos or videos for the download button to appear at all. Once the gallery is ready, SmugMug sends an email with the download link. That link expires automatically after two weeks — and the visitor can request a fresh link any time. If the gallery has a lot of photos, the download is split into several .zip files, and you click each link to grab every part. That is the flow, and the flow is the same whether the visitor is you or someone else.

Here is the trap that decides whether the web route works on a phone: the links SmugMug sends are for .zip files, and .zip files are not supported on most Android devices. SmugMug's own help states it outright, and then states the fix in the same paragraph: to open .zip files on your mobile device, first grab the SmugMug app, then tap your download link — your photos will automatically unzip to your photos roll on Android. So the web route on a phone is really a web-plus-app route: request the link in the browser, open the email, tap the link with the SmugMug app installed, and the photos unzip into the photos roll — the Pictures folder — where the Gallery shows them and the backup can reach them. Do not tap the link in a browser that tries to download the ZIP into Downloads and leaves it there: SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives, so a ZIP sitting in Downloads is a file the backup will skip, and the photos inside it are still zipped. Unzip it first — through the app tap, or in the Files app — and then back up the files it contains.

As the account owner, you have a second route that is cleaner, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the route for your own galleries. In My Site, Site Organizer, select the gallery, click Select All to select every image, then click the download icon. SmugMug packages the gallery and emails you the .zip link when it is ready. Two differences from the visitor route are worth knowing: photos you hid with the Hide option in the photo tools are included in this owner download, while they are excluded from a guest download; and a link you request as the logged-in owner from Site Organizer does not expire, while a link requested as a logged-out guest expires in two weeks. If your gallery is small and you decide to wait, the pop-up window updates automatically with your downloadable .zip link, and you can copy the link and send it along to your fans. If you subscribe to SmugMug Source and have RAW files in the gallery, those are included with your gallery download as the logged-in account owner, along with any .xmp or sidecar files — and if you do not want those sidecar files included, or you plan to send the download link to someone else, request the download as a guest instead.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. The download size is the owner's choice, not yours: the gallery settings hold a Download Size option that defaults to Original, and the owner can change it to a lower resolution at any time — but as the account owner, when you are logged in, you always receive the original size of your files when using the download options. A visitor receives whatever size the owner chose. The ZIP is prepared on SmugMug's side, and the email arrives when it is ready, not when you ask — request it, wait, download it, unzip it, and then back up the files it contains. Each time you upload more photos to the gallery, you need to request a new download, or the new images will not be included in the .zip file. For ten photos, save them in the app. For a whole gallery, use Save Gallery to Device in the app, or the download-all ZIP. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Pictures folder — or the unzipped batch folder — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole account — what SmugMug does and does not export

For a whole account, the honest answer is that SmugMug has no one-tap whole-account export, and this article says so instead of inventing one. There is no settings page that zips your entire account, no data-request button that compiles everything you ever uploaded, and no archive that arrives by email with all your galleries in it. The export unit on SmugMug is the gallery: one gallery at a time, through the download-all button or Site Organizer, one ZIP per gallery, one email link per gallery. If you have ten galleries, that is ten downloads. If you have a hundred, that is a hundred — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

The route that does exist for a whole account is the gallery-by-gallery route, and it is worth doing in order, because the order is what keeps it from becoming a mess. Start with the galleries that matter most — the ones with no other copy anywhere — and work down the list. For each gallery, request the download as the logged-in owner from Site Organizer, so the link does not expire and the hidden photos and RAW files are included. Wait for the email, tap the link with the app installed so it unzips to the photos roll, and back up the files it contains before requesting the next gallery — unzip first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives. Keep each gallery's files in their own dated folder on the drive, so the drive ends up with the galleries in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower than a single export would be, and it is the route that exists. The app's Save Gallery to Device is the faster route for a single gallery, and the Site Organizer ZIP is the route for the originals with the most control. Use each one for the job it fits.

Two facts about the gallery downloads are worth stating because they decide who they work for. First, they are for galleries you can download: your own galleries, and other people's galleries where the owner switched Allow Downloads on. They do not provide an export for someone else's account, and they do not include photos you bookmarked but did not download — a library of bookmarks is not a library you can export. Second, the download link is not locked with a password and can be opened by anyone who gets the link — SmugMug's own help states that, and it is worth taking seriously: do not forward a gallery download link in a group chat and assume only the people you meant will open it. If the gallery needs protection, the owner can turn on Require Download Password, which asks visitors for a password before they can download — and the password is whatever the owner chooses, changeable at any time.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what the owner enabled. SmugMug offers no export for an account you do not control, no download-a-whole-site button, and no save-them-all in the app beyond a single gallery. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take a username or a gallery link and fetch the media — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them: they are outside SmugMug, they need your trust to run, and several of them ask for your SmugMug login, which is something they do not need for a public gallery and which puts your account at risk. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: save the photos you actually want one by one, or the whole gallery where the owner left downloads enabled, or ask the photographer for the files. A thousand one-by-one saves is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a saved SmugMug photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a saved photo in the Pictures folder copies with the rest of the library. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the SmugMug save folder in shared storage.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: save the photo, open the gallery, and look for it in the Pictures folder. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in the Pictures folder: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into SmugMug. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask SmugMug for anything. A photo still on SmugMug's servers is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on SmugMug's servers is invisible to the file manager. The save is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at SmugMug; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up SmugMug photos without saving them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Pictures folder came from SmugMug, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the SmugMug route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the save that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the SmugMug photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the Pictures folder and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Pictures folder for SmugMug saves yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on this exact folder — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a saved photo shows the save date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo saved from SmugMug shows the day you saved it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a saved file is the save time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a saved SmugMug photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you saved it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For SmugMug, the taken date almost certainly did not survive, and the reason is the quality section below: SmugMug serves you its own copy of the photo, and a served copy is the step most likely to rewrite or drop the metadata. This article states that as the expected case, not as a certainty, because no source this run tested the taken date after an in-app save — but the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app: the file is written by the save, at save time, and its modified date is the save date. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: save one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you save a hundred of them.

There is one way in which SmugMug is better than the other clouds on the date, and it is worth stating fairly. The gallery download as the logged-in owner gives you the original size of your files — SmugMug's own help says so — and an original file is the one most likely to carry its taken date with it. That is the opposite of OneDrive, where Microsoft's own support forum says the taken date can be replaced by the upload date, especially by camera upload or a bulk upload, before you ever tap Save. For SmugMug the risk is at the save, not at the upload, and the defence is to know it before you save the batch: use the owner gallery download when the dates matter, and the in-app save when the photo matters.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. SmugMug names the saved file itself, and two saves of the same photo — or two photos SmugMug named the same way — can arrive in the Pictures folder under the same name, so the second save either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo on SmugMug if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to save a batch and copy it to the drive before saving the next, or to rename the files in the Pictures folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to save.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Save one gallery's photos, back up the Pictures folder, clear it, save the next gallery's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the galleries in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the saved copy is SmugMug's copy — and Original on the web

A photo saved from the SmugMug app is not necessarily the photographer's original file. It is the copy SmugMug serves for the phone, saved into your Gallery, and the difference is real even when it is not measured in pixels on the spot. SmugMug stores multiple versions of every upload, and the app save gives you the served version, not a size you chose: there is no size menu in the app save, and no Original button on the overflow menu. The saved copy is a real photo, and it is good enough for the gallery, the drive, and a print at ordinary sizes. It is not necessarily the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app save may be the wrong tool, and this article says so instead of letting you discover it on the drive.

The route to the original is a gallery setting, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the only route that guarantees the bigger file — and it is the owner's setting, not yours. The gallery settings hold a Download Size option that defaults to Original, and the owner can change it to a lower resolution at any time. As the account owner, when you are logged in, you always receive the original size of your files when using the download options — SmugMug's own help says so, and that is the guarantee. As a visitor, you receive whatever size the owner chose, and the download size the owner set is the size you get. On the desktop site, the download tool lets you choose to download the original or the edited version of a photo, and to pick the format — JPG, TIF, or PNG — so a visitor download at Original is the full file, and an owner download is always the full file. On a phone it is a fiddly detour per photo for a gain that is often small, and the honest advice is to use the gallery download at Original when the photo matters at full size, and to use the in-app save for everything else: the in-app save is an overflow menu and a tap, and the gallery download is a request, a wait, and an unzip. Use each one for the job it fits. For your own photos the download is always the original; for someone else's it is the size the owner chose.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because SmugMug is a photo library that holds videos too. The app save covers videos the same way it covers photos: open the video in the SmugMug app, use the overflow menu and Save to Device, and the file lands on the phone — SmugMug's own help says "a photo or video you want to save". The backup covers it from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, so a saved photo and a saved video both copy with the default run. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: save the video, open the Pictures folder, and look at what arrived. If it plays, it saved as a video; if it arrived as a single still frame, the save caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, save it from the gallery download, where the ZIP holds the photos and videos from the gallery together, and check what arrived before you save the next twenty.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: save one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the saved copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before saving — wait for the photo to load crisp before you tap the overflow menu, because a save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the saved file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the save is worth doing at all. A photo saved from SmugMug is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the size: the saved copy is SmugMug's copy, and no backup step will put the missing pixels back. Save the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. If it is not, take the gallery-download route for that one photo, and save the rest in the app.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest save, a middle one, and the most recent save. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Pictures folder on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the Pictures folder holds the photos plus whatever else landed there — but the photo count should match the number you saved, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you saved, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second save overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the Pictures folder before the backup ever saw it. Save one batch at a time and copy each batch before saving the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you saved, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the SmugMug batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a saved photo shows the save date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the save, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, save that photo again from the gallery download at Original size, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in SmugMug's copy, not at upload — and the owner gallery download is the route that keeps it, because it gives you the original files.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your SmugMug login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The gallery download needs no third party at all: the owner switches Allow Downloads on, and the download-all button does the rest. A tool that asks for your SmugMug password to download a public gallery is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours. Several unofficial tools claim to download SmugMug galleries faster, and they pose exactly this risk: you must enter your login credentials into unknown software, which violates SmugMug's terms of service in some cases and puts your account at risk. The in-app save needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not bookmark galleries and expect them on the drive. A bookmark keeps the gallery inside your SmugMug account, not a file, and a bookmarked gallery the photographer deletes is a bookmark that points at nothing. Bookmark the gallery to find it later, and save the photos to keep them. They are two different taps for two different jobs, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing in the Pictures folder — because the photos were never saved, only bookmarked.

Do not save a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand overflow-menu-and-save taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Save the handful you actually want in the app, use Save Gallery to Device or the download-all ZIP for a gallery, and work through your own galleries one by one from Site Organizer. The gallery download is slow and the app save is fast, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the gallery download to reach someone else's account: it is for a gallery whose owner left downloads on, and it will not contain your bookmarks either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the feed as the only copy. SmugMug is a library on someone else's server, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can go private or be closed, and a paid plan that lapses can take the galleries with it. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the feed is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
