Save QNAP photos to USB from Android

Your QNAP photos live on your NAS, not on your phone. Save them with Qfile Pro — they land in its Download Folder — then copy them to a USB drive, no computer.

November 27, 2026 · 12 min read

TL;DR: A QNAP photo is not on your phone until Qfile Pro saves it — and looking at it in Qfile Pro or QuMagie does not save it: the photo you are looking at lives on your NAS, and QNAP's own docs give the save as a real step with a real name. Open Qfile Pro, pick your NAS, tap the three-dot menu on a photo or a folder, and choose Save to downloads — Qfile Pro runs it as a background task, and the file lands in the Download Folder (Menu, then Offline Files, then Download Folder), inside a subfolder named after your NAS. From there the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so a downloaded QNAP photo that shows in the gallery copies along with everything else when you plug in the drive and tap Backup, no computer. The honest limits, up front: the wrong app is the first trap — QuMagie Mobile is QNAP's photo viewer, and its own quick-start guide has no download-to-device step at all, so a photo you can see in QuMagie is still a photo on the NAS; QNAP publishes no absolute Android path for the Download Folder, so this article names the folder as QNAP names it and does not invent a path, and the gallery check is what proves the default run sees a download; the save is one photo — or one folder — at a time, and QNAP's download page documents no per-photo multi-select, so a scattered library is a folder-by-folder job, while the whole-NAS bulk route is the button on the NAS itself, not on the phone; Qfile Pro's automatic upload runs the other way, phone to NAS, and there is no documented auto-download; and the From-picker route at the Download Folder is code-supported but untested — no SnapDisk test has picked that folder yet — so the save-to-gallery-first workflow in Method 1 is the proven route. As of November 27, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated QNAP-photos-to-USB how-to: the results are QNAP's own download docs, which stop before the USB step, QNAP's own QuMagie guide, which never downloads at all, and generic phone-to-USB guides that never open a QNAP app.

The wall: a QNAP photo is not on your phone until you save it — and viewing it is not saving it

Start with the two mistakes this job turns on, because they look like the same tap and they are not — and with QNAP the first one is worse than in the email cases, because QNAP gives you two apps that both show you the photo, and only one of them can save it. The first mistake is the quiet one: opening Qfile Pro or QuMagie Mobile and seeing the photo does not put the photo on your phone. The photo you are looking at lives on your NAS — a box on a shelf in your home, holding the files — and the copy the app fetches to show you the preview is a streamed view, not a file on the phone. A NAS full of photos you have browsed and looked at is, from the phone's point of view, a NAS full of nothing: the files are on the NAS, not yours on the phone, and the day the NAS is off, the network is down, or the drive in the NAS fails, the photos are unreachable. If you have never saved a photo from the NAS, there is no QNAP folder on your phone at all, and pointing SnapDisk at a folder that does not exist copies nothing. That is the wall, and it is quieter than in the messaging-app cases: WhatsApp at least saves the photo to the phone when it arrives, and the work there is finding the folder. QNAP saves nothing on arrival and nothing on viewing — the save is a step you take, one photo or one folder at a time, and the step is the whole first half of this article.

The second mistake is opening the wrong app for this job. QNAP ships two photo-facing apps for Android, and they do opposite things for a USB backup. Qfile Pro is the file manager: it browses the NAS, and its own docs give the download as a named control — Save to downloads — which writes a real file to the phone, into the Download Folder. That file is what SnapDisk copies to the drive. QuMagie Mobile is the photo viewer: it browses, searches, tags faces, builds albums, and shares the photos on your NAS — and it uploads photos from your phone to the NAS, or backs them up there automatically. Every direction in its own quick-start guide runs phone to NAS or stays on the NAS: view, search, tag, create albums, back up from the mobile device to the NAS, share by link. There is no download-to-device step anywhere in the guide — no Save, no Download, no offline copy — so a photo you found in QuMagie is still a photo on the NAS, and no USB drive plugged into the phone can reach it. QuMagie is a real app and a good viewer, but for this job it is the wrong app, and the article says so plainly rather than letting the reader discover it after an evening of tapping. For this article, Qfile Pro is the only app that counts, and every time this article says save, it means Qfile Pro's Save to downloads, not a look at the photo in either app.

Two scope facts, because they decide what this article can and cannot promise. First, the save is one photo — or one folder — at a time, and QNAP's download page documents no per-photo multi-select: the page is titled "Downloading files and folders" and its step is "identify a file or folder, and then tap" the menu — one file or one folder per save. A folder of photos is one save, which is the bulk route this article gives first, but a library scattered across twenty folders is twenty saves, and a whole NAS is not a phone job at all — the whole-NAS route is the button on the NAS itself, in Method 3. Second, not every photo on the NAS is reached the same way. A photo in a folder you can browse in Qfile Pro saves with the steps in Method 1. A photo you can only see in QuMagie — because it lives in a QuMagie content-source folder you have not browsed in Qfile Pro — still saves the same way once you find its folder in Qfile Pro: QuMagie content sources are ordinary folders on the NAS, and Qfile Pro browses the same folders. And if the NAS is not reachable — the phone is away from home and myQNAPcloud is not set up — Qfile Pro cannot even list the photos, let alone save them: the Beginner's Guide says to register a QNAP ID and set up myQNAPcloud Link on the NAS first, then log the app in with that ID, and that setup is the precondition for everything below.

And a word on whose photos these are, because QNAP photos are usually yours in a way Pinterest pins are not. The photos this article covers are the ones on your own NAS — the family archive you moved off a computer, the scans of old photographs, the camera roll you backed up to the box on the shelf so it would survive a lost phone — and saving them to your own drive for your own records is the job. They are also, in the plain sense, sometimes photos someone else took: a photographer's work, a family member's scan, a client's product shot. Saving a file does not grant permission to reuse it — so re-posting a photo from the NAS as if it were yours, or sharing it onward as your own work, is a different act, and the same courtesy applies here as anywhere: the person who took it is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else. This article's methods copy the file to your drive; what you do with it afterwards is yours to decide, and asking is the honest step before anything leaves your phone. Only save content you have permission to keep — and this article makes no watermark-removal claim anywhere: SnapDisk copies the file exactly as Qfile Pro saved it.

Where downloaded QNAP photos land — the Download Folder, and the gallery check

This section exists because the folder is the one fact a guide can get wrong in a way that wastes an evening — and because the folder decides which SnapDisk route works. The answer is short, with one fork in it and one honest unknown. A file saved from Qfile Pro with Save to downloads lands in Qfile Pro's Download Folder on the phone, and you find it inside Qfile Pro itself: open Qfile Pro, tap Menu, then Offline Files, then the Download Folder tab. QNAP's own docs say so in their own words: the "Managing downloaded files and folders" page gives exactly that path — Menu, then Offline Files, then the Download Folder tab — and QNAP's own FAQ, for Qfile Pro 4.0.0 and later, gives the same folder under its older name on 4.0.0 and earlier, Menu, then Offline, then Download Folder. Inside the Download Folder, the files sit in a subfolder named after the NAS they came from: QNAP's own download page says "downloaded files and folders appear in a folder with the same name as the source device", and the managing-downloads page says to tap a device folder to view all the files downloaded from that device. That agreement is the honest centre of this article: unlike the Pinterest case, where the guides disagreed about the folder, the QNAP docs agree, and the folder is named by QNAP's own docs as well as by its own FAQ.

The honest unknown is the absolute path, and this article states it rather than inventing one. QNAP names the folder in the app — Download Folder, with a per-device subfolder — but none of QNAP's pages read this run gives the absolute Android path it sits at, no /sdcard/Download, no /storage/emulated/0/... path, nothing a file manager can be pointed at by address. So this article does not give one. Two facts narrow it down without naming it: QNAP's own FAQ says Qfile Pro "requires 'All file access' permissions to access downloaded files", which is the permission for shared storage rather than an app-private folder, and QNAP's own Play listing says the app supports "changing the download folder on an external SD card", which means the folder is a real folder on shared storage that you can move — but neither fact is a path, and this article does not turn them into one. The check that settles it is the gallery check, and it is the same check the whole series uses: open your gallery app and look for the photo. A downloaded photo that shows in the gallery is an ordinary indexed file, and the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index, so a photo the gallery shows is a photo the default run sees. If it is not in the gallery, look in Qfile Pro under Menu, then Offline Files, then Download Folder: the file is still a file on the phone, and Method 2 reaches it. Do not look in DCIM/Camera — that is where the camera puts its own photos, not where Qfile Pro puts downloads, and a QNAP save will never be there. The one honest uncertainty in this section is whether a given phone's gallery shows Download Folder photos: most gallery apps show a Downloads album, and Google Photos shows them under On this device, but the gallery is the check, not the promise — if the photo is in the Download Folder and not in the gallery, the default run may not see it, and Method 2 is the route. That is the same check the whole series uses, and it is stated here rather than assumed.

One more scope fact for this section, because it decides what the drive copy holds: the download is the file exactly as it sits on the NAS — byte-for-byte, not a re-encoded copy — so whatever the NAS holds is what lands in the Download Folder. Qfile Pro downloads the file; no conversion step appears anywhere in QNAP's download docs, and the save does not shrink the photo, does not strip it, and does not convert it. A downloaded photo keeps its original file and its metadata, including the date it was taken when the file carries it, which is why SnapDisk's oldest-first order puts it where it belongs: by the date taken when the file has one, falling back to the download date when it does not. If a downloaded photo looks smaller than the original, the NAS holds the smaller file: the photo was shrunk before it reached the NAS, or it was uploaded small in the first place. SnapDisk copies the file exactly as Qfile Pro saved it — byte-for-byte, no conversion, no quality change — but it cannot improve a file that arrived small. And one QNAP-specific wrinkle worth naming here, because it looks like a failed download when it is not one: Qfile Pro runs each download as a background task, and a large folder takes a while — the docs send you to the background-tasks page for managing them — so a folder that has not appeared yet may still be downloading, not failed. Give it time on a steady connection before saving it again.

What you need

An Android phone — Android 8.0 or later, which is Qfile Pro's minimum version, and also past SnapDisk's 7.0 minimum — with Qfile Pro installed and signed in to your NAS, plus one USB drive your phone can read: a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Qfile Pro itself is free from Google Play, and it needs a QNAP NAS running QTS 4.0 or later, or QuTS hero 4.5.0 or later. SnapDisk itself is installed from the v0.1.2 APK — it is not on Google Play yet — so install it by allowing "install unknown apps" for the browser or Files app you download it with, which is standard Android. You need nothing else for the main route: no file manager with hidden files, no extra app, no account beyond the NAS login, no third-party downloader. The save step is inside Qfile Pro itself, and the backup is SnapDisk's default run. Check the drive's space first: QNAP photos are small — a photo is a few megabytes at most — so space is almost never the problem for the QNAP half, but look anyway, because a whole-library default run copies everything, not just QNAP. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit, and exFAT is the format to use for a photo drive.

Before any method, settle the two questions that decide whether the phone route works at all: can the phone reach the NAS, and does it see the USB drive? Open Qfile Pro and check the NAS appears in the Device List — if the phone is away from home, myQNAPcloud must be set up on the NAS and the app logged in with your QNAP ID, per QNAP's own Beginner's Guide; if the NAS does not appear, the save cannot start. Then plug the drive in and open Settings > Connected devices or Settings > Storage — the drive should appear there. That check is the test, and it takes ten seconds. If the drive does not appear, try a different adapter first — cheap OTG adapters are the usual failure — then a different drive, and a powered hub if the drive is a spinning hard drive rather than a flash drive. And one warning up front, because it applies to every route below: do not delete anything from the phone — or from the NAS — until the USB copy is done and verified. The photos on the NAS are the originals, and the photos in the Download Folder are your only copy outside the NAS, and deleting from either before the check is how a photo ends up nowhere at all. The same goes for the NAS folders themselves: do not tidy the NAS until the drive copy is checked, because the NAS is the only route back to a photo you have not saved yet.

Method 1: Save the photos in Qfile Pro, then back up with SnapDisk

This is the route to use, because it is the only one that joins the whole job — the QNAP photo, out of the NAS and into the Download Folder, onto a USB drive plugged into the phone, no computer — using only the apps already on the phone plus SnapDisk. It has two halves, and they have to happen in order: the save, which puts the photos on the phone as files, and the SnapDisk copy, which puts them on the drive. The first half is the work. The second half is the backup. Skipping the first half is the mistake this article exists to prevent: running SnapDisk's default backup with nothing saved copies every photo in the gallery — and zero QNAP photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no QNAP photos to copy.

  1. Save the photos from inside Qfile Pro. Open Qfile Pro on your phone. Tap the device-list icon on the app bar — the Device List window opens — and tap your QNAP device to select it; the Folders window appears. Browse to the folder holding the photos, find a photo, and tap the three-dot menu on it: the file and folder action menu opens. Tap Save to downloads. Qfile Pro creates the download task and adds it to its background tasks, and the photo lands in the Download Folder, inside the subfolder named after your NAS. Do it one photo — or one folder — at a time: tap the three-dot menu on a whole folder and choose Save to downloads, and the whole folder saves in one task. Work through the folders that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting. If Qfile Pro asks for All file access permission the first time you open the Download Folder, allow it, or the app cannot reach its own downloads. For a photo you found in QuMagie first: note its folder — QuMagie content sources are ordinary folders on the NAS — then open that same folder in Qfile Pro and save it there; QuMagie itself has no download step to use.
  2. Check the photo is on the phone. Open your gallery app and look for the photo. If it is there, the save works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see it, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If it is not in the gallery, open Qfile Pro, tap Menu, then Offline Files, then the Download Folder tab, and tap the folder named after your NAS: the file should be there by name. If it is in neither place after a save, check that Qfile Pro has All file access permission — Settings > Apps > Qfile Pro > Permissions — and save one photo again, watching for the download to finish; Qfile Pro runs downloads as background tasks, so a large folder takes a while and a weak connection stalls it the same way it stalls any download. A save that never completes is usually a connection problem, not a Qfile Pro problem: the file is coming from your NAS, and if the phone cannot reach the NAS — no local network, no myQNAPcloud — nothing downloads at all.
  3. Do not look for an auto-download setting — there is not one. Qfile Pro has an auto-upload setting that moves photos from the phone to the NAS — "Auto upload photos from the photo gallery", in QNAP's own blog's words — and QuMagie Mobile has the same direction of backup, phone to NAS. Neither app documents an auto-download that pulls NAS photos onto the phone by itself. Microsoft's Outlook has the same shape — no auto-download — and Gmail has the setting, but QNAP does not: every photo still on the NAS needs the same Save to downloads tap each. There is no setting in this article that makes the job smaller over time instead of bigger, and this article does not invent one: the photos that arrive on the NAS next week arrive as NAS files, not as phone files, and the next backup picks them up only after a save session first. That is the honest difference from the Gmail case, and it is the reason the save session matters more here.
  4. Plug in the drive and run SnapDisk. Plug the USB drive into the phone. SnapDisk offers the backup in a notification — tap Backup — or open the app and tap Start. The run copies oldest-first, verifies every file by size after copying, and shows progress with Pause and Stop in the notification. The free run covers up to 5 GB, and QNAP photos are small, so one run is almost always enough for the QNAP half — though a whole-library run copies everything else too, and that is the point: the downloaded photos come along with the rest, with no folder to pick and no step to remember. Android 15 and later give a dataSync foreground service 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  5. Skip the share links — or download them another way. Qfile Pro lets you create a download link for a file and send it by email or SMS, and QuMagie lets you share albums by link too — but a link is not a file on your phone. A share link opens the photo in a browser on the NAS, and the USB leg still has not happened. Do not use a link as the save step: open Qfile Pro and use Save to downloads, which is the step that writes the file. A photo that only exists as a link is still a photo that lives on the NAS until you download it, and the USB leg still has not happened.

Two honest limits. First, the save is not retroactive in the other direction either: saving a photo today does not put the photos you only looked at yesterday onto the phone — a photo you viewed in Qfile Pro or QuMagie is still on the NAS until you save it. That is step 1's job, and it is a manual job: one tap per photo or per folder, with no auto-download setting to catch the ones still to come. Second, the 5 GB free cap is per run — a QNAP album will never reach it, but a whole-library default run might, and running it again picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Third, SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the photos from the Download Folder after the check is always your step, never the app's — and deleting them from the NAS is QNAP's, not SnapDisk's, and it does not free the drive copy. The drive copy is the backup; the Download Folder copy is the original until you decide otherwise.

Copying only some QNAP photos? If you would rather copy just a few photos than the whole library, the From folder picker still takes a folder, not single files — so save the ones you want into their own folder on the NAS first, download that folder, then point the picker at the Download Folder. The picker route keeps only recognised photo and video extensions. If the drive is unplugged mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

And the check that matters for this article: after the run, compare the count against the Download Folder. A green backup that copied the whole library has still copied zero QNAP photos if nothing was ever saved from the NAS — which is exactly what happens when the photos were only viewed in Qfile Pro or QuMagie. Open the drive and look for the QNAP photos by name: the default run mirrors the source tree, so the photos should be there under their own names. If they are missing, the save is the suspect, not the run — save the photos from Qfile Pro first, then run again. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — the only files it ever deletes are its own half-finished .part files — so deleting the originals after the check is always your step, never the app's.

Method 2: Point the From picker at the Download Folder

If you would rather copy just the QNAP photos and nothing else — and the photos are already saved — point SnapDisk's From folder picker at the Download Folder. Open SnapDisk, tap the From folder picker, navigate to the Download Folder — it sits inside Qfile Pro's storage, under its own name — and tap Backup. The same 5 GB free cap applies, and the same oldest-first order, size verification, Pause and Stop. The folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the Download Folder reaches every file under it, including the per-device subfolder, in one run.

The honest limit on this route is that it is the code-supported route, not the test-proven one — and this article says so rather than claiming a test that never ran — and the folder itself is the second honest limit. The picker is the system's folder picker, and the code has no QNAP path handling anywhere — zero matches for "qnap" or "qfile" in the app's source — so the navigation is yours, step by step, through folders whose names are ordinary. But no SnapDisk test has picked the Download Folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first. Two things are in this route's favour, and they are worth stating because they are the reason the route is code-supported rather than blocked: the Download Folder is not hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and QNAP's own FAQ says Qfile Pro needs All file access to reach its downloads while its own Play listing says the download folder can be moved to an external SD card — both of which point at shared storage, outside Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks some app folders does not apply here at all. And the folder walk keeps only recognised photo and video extensions — a document in the Download Folder is simply skipped, and a link is never copied, because a link is not a photo or a video. For a whole NAS, use Method 3 — this article is only about the photo and video files. One more note for the photos that show in the gallery: those are already covered by the default run, so the Download Folder picker adds nothing for them — the default run reads the media index and the Gallery is the index.

Method 3: Bulk routes — a whole folder in Qfile Pro, and the button on the NAS

QNAP has a bulk save of its own on the phone, and a bulk route off the phone — and they are worth naming honestly, because they are the first results for every bulk-save search — and only one of them is this job. On the phone, the bulk route is the folder save in Qfile Pro: QNAP's own download page covers files and folders together, so tap the three-dot menu on a photo folder on the NAS, choose Save to downloads, and the whole folder saves in one background task. That is the phone bulk route, and it is the one to use for a library organised into folders — one tap per folder, and the folders you care about most first. There is no documented per-photo multi-select on QNAP's download page, so do not go looking for a tick-box mode the docs never mention: the folder is the unit of bulk, and a scattered library is a folder-by-folder job.

Off the phone, the bulk route is the button on the NAS itself. QNAP's own product page for the TS-431P says the front-panel USB port has a one-touch-copy function "for you to instantly back up data on USB drives to the TS-431P or alternatively to back up data from the TS-431P to USB drives" — plug a USB drive into the front USB port of the NAS, press the button, and the NAS copies its own data to the drive, or the drive's data to the NAS. QNAP's own blog describes the same button the other way: plug a USB drive into the front port, press it, and the NAS backs the drive up to itself — and since QTS 4.3.4 it does the same for a mobile device plugged in by USB. But none of that is a phone-to-USB backup, and the difference is where the drive is plugged in. The one-touch-copy route puts the drive in the NAS, not in the phone: the files land on a drive you can open anywhere, and they never touch the phone at all. So use it for what it is: the way to get a whole NAS off the box in one sitting, for the day you need the whole NAS at once, standing at the NAS. For plain photo files on a drive you can open anywhere, save the photos to the phone first — Method 1 — and copy them to a USB drive with SnapDisk, no computer. The two routes are not rivals; they are different shapes, and this article is about the plain-files one. And the same permission rule applies to both: only save content you have permission to keep.

Can SnapDisk read the Download Folder directly?

This is the question the whole article turns on, and it deserves a straight answer in three parts — what the code proves it can do, what is untested, and what would prove the rest. The first part is proven, from the code at the current base and from how Android's media index works — and it is the same shape as the Gmail and Outlook cases. SnapDisk's default run reads the phone's media index — it queries the MediaStore images and videos tables, oldest-first by date taken, with no folder filter. A QNAP photo downloaded to the phone is an ordinary indexed file: it shows in the gallery, and gallery apps read the media index. The photo's presence in the gallery is the evidence the file is indexed — if the media scanner had skipped it, the gallery would not show it at all; it does show, so there is no such skip. The query is in PhotoQuery.kt, the no-folder-filter shape is the code's own design — the query call passes no selection at all — and the gallery evidence is the platform fact that closes the loop. So the default run sees downloaded QNAP photos, and it sees them with no step beyond the save. That is not an assumption: it is the code's query shape plus the gallery evidence, stated as such. And the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only — the query reads every indexed image and video on the phone, whatever folder it sits in, so the Download Folder is covered the same way DCIM is.

The second part is untested, and this article marks it as such rather than guessing in either direction: whether SnapDisk's From folder picker reaches the Download Folder end to end on a real phone. The picker is the system's folder picker — ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree, in MainActivity.kt — and it opens with no folder pre-selected: there is no initial-folder hint anywhere in the code, and no QNAP path handling anywhere in the app — zero matches for "qnap" or "qfile" in the app's source. The navigation chain is an ordinary folder — the Download Folder, not dot-prefixed, so not hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be — and QNAP's own FAQ and Play listing point at shared storage rather than Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks some app folders does not apply here. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the Download Folder should reach every photo under it, including the per-device subfolder. But no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first. The third unknown in this section is QNAP's own, not SnapDisk's: QNAP publishes no absolute Android path for the Download Folder, so this article cannot state where the picker will find it by address — only that it sits under its own name, and that the gallery check is what proves the default run sees a download.

The third part is what the code does prove about the rest of the job, so the limits above are not mistaken for a wider doubt. The manifest requests the media-read permissions (READ_MEDIA_IMAGES and READ_MEDIA_VIDEO, plus the older READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE capped at Android 12), the notification permission, and foreground-service permissions — and no INTERNET permission at all, zero occurrences, so the photos physically cannot leave the device. It does not request all-files access — no MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE anywhere — which is the permission a file manager uses to browse another app's folders freely, and the absence is the reason SnapDisk cannot simply walk into the Download Folder the way a file manager can; the folder picker is the route instead, and it needs your tap. The backup itself is the verified core: oldest-first by date taken with a date-modified fallback (PhotoQuery.kt), every file size-verified after copying (BackupEngine.kt), Pause and Stop in the notification (NotificationHelper.kt, BackupService.kt), files of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive skipped with their reason instead of stalling the job (BackupService.kt), and the free run capped at 5 GB with Pro a one-time $9.99 purchase (ProPrefs.kt). None of that changes the picker verdict — it bounds it. The backup engine is proven; the end-to-end pick of this exact folder is not yet tested.

Photos already on the NAS vs photos you uploaded from the phone

The route is the same for both, but the starting point is not, and it is worth saying which one you are saving before you start. Photos already on the NAS — the case this article is really about — are the ones the save governs: with a save they land in the Download Folder, and without it they are not on the phone at all, and the methods above are written for them. Photos you uploaded from the phone are different, because you usually have a better original: a photo you sent to the NAS with Qfile Pro's auto-upload, or backed up with QuMagie Mobile, is almost certainly still in your camera roll, in DCIM/Camera, at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file that SnapDisk's default run already covers — and the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only, so it is covered either way. Back that up instead. The copy on the NAS is the file you uploaded — the same file, byte-for-byte — and the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping. That matters more than it sounds, because the photos you upload are often the ones you care about most: a picture of the finished work for a client, a photo of the goods before dispatch, the shot you took to show someone what something looks like. The original is the one they will ask for again. The QNAP route for a photo you uploaded is only needed when the original is gone — you deleted it from the camera roll, or it was a photo you took inside another app and never saved — and then the NAS copy is the only one left: open Qfile Pro, save it the same way as a NAS photo, and the default run picks it up like any other indexed photo, or point the From picker at the Download Folder and copy it directly.

One related corner is worth a line, because QNAP users hit it and it looks like a shortcut that is not one: Qfile Pro keeps its working data under Android/data, and a guide may tell you to dig in Android/data for a cached photo. That is a different thing from saved photos — the cached copies are fragments keyed to the installation, in a temporary folder, not your photos in any usable sense — and Android/data is guarded by scoped storage, so a file manager may not even show it. Do not go digging in Android/data for your QNAP photos: they are not there. The photos are in the Download Folder once saved, or nowhere at all when they are not, and those are the only two places this article's methods touch. And one QNAP-specific note for the uploaded half: because Qfile Pro's auto-upload and QuMagie Mobile's backup both run phone to NAS, a photo you uploaded is a copy, not a move — the original stays in the camera roll unless you deleted it yourself, and Free Up Space-style cleanup is not a QNAP feature to watch for here.

Checking the drive after the copy

The check has two halves, and they have to happen in order: first that the save actually produced a file on the phone, then that the drive holds it. After the SnapDisk run finishes, open the drive on a computer or on the phone itself and count — by file name, not by folder name, because QNAP publishes no absolute path for the Download Folder and this article will not guess what the drive copy's folder is called. The drive should hold the QNAP photos you saved, under their own names, and a few opened at random should render fully. Open a QNAP photo itself — not just any photo on the drive — because a run that covered the rest of the library would still look complete on a random check while the one set you cared about was missing. If the count is short, the run is not the first suspect — the save is. Check the Download Folder in Qfile Pro: is the photo actually there, and does it hold the photos you expected? If the Download Folder is empty, the photos were never saved — save the ones that matter from Qfile Pro, and run again. If the Download Folder holds them and the drive does not, the run is the suspect, and the run needs repeating, not a different folder.

If the drive's free space ran out mid-run, the run stops with the full error shown; free space by moving the drive's contents to a bigger drive first, or back up to a second drive. If the run stopped at the 5 GB free cap — unlikely for a QNAP album alone, possible on a whole-library default run — run it again and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. And the check that matters most for a photo that only existed on the NAS: confirm the drive copy before you delete anything from the phone or the NAS. The copy in the Download Folder is an ordinary file and will survive; the copy on the NAS depends on the NAS keeping it, and a deleted folder or a failed drive ends that. The drive is a plain folder of plain files. Anyone who picks up the drive can open them. That is the price of a copy you can open anywhere, and it is worth stating before the photos leave the phone.

What to avoid

  • Assuming viewing a photo in Qfile Pro or QuMagie puts it on your phone. It does not — viewing the photo shows you a streamed copy from the NAS, and QuMagie has no download step at all. A photo you only viewed lives on the NAS, not in any folder. A one-tap backup copies zero QNAP photos until you save them with Save to downloads first.
  • Assuming QuMagie Mobile can download. It cannot — QuMagie Mobile views, searches, tags, and shares the photos on your NAS, and it uploads photos from your phone to the NAS. Its own quick-start guide contains no download-to-device step. Open Qfile Pro for the download, not QuMagie.
  • Assuming Qfile Pro's auto-upload works NAS to phone. It does not — auto-upload moves photos from the phone to the NAS, and QuMagie Mobile's automatic backup does the same. Neither app documents an auto-download. Every photo still on the NAS needs the same Save to downloads tap each.
  • Assuming the one-touch-copy button copies to a phone-plugged drive. It does not — the button is on the NAS, and the drive plugs into the NAS front USB port. A drive plugged into the phone is not part of that copy. The NAS button is the whole-NAS route, standing at the NAS; the phone route is Qfile Pro's save.
  • Assuming a share link is a save. It is not — a Qfile Pro download link or a QuMagie share link opens the photo in a browser on the NAS. A link is not a file on your phone. Save to downloads is the step that writes the file; a link is the step that does not.
  • Assuming the saved photo lands in Downloads or DCIM. It does not — Qfile Pro saves land in its Download Folder, inside a subfolder named after your NAS, reached in the app at Menu, then Offline Files, then Download Folder. DCIM/Camera is where the camera puts its own photos. Looking in DCIM for a QNAP save is looking in the wrong folder — and QNAP publishes no absolute path for the Download Folder, so do not trust a guide that gives you one.
  • Assuming the saved file is a smaller or re-encoded copy. It is not — the download is the file exactly as it sits on the NAS, byte-for-byte. Qfile Pro downloads the file; no conversion step appears anywhere in QNAP's download docs. If a downloaded photo looks smaller than the original, the NAS holds the smaller file.
  • Assuming the default SnapDisk run covers photos you only viewed on the NAS. It cannot — proven from the code and the platform: the default run reads the media index, and a photo that was never saved never enters the index. A green backup copies zero QNAP photos from a NAS you never saved from.
  • Assuming the From picker route is test-proven. Untested — no SnapDisk test has picked the Download Folder yet. The code supports it and the folder name is ordinary, but do not plan a backup around it as the only route; Method 1's save-to-gallery-first workflow is the proven one.
  • Assuming the uploaded copy is the one to back up. A photo you uploaded to the NAS is usually still in DCIM/Camera at full quality. The NAS copy is the same file you uploaded, but the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping; use the NAS copy only when the original is gone.
  • Looking in Android/data for your QNAP photos. That folder holds Qfile Pro's working data — cached fragments in a temporary folder, not your photos — and scoped storage guards it. The photos are in the Download Folder once saved, or nowhere at all when they are not.
  • Assuming a download that has not appeared yet has failed. It may still be running — Qfile Pro runs downloads as background tasks, and a large folder takes a while on a weak connection. Give it time before saving it again, or you will save it twice.
  • Assuming screen-off continuation under battery saver. UNVERIFIED — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for a library with long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, and a library with long 4K clips will hold one. SnapDisk skips those files and lists them — use exFAT.
  • Assuming the free 5 GB cap covers the whole library. It is per run. A library over 5 GB needs several free runs — run it again and it picks up where it stopped — or one $9.99 Pro purchase.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. An unplug mid-copy leaves a half-copied library that looks finished until you count it.
  • Checking the drive by opening any photo. Open a QNAP photo itself — not just any photo on the drive. A random check passes even when the one set you cared about was never copied, because the photos that did copy render fine.

Rule of thumb: Save first, then back it up — viewing a photo in Qfile Pro or QuMagie is a look at the NAS, Save to downloads is the step that puts a file on your phone, and the Download Folder is the bridge to the drive. The save is one tap per photo or per folder; the backup after it is one tap for all of them.
