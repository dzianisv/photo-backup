Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. It mounts on plug-in on most Android phones with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. OTG support is confirmed in the sources I found on the original Explore, but unconfirmed on the Explore R — so on the Explore R the drive mounting is the test itself: if it appears in Settings > Storage, the phone hosts drives and the rest of this method works; if it never appears, the phone does not host drives and Method 4 is the route. Both models run Android 7.0 or later, so SnapDisk installs on either.
  2. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Land Rover Explore camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  3. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: a file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

A file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — the YouTube guides' route is an OTG connection with the built-in file manager, and askfilo's route is the same, and either is the manual route for external storage: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. 2. Open the file manager (Files by Google on Land Rover Explore phones) and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • OTG support is unconfirmed on the Explore R. The original Explore lists USB OTG support — FindPare lists "USB OTG: Yes" and Notebookcheck's review says the USB-C connection "supports USB OTG" — but no source I found mentions OTG on the Explore R at all, so plug a drive in and check Settings > Storage on that model before relying on it. The drive mounting is the test.
  • One spec page disagrees about the Explore R. mobilespecs.net lists the Explore R as Android 7.0 with a MediaTek Helio X27 — the original Explore's spec — while mobiles4sale, UL benchmarks, and outdoorsmagic list it as Android 9 with a Helio P35, so that page is not used as a source for the Explore R here. Either way the Explore R clears SnapDisk's Android 7.0 minimum.
  • The microSD capacity is unlisted in the sources found. Both models take a microSD card, but no source I found states the maximum size, so check your own card before relying on it — and a card is still not a USB drive: it stays in the phone and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader.
  • Both models have USB-C, so the adapter is the same either way. The Explore and the Explore R both have a USB Type-C port, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into either one and a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter on either one. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into either Explore model, both of which have USB-C — so an adapter is only needed for a regular USB-A drive, and it must be a USB-C OTG adapter, not a micro-USB one. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. Land Rover's own file advice is a computer copy with a USB cable — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a manual page for a phone-to-USB guide. The Explore manual and the Land Rover Explore spec tables are real Land Rover pages — but the manual only says to back settings up to Google's servers and to move files with a USB cable to a computer, and the spec tables only list an Android version, a chipset, or a USB connector type. Neither of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the phone.
  • Mistaking OTG support on one model for OTG support on the other. The original Explore lists USB OTG support — FindPare lists "USB OTG: Yes" and Notebookcheck's review says the USB-C connection "supports USB OTG" — but no source I found mentions OTG on the Explore R. Test with a drive on the Explore R, and use Method 2 or 4 on that model if the drive never mounts.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — both Explore models take a microSD card — but the card stays in the phone, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On the Explore the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
