What a panorama actually is

A panorama is a wide .jpg with, sometimes, a note inside it. There are two kinds, and the difference between them is the whole article. The first is the plain sweep: you pan left to right, the phone stitches one row of frames into a rectilinear or cylindrical projection and saves it flat. That file is a standard wide JPEG with no spherical metadata at all — the panorama-ness is just pixels, a very wide image, and any viewer that can open a .jpg can open it. The second is the Photo Sphere: the phone guides you through a grid of overlapping shots, stitches them into an equirectangular projection — a sphere flattened into a rectangle, the same way a map of the earth gets stretched at the poles — and embeds XMP metadata that tells the viewer to wrap it back into a sphere. Google's own Photo Sphere XMP page says the metadata lives in the Google Photo Sphere namespace, http://ns.google.com/photos/1.0/panorama/, embedded inside the image file according to the Adobe XMP standard, and that the GPano parameters — ProjectionType equirectangular, the compass heading, the crop fields — are what make a viewer display it as a photo sphere rather than a flat image. When Google Photos detects the GPano metadata in a JPEG, it adds a small sphere icon, and tapping it switches to a gyroscope-driven mode where you tilt and rotate the phone to look around.

The numbers make the shape concrete. A standard panorama is one row of frames stitched into a strip; a photosphere is a grid of overlapping shots across several rows, stitched into a full sphere. One panorama write-up puts a standard panorama at five to ten megabytes, and a photosphere of the same scene at fifteen to twenty-five — partly because it holds more total pixel data across the grid, and partly because the equirectangular projection is inherently less efficient at storing detail. On a Pixel 9, the panorama mode changed shape: Google's own blog says panorama mode used to work via video — the camera scanning the scene as you move — and now uses the HDR+ photo pipeline, stacking the photos and all their data together into a stitched panorama, with full 360 capabilities and even vertical panoramas. The file that comes out is still a .jpg in DCIM/Camera, whatever the pipeline behind it.

Two things follow from that shape, and they decide the backup. First, the file is big for a still: a panorama holds many frames' worth of pixels, so a panorama library adds up faster than a library of ordinary stills — and the 5 GB free cap below bites sooner than it does for stills, though far later than it does for video. Second, the risk is not that the file gets left behind — it is a .jpg in DCIM/Camera, and every route in this article copies .jpg files — but that the file gets re-saved. A copy that streams the bytes keeps the GPano note; a share, an edit, or a re-encode that writes a fresh file may not. That is the same shape as the slow-motion article's risk, where one file hides a note inside it, and the opposite of the burst article's risk, where many files hide behind one thumbnail — here one file hides a note inside it, the same shape as the motion photo in the WL-193 article, where one .jpg hides a video inside it. The check is different too: for a burst you count files, for a panorama you open it in the viewer and look for the spin.

One more form to know about, because it confuses people: a panorama that opens flat is not necessarily broken. A viewer that does not read the GPano tags shows the equirectangular image as a flat wide strip — stretched at the poles, distorted, looking wrong — and that proves nothing about the file. The metadata may still be inside it, and the same file opened in Google Photos on the phone may spin perfectly. The check is the phone's own Google Photos, which reads the note — not a computer viewer, which usually does not.

What you need

  • An Android phone with panoramas on it. On a Pixel, open the Camera app, tap Panorama, and sweep — or, on a Pixel 9 and later, follow the dots the camera shows you, pausing on each one while it snaps. On a Samsung Galaxy, open the Camera app, tap More, then Panorama, and sweep. Then check the count: open a panorama in Google Photos and look for the sphere icon — so you know which files are the Photo Spheres before you back them up. If a panorama opens as a flat wide strip with no sphere icon, it was a plain sweep, and no backup can make it spin.
  • A USB drive with enough free space. Count first: panoramas run larger than ordinary stills — a standard panorama is a few megabytes, a photosphere several times that — so add up the DCIM/Camera folder in the Files app before you buy the drive, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • A drive formatted exFAT. The format reference for this article is DriveForge's exFAT-vs-FAT32-vs-NTFS guide: exFAT has no 4 GB per-file limit and is read and written by current Android phones, Windows, and Mac. FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more — a single panorama never reaches that, but the long 4K videos sitting next to your panoramas can, and exFAT is the practical format for the whole folder. NTFS is a Windows format that most Android phones cannot open on their own — do not use it for a phone backup drive.
  • An OTG adapter if your drive is USB-A. A dual USB-C drive plugs straight in; a standard USB-A stick needs a data-capable OTG adapter. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for the drive.
  • SnapDisk v0.1.2. Not on Google Play yet — install the APK from the GitHub release page. It needs Android 7.0 or later (minSdk 24 in the repo). No account, no cloud, no ads, and no internet permission — your photos physically cannot leave the phone.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

This is the route this article is for, and for .jpg panoramas both routes are certain — which is the honest centre of this article, and different from the burst, HEIC and RAW articles, where only the folder route was certain. The default run — leave From unpicked — reads the phone's MediaStore Images table with no folder filter and no MIME filter, verified in the repo as PhotoQuery.queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, called from BackupService when no From folder is picked — and a panorama is a .jpg file, which every Android MediaStore indexes, so it is in the result set by construction. The folder route — tap the From picker, browse to the phone's storage, and choose DCIM or DCIM/Camera — is the certain one for every format in the list: the From picker is the system folder picker (verified in the repo as OpenDocumentTree), the tree walk recurses into subfolders, and it copies every file whose extension is in the list. .jpg is in that list, verified in the repo's PhotoQuery.kt, so a DCIM pick copies every panorama in the tree whatever its kind — plain sweep or Photo Sphere alike. Then pick the To folder on the drive, in the same picker, and tap Backup. The copy runs in the background: oldest-first by the date the photo was taken, with a progress notification showing percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, plus Pause and Stop.

There is no format split to know about here, and that is the point: a panorama is a .jpg, and .jpg is the one format every route covers. The default run is certain for panoramas for the same reason it is certain for ordinary stills — the MediaStore Images table holds every .jpg on the phone, panorama or not, because nothing in the row marks a file as a panorama. The folder route is certain in every case, and the default route is certain for .jpg. That is the whole rule, and it is simpler than the other format articles' rule because the common case here — .jpg — is covered by both routes.

The spin survives because the copy never touches the files' bytes. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — there is no transcoding step anywhere in its copy path — and it checks the size of every file after copying, renaming the .part file to the final file only after the size verification passes. A panorama that arrives on the drive is the same .jpg that left the phone, GPano metadata included. And here is the check that matters, because it is the one most people get wrong for panoramas: open it in the viewer, do not just look at the thumbnail. Copy one panorama back to the phone, open it in Google Photos, and look for the sphere viewer — tilt the phone and watch the scene move. Or, if you would rather not copy it back, compare the file size and dimensions on the drive against the phone: a matching size and matching dimensions mean the file arrived whole, and a whole file carries its metadata with it. A panorama that opens as a flat strip on a computer does not prove the spin is gone — most computer viewers do not read the GPano metadata, so they show the flat equirectangular image while the note is still inside it. The phone's own Google Photos is the check, because it reads the note. This article does not promise the spin survives on every viewer on every computer — that is a property of the viewer, not of the file — and it says so instead of promising it.

Two honest limits on this route. First, there is no panorama-only mode on the default run: it copies the whole library, oldest-first, panoramas mixed in with stills and ordinary videos in date order. If you want panoramas only, that is what the DCIM/Camera pick is for — and even then, the pick copies every photo in the folder, panorama and ordinary alike; there is no way to pick only the panoramas except by selecting them by hand in Method 2. Second, screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified: SnapDisk copies as a foreground service — the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no panorama run has ever been tested, so this article says so instead of promising it. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it. On Android 15 and later a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours, so a very long copy can be paused by Android itself, and opening the app resets the timer (WL-34).

What it does have, and what is genuinely panorama-relevant, is a choose-photos feature: back up just the photos and videos you pick. But its free tier is a softer trap for panoramas than for bursts or slow motion: 200 files or 2 GB per backup, and a panorama collection is usually dozens of files, not hundreds, so the 200-file cap rarely bites — the 2 GB cap is the one that can bite a big photosphere library, against SnapDisk's 5 GB per run with no file count. What SnapDisk adds is the oldest-first order across the whole library, the detailed progress notification with Pause and Stop, per-file size verification with .part resume, and this article's rule: both routes provably copy every panorama, because a panorama is a .jpg.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you would rather not install anything, the Files app does the same copy by hand — and for panoramas it has one advantage: you see exactly which files you selected. Plug the drive in, open Files by Google (or your phone's file manager), browse to DCIM/Camera, long-press one panorama, tap the rest, tap Copy, navigate to the USB drive, and tap Paste. The file manager shows you the individual .jpg files, not the gallery's panorama badge — so you can see the file sizes as you select, which is the check the gallery hides from you. Panoramas are the wide ones: in list view, a panorama's dimensions give it away, and its file is several times an ordinary still. Check the drive afterwards: compare the file sizes and dimensions against the phone, and copy one panorama back to the phone and open it in Google Photos to confirm the spin arrived.

Three warnings specific to panoramas on this route. First, copy in the Files app — do not share from the Gallery. Sharing a panorama from the Gallery hands the file to another app, and what that app does with it — re-encode it, strip the metadata, save a fresh file — is out of your control. Copy in the Files app moves the file itself; Share hands the file to another app. Second, do not use Move. The guides that suggest Move are telling you to delete the originals as the copy goes — and a Move that is interrupted halfway leaves some panoramas on the drive, some on the phone, and no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete — that order is what keeps your panoramas safe. Third, do not judge the copy by opening it on the computer. Most computer viewers do not read the GPano metadata, so a perfectly good copy opens as a flat wide strip on a computer — and that proves nothing about the file. The check is the phone's Google Photos, which reads the note.

What you give up is everything SnapDisk automates. There is no oldest-first order — you copy in whatever order you selected. There is no progress notification with speed and time left, no Pause and Stop, and no resume: if the drive is unplugged mid-copy, you start that file again and work out yourself which panoramas already arrived. There is no per-file size verification, and no skipped-file list — a file that does not fit, or a copy that silently stops, fails quietly. For a few panoramas that is fine. For a library of them, the manual route is an afternoon of tapping and checking, and the one-tap route is the honest recommendation. The manual route is also the fallback for the one thing SnapDisk's default run cannot do: copy panoramas only. If you want just the panoramas and nothing else, select them by hand in DCIM/Camera and copy them the same way.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

Before you trust the cloud with panoramas, check what it actually holds. Google Photos backs up the camera folder, and a panorama uploaded there keeps its file — but the default is not Original quality. Google's own help page says Storage saver uploads "may be compressed into a different image format, like a .jpg" — and it says more that bites panoramas specifically: photos larger than 16 MP are resized to 16 MP. A panorama is a wide, high-resolution image, often over 16 MP, so Storage saver resizes it — and a re-save that writes a fresh file has no reason to preserve the GPano metadata that makes it spin. Whether the spin survives a Storage saver re-encode is not something this article promises either way — no panorama run through the cloud has ever been tested here — so the rule is the same as for every other format in this series: copy to the drive first, at original quality, and check the copy, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. The setting is one tap in Photos settings — easy to have wrong for years without noticing, because the panorama looks identical as a flat strip.

One more cloud detail, because it confuses people with panoramas specifically: the spin is metadata, and metadata is the first thing a re-encode drops. A panorama that comes back from the cloud as a flat strip may have lost the note, or may be opening in a viewer that never read it — and from the phone alone you cannot tell which. That is why the check in this article is a copy back to the phone and an open in Google Photos, not a glance at a thumbnail. Copy to the USB drive first, open one panorama on the phone and watch it spin, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. And never delete the phone originals on the strength of a cloud badge alone. Check the drive copy, watch it spin, then delete — that order is what keeps your panoramas safe.

The 5 GB free cap, and why panoramas feel it less than video

SnapDisk's free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — verified in the repo as FREE_BACKUP_BYTES_LIMIT, 5 GiB — and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. For panoramas, 5 GB goes further than it does for video: a panorama library is usually dozens of files at a few megabytes each — a standard panorama five to ten megabytes, a photosphere fifteen to twenty-five — so a whole panorama collection is often hundreds of megabytes, not gigabytes, and one free run covers it. The cap bites when the library mixes panoramas with the long 4K videos sitting next to them in DCIM/Camera, which is what the folder route copies too. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (product id pro_unlock, verified in the repo's billing code) that auto-starts the backup when a disk is connected and removes the size limit, so a whole season copies in one run.

The cap is per run, not per library, and that distinction is what matters here: the free version can finish any panorama library if you run it again, because files already copied are never copied twice. The count to do before you start is the library size: open the Files app, look at the DCIM/Camera folder size, divide by 5 GB, and that is how many free runs the library needs. Do the arithmetic before you plug the drive in, not halfway through the copy.

One more size note: a file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped with its reason and the job continues — verified in the repo's BackupService. A single panorama never reaches that, but a long 4K video sitting next to your panoramas can — and that file is skipped and listed, the panoramas around it copy normally, and the fix is the same exFAT format recommended above.

What this does not do

  • It does not convert your panoramas — to anything. SnapDisk copies the .jpg exactly as it is. Converting a panorama to a smaller file, or to any other format, re-encodes it — and a re-encode is exactly the step that can drop the GPano metadata. That is your editor's or your computer's job, on a copy, never on the only original.
  • It does not stitch panoramas for you. Stitching is the camera's job — the sweep, the dots, the HDR+ pipeline — not SnapDisk's. SnapDisk copies the finished .jpg files; it does not make them.
  • It does not extract the sphere to a flat image. The spin stays inside the .jpg, as metadata — flattening it is a separate tool's job, on a copy, never on the only original. The backup's job is to keep the original intact.
  • It does not copy panoramas only on the default run. The default run copies the whole library, oldest-first, panoramas mixed in with stills and ordinary videos in date order. Panoramas only is the by-hand pick in Method 2 — and even then, you select them one by one in the file manager.
  • It does not promise the spin plays on every viewer. A viewer that does not read the GPano metadata shows a flat wide strip — the file is intact, the viewer just does not apply the spin. The check is the phone's own Google Photos, which reads the note.
  • It does not read or index your panoramas. There is no panorama detection and no search inside the files — SnapDisk copies files, and finding one panorama among a thousand .jpg files is your file manager's job, on the drive, after the copy.
  • It does not back up anything but photos and videos. No app data, no messages, no system state — and no browser tabs, notes, or clipboard history. Those live in the app's own storage, not in the photo folders, and they need their own backup.
  • It does not delete anything. SnapDisk only copies — the only delete calls in its source touch .part files, never your photos — so freeing space is a two-step flow you do yourself: copy to the drive, open one panorama on the phone and watch it spin, then delete the originals from your phone. That flow is covered step by step in How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive.
  • It does not promise the copy keeps running with the screen off under battery saver. No wake lock, no battery-saver handling in the repo, no panorama run ever tested — stated plainly above, not promised. Keep the phone awake for the run.
  • It does not restore or view your photos. There is no in-app viewer and no restore feature — you open the copied .jpg files in a viewer or on your computer yourself. The SnapDisk vs VaultDrive comparison covers what the rivals add here, and what they charge for it.

What to avoid

  • Assuming a panorama is a special format. A panorama is an ordinary wide .jpg — and a Photo Sphere is the same .jpg with a note inside it. The backup does not need to understand panoramas — it needs to copy the file without changing it.
  • Sharing the panorama when you meant to copy it. Sharing from the Gallery hands the file to another app, which may re-encode it and drop the metadata. Copy in the Files app; Share hands the file to another app.
  • Judging the copy by opening it on a computer. Most computer viewers do not read the GPano metadata, so a good copy opens as a flat wide strip there. The check is the phone's Google Photos — copy one panorama back and watch it spin.
  • Letting Storage saver re-encode the panoramas. Google Photos Storage saver uploads "may be compressed into a different image format, like a .jpg" — and photos larger than 16 MP are resized to 16 MP, which catches most panoramas. Copy to the drive first, at original quality.
  • Assuming the default run misses panoramas. It does not — a panorama is a .jpg in the MediaStore Images table, and the default run reads that table with no filter. Both routes are certain for panoramas.
  • Using Move instead of Copy in the Files app. Move deletes the originals as the copy goes — an interrupted Move leaves panoramas split between phone and drive with no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete.
  • Formatting the drive NTFS. Most Android phones cannot open NTFS on their own. Use exFAT — DriveForge's guide is the format reference — and keep FAT32 only for a drive an older device must read.
  • Buying a drive before counting the library. Panoramas run larger than ordinary stills — a photosphere can be fifteen to twenty-five megabytes. Count the DCIM/Camera folder first, then buy the size, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy — and for a panorama, the check is opening it in Google Photos and watching it spin. Copy, watch it spin, then delete from the phone — never the other way round.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Unverified — no wake lock, no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  • Starting a multi-gigabyte copy on a nearly dead battery. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Searching for the wrong thing. The pages the search returns are generic transfer guides, our own general articles, and a Google Photos community thread — none of them is a panorama backup guide. Search for the panorama photos, not the photos, and the gap is obvious.

Rule of thumb: a panorama is an ordinary wide .jpg — and a Photo Sphere is the same .jpg with a note inside it — so the copy has to be byte-for-byte, and the check has to prove the spin is still there. Copy, copy one panorama back and watch it spin in Google Photos, then delete. That order is what keeps your panoramas safe.
