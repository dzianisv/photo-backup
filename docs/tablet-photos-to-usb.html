That is Method 1. The manual versions get Methods 2 and 3, because the guides already describe them and the one-tap version is the only thing left to add.

What you need

  • An Android tablet that supports USB OTG, or an iPad with a USB-C or Lightning port. OTG is the first thing to check on an Android tablet — most modern tablets have it, per the ssa-digital guide, but some budget or kids tablets do not, and on some tablets it has to be switched on in Settings first. If the tablet has no host support, no method below works on it — see Method 5.
  • A USB drive with enough free space for the whole library, including videos. A tablet library is often bigger than a phone library — a shared family tablet holds years of photos — so check the size before you start, and buy a bigger drive before the copy, not during it.
  • An adapter that matches the tablet's port, unless the drive already matches it: a USB-C drive plugs straight into a newer tablet; a regular USB drive needs a USB-C to USB-A adapter on a newer tablet, or a micro-USB OTG cable on an older tablet. On iPad, a USB-C adapter for USB-C models, or Apple's Lightning to USB 3 Camera Adapter for Lightning models.
  • An exFAT drive if the tablet supports it — exFAT is what every guide recommends, it works on Android and iPadOS, and it has no 4 GB single-file limit. A drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied — and reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • For a spinning hard drive, or a camera card and a drive at the same time: a powered USB-C hub and a power source — a PD wall adapter at home, a PD power bank in the field. A tablet supplies limited power over its port, per the thecomputerbasics guide, and a spinning drive may appear and then disconnect.
  • For a tablet with a microSD slot: the card can stay in the slot while it backs up — the ZTE Axon tablet, for one, takes microSD up to 128 GB, per mobilespecs — but check the card's folders before picking, because a card formatted by a camera may not use DCIM.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do not delete anything from the tablet until the check is done.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the tablet will keep filling up — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the tablet to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One port, one job at a time — unless there is a hub. The tablet's own photos need only the drive; a camera card plus a drive needs the hub. The methods below run in that order.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the Android tablet, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely inside the tablet — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start.

  1. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for photos stored in internal storage: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  2. Check From. If the tablet's photos live in internal storage, leave it on the default. If they live on the tablet's microSD card, tap the From picker, browse to the card — it appears as removable storage in the picker — and choose the card's DCIM folder, or whatever folder on the card holds the photos. The picker is the system folder picker (OpenDocumentTree in MainActivity.kt), so it reaches the card, not just internal storage. If the card was formatted by a camera, check its folders first: the photos may sit in a folder that is not called DCIM.
  3. Check To. It should point at the drive — /disk/DCIM by default. If the drive is not plugged in yet, plug it in now and let the tablet see it before you pick.
  4. Plug the USB drive into the tablet. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  5. Tap Backup in the notification. SnapDisk copies photos and videos oldest-first from the tablet to the drive, in the background, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish. A tablet library can be large, so this is the step where the progress bar earns its keep — and if you unplug the drive mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back, because files already copied are never copied twice, per the repo README.
  7. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good. Keep the tablet as the second copy until you have checked — SnapDisk only copies, it never deletes.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one notification tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. On a tablet with a library bigger than 5 GB, that means several taps — which is exactly the case Pro is for: Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size — and it is the hands-free route here: with Pro set up, plugging the drive in starts the backup with no tap at all, so the tablet backs itself up every time you plug the drive in. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

Method 2: file manager — the Android tablet, by hand

Same tablet, no app to install. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every tablet guide above describes:

  1. Plug the USB drive into the tablet — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive, or a micro-USB OTG cable on an older tablet.
  2. Check the tablet sees the drive. If nothing appears, the drive does not mount and this method does not work on that tablet — check the OTG setting first (Settings > Connected Devices > USB OTG, or similar, per the ssa-digital guide), then try a different adapter, then check the model's specs for host support.
  3. Open the tablet's file manager and find the photos — usually DCIM/Camera in internal storage, or the card's DCIM folder if the tablet has a microSD slot and the photos live there.
  4. Select the photos and videos, copy them to the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish. Copy, not Move: the file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the tablet.
  5. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.

The file-manager copy works on any Android tablet with USB OTG, with no version floor at all — it is the route when SnapDisk cannot install, because the tablet runs below Android 7.0. What it does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if you unplug mid-copy — and on a tablet library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time. The ceedo guide's warning applies here too: using the wrong adapter is the number one cause of failed transfers, so match the adapter to the port before you blame the drive.

Method 3: iPad — Photos and Files, by hand

An iPad has no microSD slot, SnapDisk does not run on it, and there is no one-tap version on the iPad side — the copy is the Files app, step by step, as the ceedo and thecomputerbasics guides describe:

  1. Plug the drive into the iPad — a USB-C adapter on iPad models with USB-C, Apple's Lightning to USB 3 Camera Adapter on older models. Check the drive appears in the Files app under Locations before selecting anything.
  2. In Photos, select the pictures and videos you want. For archival originals, use Share and Export Unmodified Original — the export choice matters, per the pixoneye guide: exporting as-is preserves the original file, and exporting a copy may convert it.
  3. Choose the drive in the Files destination picker and save into a folder you can identify later. Wait for the export to finish — a large library takes a while, and the iPad needs to stay awake and powered.
  4. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If the originals live only in iCloud with Optimize iPad Storage on, download them to the iPad first: a cloud-only photo is not on the iPad to copy.

The iPad route is honest manual work, and it has one trap the Android route does not: Photos and the file system can show different collections, so a DCIM copy and a Photos export are not the same set — check what is actually on the drive, not what the gallery count says. If the iPad is the family tablet and the library is large, the export runs in batches, and each batch gets its own folder and its own check.

Method 4: the tablet as a bridge for a camera card

This is the PetaPixel route, and it is the one place the phone-bridge pattern from the SD-card article reappears for tablets — because here the photos are not the tablet's, they are the camera's, and the tablet is the bridge between the card and the drive:

  1. Connect the powered hub to the tablet, and power the hub — a PD wall adapter at home, a PD power bank in the field. A powered hub gives the connected devices their own power, so the tablet stays charged during a long transfer instead of draining.
  2. Insert the camera's card into the hub's SD slot, or into a card reader plugged into the hub. Plug the SSD or USB drive into the hub as well.
  3. Open the tablet's file manager. The card and the drive both appear — on iPad in the Files app, on Android in Google Files or the maker's file manager.
  4. Select the photos and videos on the card, tap Copy, choose the drive as the destination, and tap Paste. The files go straight from the card to the drive without ever taking up space on the tablet's internal storage — that is the whole point of the hub, and it is what the simple two-step setup cannot do.
  5. Check the copy the way the bridge guides insist on: the file count and total size look right, then open a few photos and a video directly from the backup drive and make sure they actually are readable. The PetaPixel guide's version of the same rule is the three-copy habit: one card in the camera, one card on your person, one SSD in the checked bag.
  6. Safely eject everything once done — skipping that step risks data corruption, and a corrupted backup on the road is the costly mistake this whole workflow exists to prevent.

The one-tap version of this method is SnapDisk with the From picker pointed at the reader, on an Android tablet: plug the hub in, check the tablet sees the card, then in SnapDisk tap the From picker, browse to the card reader, and choose the card's folder — the picker is the system folder picker, so a mounted reader appears in it the same way the tablet's own card does — and tap Backup when the drive connects. The copy then runs oldest-first with progress, Pause and Stop, and resume — the parts the Files-app copy does not give you. If the reader does not mount, the file-manager copy in the steps above is the fallback, and the hub, the reader, or the card format is the thing to check.

Method 5: when the tablet has no microSD slot, or no OTG

Two different dead ends, and they are not the same problem. The slot is a convenience; the host support is the gate.

  • No microSD slot. Normal — iPads never have one, and some Android tablets do not either. It changes nothing about backing up the tablet's own photos, which live in internal storage: Methods 1 and 2 run from the default folders, and the slot was never the point. The slot only decides whether a card can stay inside the tablet while it backs up — and a microSD card from another device, a drone or an action camera or a second tablet, goes into a reader and Method 4 applies exactly as written.
  • No USB OTG. This one stops everything: without host support the tablet cannot mount a drive at all, and then neither the one-tap backup nor the file-manager copy works on it. Check the model's specs before buying an adapter — the ZTE Axon Pad's own backup answers never mention a USB drive at all, which is the tell that the drive route was not the maker's route for that tablet — and on some tablets the OTG setting has to be switched on in Settings first, so check that before concluding the tablet cannot do it.
  • Windows tablet. A Windows tablet usually has a full-size USB-A port, per the ceedo guide, so no adapter is needed — but SnapDisk is Android only and does not run on it. The copy there is File Explorer, drive to drive, and this article's Android and iPad methods do not apply.
  • Micro-USB tablet. Older Android tablets still use micro-USB, and they need a micro-USB OTG cable, not a USB-C adapter — the port shape decides the adapter, and a USB-C adapter will not fit a micro-USB port no matter how hard you push it.
  • Do not buy a tablet for its slot. If you are choosing a tablet because you want card backup, the slot is one feature among many — and a tablet without a slot still backs up its own photos to USB with one tap, and still reads a camera card through a reader. The slot only decides whether the card can stay inside the tablet while it backs up.

In short: the slot decides which method you use, not whether the backup happens — and the host support decides whether the backup happens at all. No slot means the generic route for the tablet's photos and the reader route for the camera's card, and both of those are covered above.

What this does not do

  • Does not run SnapDisk on an iPad or a Windows tablet. SnapDisk is Android only. On the iPad side the copy is the Files app, as Method 3 describes, or a rival app — and on a Windows tablet it is File Explorer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a tablet running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the file-manager copy has no version floor.
  • Does not mount a drive the tablet cannot host. The tablet has to support USB OTG to see the drive at all. Most Android tablets do, but a tablet that lists OTG No cannot mount a drive, and then neither the one-tap backup nor the file-manager copy works on it.
  • Does not power a spinning hard drive by itself. A tablet supplies limited power over its port — a spinning drive may appear and then disconnect, per the thecomputerbasics guide. Use a flash drive or an SSD, or a powered hub for a spinning drive.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not delete photos. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the tablet keeps its photos until you delete them yourself, and that step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data.
  • Does not download your cloud originals for you. If your originals live only in iCloud or Google Photos, you have to download them to the tablet first — SnapDisk copies what is on the tablet, and a cloud-only photo is in neither place.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the tablet. Copy first, verify, then delete — the same rule the SD-card article makes about the Files app's Move option.
  • Deleting from the tablet before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the tablet — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. On a tablet library the copy runs long, so the pause matters more, not less.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — it is what every guide recommends — and check the drive's format before the trip, not after you are relying on it.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the tablet before the trip.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — and with room to keep backing up the same tablet afterwards. A tablet library is often bigger than you think; check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the tablet to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable OTG adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the tablet's port before buying — the ceedo guide calls the wrong adapter the number one cause of failed transfers.
  • Assuming the tablet supports OTG. Most modern tablets do, but some budget or kids tablets do not — and on some tablets the setting has to be switched on first. Check the model's specs and the OTG setting before you buy the adapter.
  • Assuming the photos all sit in DCIM. A tablet's photos may live in several folders — camera, screenshots, downloads, app folders — and a card formatted by a camera may use a folder layout other than DCIM. Check the tablet's folders before picking, and pick the folder that actually holds the photos.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Photos backs your tablet up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup. And a cloud backup stops working the day you stop paying for storage. The mobilespecs and devicesfaq tablet backup guides are cloud or computer routes for exactly this reason — they never produce a drive you can hold.
  • Expecting Pro to follow you to an iPad. SnapDisk Pro is a Google Play purchase, so it follows your Google account to another Android tablet — it does not follow you to an iPad, and SnapDisk does not run on iPad at all.

Rule of thumb: the tablet is the original — the drive is the copy, and the copy is only a backup once you have opened photos on the drive and checked them. Point the From picker at the tablet's library, tap Backup, wait, check, and only then think about deleting anything from the tablet.
