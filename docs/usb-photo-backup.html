USB photo backup on Android: back up photos to an external drive without uploading them

Your phone's camera roll fits on a drive you can hold. A USB photo backup keeps the whole copy in your pocket — no upload, no monthly fee, no account, and nobody else's server ever sees your pictures.

November 14, 2026 · 4 min read

TL;DR: To back up photos to an external drive from Android, plug a USB drive into your phone and copy them to it. The easy way is SnapDisk: plug in the disk, tap Backup in the notification, and your photos and videos copy oldest-first in the background, with live progress and Pause / Stop. The free version covers 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size. If you'd rather do it by hand, the Files app does the same job — just slower and with no resume if you unplug mid-copy.

What you need

  • An Android phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk).
  • An external drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, an external hard drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone doesn't see the drive, try a different adapter — and note that a spinning hard drive may need its own power.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check the drive's free space first — a phone's camera roll is often tens of gigabytes.

One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the external drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?"
  2. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally.
  3. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  4. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice.

If you unplug the drive mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup.

The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you'd rather not install anything, Android's built-in Files app can do the same copy:

  1. Plug in the external drive. A notification or a "USB drive" entry should appear in the Files app under Storage devices.
  2. Open Files, browse to Internal storage → DCIM → Camera. That's where your phone's photos live. (Screenshots are in DCIM → Screenshots; other apps keep their own folders.)
  3. Select the photos, tap ⋮ → Copy to (or Move to, if you want them off the phone), then choose the external drive as the destination.
  4. Wait for the copy to finish — and don't unplug the drive until the Files app says it's done.

This works fine for a one-off copy of a few dozen photos. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time. For a full camera roll, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

What to avoid

  • Cloud-only "backup" that uploads everything first. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's not a USB backup — it's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying.
  • Copying without verifying. A copy that reports "done" without checking sizes can silently leave you with half a file. Whatever method you use, spot-check a few photos on the drive before deleting them from the phone.
  • Deleting from the phone first. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, verify, then free the space — never the other way round.

Rule of thumb: a backup you can hold in your hand beats a backup you have to log into. Copy to the drive, check a few files open, and only then delete anything from the phone.
