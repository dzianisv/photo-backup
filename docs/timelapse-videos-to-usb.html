What a timelapse video actually is

A timelapse video is a normal video file whose frames are spaced out. Your phone records it as an ordinary .mp4 saved in DCIM/Camera like every other video the camera shoots, and the speed is in the frames themselves: the camera captures individual frames at a frequency much lower than the rate they are played back at — Google's own Pixel time-lapse page says exactly that — and plays them back at normal speed, so hours of real time become seconds of video. There is no second file holding the speed, and — this is the point the whole article turns on — there is no metadata note holding it either.

The numbers make the shape concrete. On a Pixel, open the Camera app, switch to video mode, and tap Time Lapse — Google's own page lists the settings you get there: the interval between frames, the resolution (720p, 1080p, 4K), and the speed, 5x or 120x. At 120x, twenty minutes of recording becomes a ten-second clip, as Android Authority's walkthrough of the Pixel mode records. On a Samsung Galaxy, open the Camera app, tap More, then Hyperlapse — Hyperlapse is Samsung's name for the same mode — and pick a speed: 4x, 8x, 16x, and sometimes 32x, as the Samsung guides list them. HowToGeek's Hyperlapse walkthrough puts the file plainly: recording a hyperlapse is just like recording any other video — tap the shutter, let it roll, tap again to stop, and the finished clip is saved to the gallery. One distinction worth knowing: a hyperlapse is a timelapse where the camera moves a long distance while recording — all hyperlapses are timelapses, but not all timelapses are hyperlapses — and either way the file that comes out is an ordinary video.

Two things follow from that shape, and they decide the backup. First, the risk is not that the file gets left behind — it is a .mp4 in DCIM/Camera, and every route in this article copies .mp4 files — and it is not that the speed gets dropped, because there is no note to drop. That is the opposite of the slow-motion article's risk, where one file hides a note inside it that a re-encode can strip, and the opposite of the panorama article's risk, where a Photo Sphere hides GPano metadata that a re-save can drop. A timelapse survives even a re-encode with its speed intact — what a re-encode changes is the resolution and the quality, not the pace. Second, the real risk is human: the clip looks wrong. A few seconds of video for hours of recording reads as a broken or failed recording, and the mistake is deleting it as one — or sharing it from the Gallery and getting back a re-encoded copy at lower resolution, which still plays fast but is no longer the original file. The check is different too: for a slow-motion clip you watch it play slowly in the phone's Gallery, because only a player that reads the metadata applies the slow part — for a timelapse you just play it, on any player, because the speed is in the frames and every player plays it fast.

One more form to know about, because it confuses people: a timelapse made from an ordinary video afterwards. Third-party apps can take a normal clip and speed it up into a timelapse — and the file that comes out is, again, an ordinary .mp4 with the speed baked into the frames. The point is the same either way: what you back up is files, and the speed lives in the frames, never in a separate timelapse format or a metadata note.

What you need

  • An Android phone with timelapse videos on it. On a Pixel, open the Camera app, switch to video mode, and tap Time Lapse — speeds from 5x to 120x. On a Samsung Galaxy, open the Camera app, tap More, then Hyperlapse, and pick 4x, 8x, 16x or 32x. Then check the count: open a clip and watch it run fast, so you know which files are the timelapses before you back them up. If a clip plays at normal speed, it was not recorded as a timelapse, and no backup can make it fast.
  • A USB drive with enough free space. Count first: a timelapse covers hours in a short clip, so the file is small for the time it spans — but a long 4K timelapse is still a large file, and one timelapse guide notes that recording an hour in 4K can take 3–5 GB of storage — so add up the DCIM/Camera folder in the Files app before you buy the drive, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • A drive formatted exFAT. The format reference for this article is DriveForge's exFAT-vs-FAT32-vs-NTFS guide: exFAT has no 4 GB per-file limit and is read and written by current Android phones, Windows, and Mac. FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more — a long 4K timelapse can reach that — and exFAT is the practical format for the whole folder. NTFS is a Windows format that most Android phones cannot open on their own — do not use it for a phone backup drive.
  • An OTG adapter if your drive is USB-A. A dual USB-C drive plugs straight in; a standard USB-A stick needs a data-capable OTG adapter. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for the drive.
  • SnapDisk v0.1.2. Not on Google Play yet — install the APK from the GitHub release page. It needs Android 7.0 or later (minSdk 24 in the repo). No account, no cloud, no ads, and no internet permission — your photos physically cannot leave the phone.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

This is the route this article is for, and for timelapse clips both routes are certain — which is the honest centre of this article, and different from the HEIC article, where only the folder route was certain for .heic. The default run — leave From unpicked — reads the phone's MediaStore Video table with no folder filter and no MIME filter, verified in the repo as PhotoQuery.queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, called from BackupService when no From folder is picked — and a timelapse clip is a .mp4 or .mov file, which every Android MediaStore indexes, so it is in the result set by construction. The folder route — tap the From picker, browse to the phone's storage, and choose DCIM or DCIM/Camera — is the certain one for every format in the list: the From picker is the system folder picker (verified in the repo as OpenDocumentTree), the tree walk recurses into subfolders, and it copies every file whose extension is in the list. Both .mp4 and .mov are in that list, verified in the repo's PhotoQuery.kt, so a DCIM pick copies every timelapse clip in the tree whatever its format. Then pick the To folder on the drive, in the same picker, and tap Backup. The copy runs in the background: oldest-first by the date the video was taken, with a progress notification showing percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, plus Pause and Stop.

There is no format split to know about here: the default run is certain for .mp4 and for .mov — both are indexed on every phone, .mov as video/quicktime in Android's platform MIME map — so whichever your phone saves timelapse as, the default run covers it. The folder route is certain in every case too. That is the whole rule, and it is simpler than the HEIC article's rule because the common case here — .mp4 and .mov alike — is covered by both routes.

The speed survives because the copy never touches the files' bytes. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — there is no transcoding step anywhere in its copy path — and it checks the size of every file after copying, renaming the .part file to the final file only after the size verification passes. A timelapse clip that arrives on the drive is the same .mp4 that left the phone, frames spaced out exactly as recorded. And here is the check that matters, because it is the one most people get wrong for timelapse: play it, do not just look at it. Play one clip back on the phone and watch it run fast — or, if you would rather not copy it back, compare the file size and duration on the drive against the phone: a matching size and a matching duration mean the file arrived whole. Unlike the slow-motion check, this one cannot fail by player: any player plays a timelapse fast, because the speed is in the frames, not in a note a player has to read. A timelapse that plays at normal speed on the drive would mean the file itself was changed — the check is the file, not the player. This article does not promise a shared or re-encoded copy is the original — that is a property of the share, not of the file — and it says so instead of promising it.

Two honest limits on this route. First, there is no timelapse-only mode on the default run: it copies the whole library, oldest-first, timelapse clips mixed in with stills and ordinary videos in date order. If you want timelapse clips only, that is what the DCIM/Camera pick is for — and even then, the pick copies every video in the folder, timelapse and ordinary alike; there is no way to pick only the timelapses except by selecting them by hand in Method 2. Second, screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified: SnapDisk copies as a foreground service — the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no timelapse run has ever been tested, so this article says so instead of promising it. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it. On Android 15 and later a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours, so a very long copy can be paused by Android itself, and opening the app resets the timer (WL-34).

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you would rather not install anything, the Files app does the same copy by hand — and for timelapse it has one advantage: you see exactly which files you selected. Plug the drive in, open Files by Google (or your phone's file manager), browse to DCIM/Camera, long-press one timelapse clip, tap the rest, tap Copy, navigate to the USB drive, and tap Paste. The file manager shows you the individual .mp4 files, not the gallery's timelapse badge — so you can see the file sizes as you select, which is the check the gallery hides from you. Check the drive afterwards: compare the file sizes and durations against the phone, and play one clip on the phone and watch it run fast to confirm the speed arrived.

Three warnings specific to timelapse on this route. First, copy in the Files app — do not share from the Gallery. Sharing a timelapse clip from the Gallery hands the file to another app, and what that app does with it — re-encode it at lower resolution, save a fresh file — is out of your control. The speed would survive, because it is in the frames, but the file that comes back may not be the original. Copy in the Files app moves the file itself; Share hands the file to another app. Second, do not use Move. The guides that suggest Move are telling you to delete the originals as the copy goes — and a Move that is interrupted halfway leaves some clips on the drive, some on the phone, and no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete — that order is what keeps your timelapse clips safe. Third, do not judge the copy by the file size. A timelapse is short — a few seconds for hours of recording — so a small file is normal, and a small file is not a failed copy. The check is playing it: a timelapse that runs fast is a timelapse that arrived.

What you give up is everything SnapDisk automates. There is no oldest-first order — you copy in whatever order you selected. There is no progress notification with speed and time left, no Pause and Stop, and no resume: if the drive is unplugged mid-copy, you start that file again and work out yourself which clips already arrived. There is no per-file size verification, and no skipped-file list — a file that does not fit, or a copy that silently stops, fails quietly. For a few clips that is fine. For a library of them, the manual route is an afternoon of tapping and checking, and the one-tap route is the honest recommendation. The manual route is also the fallback for the clips MediaStore does not index: the default run reads the MediaStore Video table with no format filter, so a .mov timelapse clip is included like any .mp4 — but a clip in a folder MediaStore skips, for example a folder containing a .nomedia file, never reaches the default run, and the way to copy it is to select it by hand in DCIM/Camera and copy it the same way.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

Before you trust the cloud with timelapse, check what it actually holds. Google Photos backs up the camera folder, and a timelapse clip uploaded there keeps its file — but the default is not Original quality. Google's own help page says Storage saver stores photos and videos "at a slightly reduced quality" — and it says more that bites timelapse specifically: videos higher than 1080p will be resized to high-definition 1080p, and "some information, like closed captions, might be lost". A 4K timelapse backed up at Storage saver comes back as a 1080p file — still fast, because the speed is in the frames and a resize changes resolution, not frame spacing — but no longer the original file. Whether the speed survives a Storage saver re-encode is not something this article promises either way — no timelapse run through the cloud has ever been tested here — so the rule is the same as for every other format in this series: copy to the drive first, at original quality, and check the copy, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. The setting is one tap in Photos settings — easy to have wrong for years without noticing, because the clip still plays fast at 1080p.

One more cloud detail, because it confuses people with timelapse specifically: the speed is in the frames, and the resolution is the thing a re-encode changes. A timelapse that comes back from the cloud looking soft or low-resolution has not lost its speed — it has lost its pixels — and from the phone alone you cannot tell whether the file on the cloud is the original or a resized copy. That is why the check in this article is a copy to the drive first and a play on the phone, not a glance at a cloud thumbnail. Copy to the USB drive first, play one clip and watch it run fast, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. And never delete the phone originals on the strength of a cloud badge alone. Check the drive copy, watch it run fast, then delete — that order is what keeps your timelapse clips safe.

The 5 GB free cap, and why a timelapse feels it less

SnapDisk's free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — verified in the repo as FREE_BACKUP_BYTES_LIMIT, 5 GiB — and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. For timelapse, 5 GB goes further than it does for ordinary video, and much further than for slow motion: a timelapse covers hours in a short clip, so the file is small for the time it spans — where a second of Super Slow-mo holds 960 frames, a timelapse holds a few frames for a whole minute of real time. A whole timelapse collection is often one free run. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (product id pro_unlock, verified in the repo's billing code) that auto-starts the backup when a disk is connected and removes the size limit, so a whole season copies in one run.

The cap is per run, not per library, and that distinction is what matters here: the free version can finish any timelapse library if you run it again, because files already copied are never copied twice. The count to do before you start is the library size: open the Files app, look at the DCIM/Camera folder size, divide by 5 GB, and that is how many free runs the library needs. Do the arithmetic before you plug the drive in, not halfway through the copy.

One more size note: a file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped with its reason and the job continues — verified in the repo's BackupService. A single timelapse rarely reaches that, but a long 4K one can — and that file is skipped and listed, the clips around it copy normally, and the fix is the same exFAT format recommended above.

What this does not do

  • It does not convert your timelapse clips — to anything. SnapDisk copies the .mp4 exactly as it is. Converting a clip to a smaller file, or to any other format, re-encodes it — and a re-encode is exactly the step that can drop the resolution, even though the speed survives. That is your editor's or your computer's job, on a copy, never on the only original.
  • It does not slow a timelapse down, or make ordinary videos into timelapses. Turning an ordinary clip into a timelapse is the camera's Time Lapse mode — or an editor's speed change, on a copy — not SnapDisk's job. SnapDisk copies files; it does not edit them.
  • It does not split a timelapse into still frames. The frames stay inside the .mp4 — extracting them is a separate tool's job, on a copy, never on the only original. The backup's job is to keep the original intact.
  • It does not copy timelapse clips only on the default run. The default run copies the whole library, oldest-first, timelapse clips mixed in with stills and ordinary videos in date order. Timelapse clips only is the DCIM/Camera pick in Method 1 — and even then, every video in the folder copies, timelapse and ordinary alike.
  • It does not promise a shared copy is the original. Sharing hands the file to another app, which may re-encode it at lower resolution — the speed survives, because it is in the frames, but the pixels may not. The check is the drive copy, not the shared one.
  • It does not read or index your timelapse clips. There is no timelapse detection and no search inside the clips — SnapDisk copies files, and finding one clip among a thousand .mp4 files is your file manager's job, on the drive, after the copy.
  • It does not back up anything but photos and videos. No app data, no messages, no system state — and no browser tabs, notes, or clipboard history. Those live in the app's own storage, not in the photo folders, and they need their own backup.
  • It does not delete anything. SnapDisk only copies — the only delete calls in its source touch .part files, never your photos — so freeing space is a two-step flow you do yourself: copy to the drive, play one clip and watch it run fast, then delete the originals from your phone. That flow is covered step by step in How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive.
  • It does not promise the copy keeps running with the screen off under battery saver. No wake lock, no battery-saver handling in the repo, no timelapse run ever tested — stated plainly above, not promised. Keep the phone awake for the run.
  • It does not restore or view your photos. There is no in-app viewer and no restore feature — you open the copied .mp4 files in a viewer or on your computer yourself. The SnapDisk vs VaultDrive comparison covers what the rivals add here, and what they charge for it.

What to avoid

  • Assuming timelapse is a special format. A timelapse clip is an ordinary .mp4 with the speed baked into the frames. The backup does not need to understand timelapse — it needs to copy the file without changing it.
  • Mistaking a timelapse for a broken clip. A few seconds of video for hours of recording is the point, not a failed recording. Play it before deleting anything — a timelapse that runs fast is a timelapse that arrived.
  • Sharing the clip when you meant to copy it. Sharing from the Gallery hands the file to another app, which may re-encode it at lower resolution. Copy in the Files app; Share hands the file to another app.
  • Judging the copy by the file size. A timelapse is short, so a small file is normal — hours of real time in a few seconds of video. The check is playing it, not weighing it.
  • Letting Storage saver resize the 4K timelapses. Google Photos Storage saver resizes videos higher than 1080p to 1080p — Google's own words. Copy to the drive first, at original quality.
  • Using Move instead of Copy in the Files app. Move deletes the originals as the copy goes — an interrupted Move leaves clips split between phone and drive with no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete.
  • Formatting the drive NTFS. Most Android phones cannot open NTFS on their own. Use exFAT — DriveForge's guide is the format reference — and keep FAT32 only for a drive an older device must read.
  • Buying a drive before counting the library. Timelapse files are small for the hours they cover, but a long 4K timelapse is still a large file. Count the DCIM/Camera folder first, then buy the size, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy — and for a timelapse clip, the check is playing it and watching it run fast. Copy, watch it run fast, then delete from the phone — never the other way round.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Unverified — no wake lock, no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  • Starting a multi-gigabyte copy on a nearly dead battery. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Searching for the wrong thing. The pages the search returns are generic transfer guides, our own general articles, and two OTG walkthrough videos — none of them is a timelapse backup guide. Search for the timelapse videos, not the videos, and the gap is obvious.

Rule of thumb: a timelapse video is an ordinary .mp4 with the speed baked into the frames — there is no note to lose, so the copy just has to be byte-for-byte, and the check has to prove the clip still plays fast. Pick DCIM/Camera as From, copy, play one clip back on the phone and watch it run fast, then delete. That order is what keeps your timelapse clips safe.
