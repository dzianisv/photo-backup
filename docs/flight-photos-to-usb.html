Why the night before a flight is the deadline

A flight is not a reminder to back up — it is a deadline, and the deadline is the night before, not the gate:

  • The phone is the only copy until the drive copy finishes and is checked. If the photos live only on the phone, they are one drop, one pickpocket, one forgotten taxi seat away from gone — and a trip is when all three happen most.
  • A trip is when phones get lost. Airports, taxis, hotels, beaches, restaurants — every handoff is a chance to leave the phone behind, and a phone left in a taxi in a foreign city does not come back. A backup made after the loss is not a backup.
  • Boarding is a hard deadline. Once the plane takes off, the phone is either with you or it is not — and a copy still running when the plane takes off is not a backup, because the photos that never copied are still only on the phone, and after the phone is lost there is no undo.
  • The gate is the wrong place to start. A boarding call can cut the copy off mid-way, there is no guaranteed power outlet at the gate, there is no Wi-Fi to fall back on, and one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. The gate is where a backup gets interrupted, not where it gets done.
  • The night before is the right place. Start the copy at home or in the hotel the night before, with power and time to let it finish, check a few photos on the drive, and pack the drive in the carry-on — then the flight is just a flight, and the backup is already done.
  • If the night-before copy did not finish, finish it with power, not at the gate. The hotel room or the lounge has an outlet and a chair; the gate has a boarding call. A copy finished at the hotel is a backup; a copy started at the gate is a hope.
  • The drive and the phone travel apart. The drive rides in the carry-on and the phone in your pocket — two copies in two places — because two copies in the same bag is one copy, and one lost bag takes both.

None of this is fixed by backing up after the trip — after the phone is lost, the phone either turns up or it does not, and the backup is already too late. The fix is a copy that finishes the night before, oldest-first, verified on the drive, and packed in a different bag from the phone.

What you need

  • An Android phone running Android 7.0 or later — SnapDisk needs minSdk 24 in the repo. On an iPhone, use Method 2: the Files-app copy has no version floor, and SnapDisk is Android only.
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. For a whole library, a portable SSD holds more — but a portable SSD may need a powered hub, because the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • A charged phone, or a powered hub — one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. Charge first, then plug the drive in — or use a powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do it the night before, not at the gate, and do not delete from the phone until the check is done.
  • No Wi-Fi needed — the drive copy runs entirely offline, and SnapDisk has no internet permission at all. Wi-Fi matters only if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the phone will keep filling it — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job, one night. The phone's photos need only the drive; the charger needs to be done before the drive goes in.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive, and it needs no Wi-Fi at all. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start. Two honest limits first: the copy has to finish and be checked the night before — a copy still running when the plane takes off is not a backup — and the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-a-flight run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified.

  1. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos on the phone are the ones a lost phone puts most at risk.
  2. Charge the phone first. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — so charge to full, then unplug the charger and plug the drive in. A powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  3. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  4. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  5. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  6. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  7. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  8. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it, the night before. The backup has to complete before you board, and a flight cannot wait for a backup. If the copy is interrupted, resume it — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  9. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  10. Unplug the drive and pack it in the carry-on — not in the same bag as the phone, and never in checked luggage. A checked bag can be lost, and two copies in the same bag is one copy. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A phone that has never been backed up often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size, so the next trip is covered the moment you plug the drive in. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every phone-to-USB guide above describes — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when you want the backup done now, the night before:

  1. Charge the phone first — one port holds the drive or the charger, not both — then plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  2. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  3. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  4. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder a lost phone takes with it.
  5. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big and the night is short.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  7. Unplug the drive and pack it in the carry-on — a different bag from the phone, never checked luggage — and only then think about deleting anything from the phone, after the check.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to travel, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time, with the night you have left.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step most people skip before a flight — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. And the check has a catch of its own: with no Wi-Fi at the gate, the upload cannot run at all, so the cloud check waits for Wi-Fi — the drive copy does not:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone, and on Wi-Fi, not at the gate. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and pack the drive now.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones a lost phone takes with it.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the Wi-Fi, the account, or the subscription the gate does not have.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, when Wi-Fi returns — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on Wi-Fi, an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round — and why, before a flight, the drive copy is the backup that is finished when you need it, not the upload that is still running.

At the airport: security, carry-on vs checked bag, and the gate

The copy is done the night before — the airport is where the backup has to survive, not where it gets made:

  • Security X-ray does not harm a USB drive. Airport X-ray scanners do not damage flash memory — a USB flash drive or a portable SSD goes through the scanner the same as any other electronics. Keep the drive accessible in the tray if security asks to see it, rather than buried at the bottom of the bag. The risk at the airport is not the scanner — it is the bag.
  • Drive in carry-on, never in checked luggage. A checked bag can be lost, delayed, or opened — and a backup drive in a lost checked bag is a backup that flew somewhere else. The drive rides in your carry-on, where you can see it.
  • Phone in pocket, drive in carry-on — two copies, two places. If one bag goes missing, the other copy survives. Two copies in the same bag is one copy — one lost bag takes both, and a phone and a drive in the same backpack are the same single point of failure.
  • The checked-luggage SSD in the field guides is a third copy. PetaPixel and desnoulez.fr put an SSD in checked luggage as the third copy — card in the camera, card on the person, SSD in the bag. This drive is your second copy, not your third, so it stays with you in the carry-on. If you want a third copy, the checked bag is where it goes — after the carry-on copy is already safe.
  • Do not start a big copy at the gate. A boarding call cuts it off, there is no guaranteed power outlet, and there is no Wi-Fi to fall back on. A copy still running when the plane takes off is not a backup — finished files stay on the drive and SnapDisk resumes where it stopped after you land, but anything that never copied is still only on the phone.
  • Do not count on finishing in the air. A local USB copy does not need the network, so airplane mode does not stop it — but the backup that counts is the one finished and checked before you board. Board with the copy done, not with the copy running.
  • Keep the drive with you, not in the seat pocket. A drive left in a seat pocket is a drive left on the plane — the same loss as a phone left in a taxi, with the same no-undo. Carry-on, pocket, or jacket — never the seat pocket, never the overhead bin's far corner.

The airport does not make the backup — it tests it. A drive in the carry-on, a phone in the pocket, and a copy that finished the night before is the whole plan, and the gate is where you stop worrying about it, not where you start it.

What this does not do

  • Does not protect the phone from being lost or stolen. The backup copies photos — it does not keep the phone in your pocket. After the copy, the phone still needs looking after, and the drive still needs to travel in a different bag.
  • Does not undo a loss. Once the phone is lost with photos still only on it, the photos on the phone are out of reach. The backup has to finish before you board, not after the phone is gone.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Back those up in Settings, Google, Backup, too — and export 2FA tokens and transit passes separately, because a phone that is lost takes those with everything else.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com when Wi-Fi returns. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the flight, not after the phone is lost.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-a-flight run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not charge the phone while the drive is in the port. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, then plug the drive in.
  • Does not power a portable SSD from the phone alone. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. A spinning hard drive may need its own power too.
  • Does not make a checked bag safe. A drive in checked luggage is a backup in a bag that can be lost — the drive rides in the carry-on, and the phone in your pocket.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only. On an iPhone the before-a-flight route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive — and the same rules apply: copy the night before, verify on the drive, and keep the drive in the carry-on.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Starting the copy at the gate. A boarding call cuts it off, there is no guaranteed power outlet, and there is no Wi-Fi to fall back on. The copy runs the night before, with time to finish and be checked.
  • Boarding before the copy finishes. A copy still running when the plane takes off is not a backup — and after the phone is lost or stolen, there is no undo. Copy, check, then board.
  • Packing the drive and the phone in the same bag. Two copies in the same bag is one copy — one lost bag takes both. Drive in the carry-on, phone in your pocket.
  • Putting the drive in checked luggage. A checked bag can be lost or delayed — and a backup drive in a lost checked bag is a backup that flew somewhere else. Carry-on, always.
  • Leaving the drive in a seat pocket. A drive left in a seat pocket is a drive left on the plane — the same loss as a phone left in a taxi. Carry-on, pocket, or jacket — never the seat pocket.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want — when Wi-Fi returns.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder a lost phone takes with it.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to travel, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then decide.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then decide — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it, not after the copy fails.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole library in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole library is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.
  • Assuming the bridge guides cover the phone's library. The desnoulez.fr, PetaPixel, and brightideaphotography guides back up a camera card through the phone — but none of them copies the phone's own photos, none of them runs the night before a flight, and none of them separates the drive from the phone for the trip. The card is the bridge guides' job; the phone's library is this article's.

Rule of thumb: a flight is a deadline, not a reminder — copy the whole library oldest-first the night before, check a few photos on the drive, pack the drive in your carry-on and the phone in your pocket, and board knowing the backup is already done.
