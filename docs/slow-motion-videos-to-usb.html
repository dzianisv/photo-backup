What a slow-motion video actually is

A slow-motion video is a normal video file with a note inside it. Your phone records it as an ordinary .mp4 — on some phones, a .mov — saved in DCIM/Camera like every other video the camera shoots, and the slow part is metadata: a record of which segments of the track play back slowly. Android's own media library names the thing directly — SlowMotionData, which "holds information about the segments of slow motion playback within a track" — and Samsung stores the same information in an smta metadata box inside the file. The file is not a different format, and there is no second file holding the slow part. The slowness is playback: the camera shoots at a high frame rate and the player stretches it out.

The numbers make the shape concrete. Samsung's own support page says Slow Motion allows you to record a video then play it back up to 8 times slower than normal, and Super Slow-mo plays videos back 32 times slower than normal videos — and its community explains how: Super Slow-mo shoots at 960 frames a second, and when it is played back at 30 frames a second, that is 32 times slower. One second of recording at 960 frames a second becomes about 32 seconds of playback. The slow part is also an edit choice, not a fixed property of the file: Samsung's support page walks you through choosing the portion of the video you want in slow motion with sliders at the bottom of the screen, then choosing Export or Save — the file holds the whole recording, and the slow segment is the part you picked. That is the whole shape: one ordinary video file, shot at a high frame rate, with a metadata note saying which part plays slowly.

Two things follow from that shape, and they decide the backup. First, the file is big for its length: a second of Super Slow-mo holds 960 frames, where a second of ordinary video holds 30 — so a slow-motion library adds up faster than a library of ordinary clips, and the 5 GB free cap below bites sooner. Second, the risk is not that the file gets left behind — it is a .mp4 in DCIM/Camera, and every route in this article copies .mp4 files — but that the file gets re-saved. A copy that streams the bytes keeps the metadata note; a share, an edit, or a re-encode that writes a fresh file may not. That is the opposite of the burst article's risk, where many files hide behind one thumbnail — here one file hides a note inside it, the same shape as the motion photo in the WL-193 article, where one .jpg hides a video inside it. The check is different too: for a burst you count files, for a slow-motion clip you watch it play.

One more form to know about, because it confuses people: Instant Slow-mo. On newer Samsung phones, the Gallery app can slow down any video while you touch and hold the screen — it generates the slow motion on playback, with AI frame interpolation, and the original file is untouched. If you save that slow-mo clip from the Gallery, it becomes a new .mp4 file, and that new file copies like any other video. The point is the same either way: what you back up is files, and the slow part lives in the file or in the player, never in a separate slow-motion format.

What you need

  • An Android phone with slow-motion videos on it. On a Samsung Galaxy, open the Camera app, tap More, then Slow Motion — or Super Slow-mo — and record. Then check the count: open a clip in the Gallery and watch it play slowly, so you know which files are the slow-motion ones before you back them up. If a clip plays at full speed in the Gallery, it was not recorded in slow motion, and no backup can make it slow.
  • A USB drive with enough free space. Count first: slow-motion files run large — a second of Super Slow-mo holds 960 frames — so add up the DCIM/Camera folder in the Files app before you buy the drive, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • A drive formatted exFAT. The format reference for this article is DriveForge's exFAT-vs-FAT32-vs-NTFS guide: exFAT has no 4 GB per-file limit and is read and written by current Android phones, Windows, and Mac. FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more — a long slow-motion clip, shot at a high frame rate, can reach that — and exFAT is the practical format for the whole folder. NTFS is a Windows format that most Android phones cannot open on their own — do not use it for a phone backup drive.
  • An OTG adapter if your drive is USB-A. A dual USB-C drive plugs straight in; a standard USB-A stick needs a data-capable OTG adapter. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for the drive.
  • SnapDisk v0.1.2. Not on Google Play yet — install the APK from the GitHub release page. It needs Android 7.0 or later (minSdk 24 in the repo). No account, no cloud, no ads, and no internet permission — your photos physically cannot leave the phone.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

This is the route this article is for, and for .mp4 slow-motion clips both routes are certain — which is the honest centre of this article, and different from the burst, HEIC and RAW articles, where only the folder route was certain. The default run — leave From unpicked — reads the phone's MediaStore Video table with no folder filter and no MIME filter, verified in the repo as PhotoQuery.queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, called from BackupService when no From folder is picked — and a slow-motion clip is a .mp4 file, which every Android MediaStore indexes, so it is in the result set by construction. The folder route — tap the From picker, browse to the phone's storage, and choose DCIM or DCIM/Camera — is the certain one for every format in the list: the From picker is the system folder picker (verified in the repo as OpenDocumentTree), the tree walk recurses into subfolders, and it copies every file whose extension is in the list. Both .mp4 and .mov are in that list, verified in the repo's PhotoQuery.kt, so a DCIM pick copies every slow-motion clip in the tree whatever its format. Then pick the To folder on the drive, in the same picker, and tap Backup. The copy runs in the background: oldest-first by the date the video was taken, with a progress notification showing percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, plus Pause and Stop.

The one format split to know about is .mov. The default run is certain for .mp4 — .mp4 is indexed on every phone — but .mov indexing is not promised the same way, so if your phone saves slow motion as .mov, pick the folder. The folder route is certain in every case; the default route is certain for .mp4. That is the whole rule, and it is sharper than the other format articles' rule because the common case here — .mp4 — is covered by both routes.

The slow part survives because the copy never touches the files' bytes. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — there is no transcoding step anywhere in its copy path — and it checks the size of every file after copying, renaming the .part file to the final file only after the size verification passes. A slow-motion clip that arrives on the drive is the same .mp4 that left the phone, metadata note included. And here is the check that matters, because it is the one most people get wrong for slow motion: watch, do not just look. Copy one clip back to the phone and watch it play in slow motion in the Gallery — or, if you would rather not copy it back, compare the file size and duration on the drive against the phone: a matching size and a matching duration mean the file arrived whole. A clip that plays at full speed on a computer does not prove the slow part is gone — most computer players do not read the slow-motion metadata, so they play the file at full speed while the note is still inside it. The phone's own Gallery is the check, because it reads the note. This article does not promise the slow part survives on every player on every computer — that is a property of the player, not of the file — and it says so instead of promising it.

Two honest limits on this route. First, there is no slow-motion-only mode on the default run: it copies the whole library, oldest-first, slow-motion clips mixed in with stills and ordinary videos in date order. If you want slow-motion clips only, that is what the DCIM/Camera pick is for — and even then, the pick copies every video in the folder, slow-motion and ordinary alike; there is no way to pick only the slow ones except by selecting them by hand in Method 2. Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you would rather not install anything, the Files app does the same copy by hand — and for slow motion it has one advantage: you see exactly which files you selected. Plug the drive in, open Files by Google (or your phone's file manager), browse to DCIM/Camera, long-press one slow-motion clip, tap the rest, tap Copy, navigate to the USB drive, and tap Paste. The file manager shows you the individual .mp4 files, not the gallery's slow-motion badge — so you can see the file sizes as you select, which is the check the gallery hides from you. Check the drive afterwards: compare the file sizes and durations against the phone, and copy one clip back to the phone and watch it play in slow motion to confirm the slow part arrived.

Three warnings specific to slow motion on this route. First, copy in the Files app — do not share from the Gallery. Sharing a slow-motion clip from the Gallery hands the file to another app, and what that app does with it — re-encode it, strip the metadata, save a fresh file — is out of your control. Copy in the Files app moves the file itself; Share hands the file to another app. Second, do not use Move. The guides that suggest Move are telling you to delete the originals as the copy goes — and a Move that is interrupted halfway leaves some clips on the drive, some on the phone, and no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete — that order is what keeps your slow-motion clips safe. Third, do not judge the copy by playing it on the computer. Most computer players do not read the slow-motion metadata, so a perfectly good copy plays at full speed on a computer — and that proves nothing about the file. The check is the phone's Gallery, which reads the note.

What you give up is everything SnapDisk automates. There is no oldest-first order — you copy in whatever order you selected. There is no progress notification with speed and time left, no Pause and Stop, and no resume: if the drive is unplugged mid-copy, you start that file again and work out yourself which clips already arrived. There is no per-file size verification, and no skipped-file list — a file that does not fit, or a copy that silently stops, fails quietly. For a few clips that is fine. For a library of them, the manual route is an afternoon of tapping and checking, and the one-tap route is the honest recommendation. The manual route is also the fallback for the clips MediaStore does not index: the default run reads the MediaStore Video table with no format filter, so a .mov slow-motion clip is included like any .mp4 — but a clip in a folder MediaStore skips, for example a folder containing a .nomedia file, never reaches the default run, and the way to copy it is to select it by hand in DCIM/Camera and copy it the same way.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

Before you trust the cloud with slow motion, check what it actually holds. Google Photos backs up the camera folder, and a slow-motion clip uploaded there keeps its file — but the default is not Original quality. Google's own help page says Storage saver uploads "may be compressed into a different image format, like a .jpg" — and a video re-encoded at reduced quality is a different file from the one the phone recorded, written by a compressor that has no reason to preserve a slow-motion metadata note. Whether the slow part survives a Storage saver re-encode is not something this article promises either way — no slow-motion run through the cloud has ever been tested here — so the rule is the same as for every other format in this series: copy to the drive first, at original quality, and check the copy, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. The setting is one tap in Photos settings — easy to have wrong for years without noticing, because the clip looks identical at full speed.

One more cloud detail, because it confuses people with slow motion specifically: the slow part is metadata, and metadata is the first thing a re-encode drops. A clip that comes back from the cloud at full speed may have lost the note, or may be playing on a player that never read it — and from the phone alone you cannot tell which. That is why the check in this article is a copy back to the phone and a watch in the Gallery, not a glance at a thumbnail. Copy to the USB drive first, watch one clip play in slow motion on the phone, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. And never delete the phone originals on the strength of a cloud badge alone. Check the drive copy, watch it play slowly, then delete — that order is what keeps your slow-motion clips safe.

The 5 GB free cap, and why slow motion feels it

SnapDisk's free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — verified in the repo as FREE_BACKUP_BYTES_LIMIT, 5 GiB — and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. For slow motion, 5 GB does not go as far as it does for ordinary video: a second of Super Slow-mo holds 960 frames where a second of ordinary video holds 30, so the library adds up faster than a library of ordinary clips, and a season of slow-motion clips is gigabytes where a season of stills is megabytes. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (product id pro_unlock, verified in the repo's billing code) that auto-starts the backup when a disk is connected and removes the size limit, so a whole season copies in one run.

The cap is per run, not per library, and that distinction is what matters here: the free version can finish any slow-motion library if you run it again, because files already copied are never copied twice. The count to do before you start is the library size: open the Files app, look at the DCIM/Camera folder size, divide by 5 GB, and that is how many free runs the library needs. Do the arithmetic before you plug the drive in, not halfway through the copy.

One more size note: a file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped with its reason and the job continues — verified in the repo's BackupService. A short slow-motion clip never reaches that, but a long one, shot at a high frame rate, can — and that file is skipped and listed, the clips around it copy normally, and the fix is the same exFAT format recommended above.

What this does not do

  • It does not convert your slow-motion clips — to anything. SnapDisk copies the .mp4 exactly as it is. Converting a clip to a smaller file, or to any other format, re-encodes it — and a re-encode is exactly the step that can drop the slow-motion metadata. That is your editor's or your computer's job, on a copy, never on the only original.
  • It does not extract the slow part to a separate file. The slow-motion segment stays inside the .mp4, as metadata — extraction is a separate tool's job, on a copy, never on the only original. The backup's job is to keep the original intact.
  • It does not slow down ordinary videos. Turning an ordinary clip into slow motion is the phone's Gallery feature — Samsung's Instant Slow-mo does it on playback — not SnapDisk's job. SnapDisk copies files; it does not edit them.
  • It does not copy slow-motion clips only on the default run. The default run copies the whole library, oldest-first, slow-motion clips mixed in with stills and ordinary videos in date order. Slow-motion clips only is the DCIM/Camera pick in Method 1 — and even then, every video in the folder copies, slow-motion and ordinary alike.
  • It does not promise the slow part plays in slow motion on every player. A player that does not read the slow-motion metadata plays the file at full speed — the file is intact, the player just does not apply the slow part. The check is the phone's own Gallery, which reads the note.
  • It does not read or index your slow-motion clips. There is no slow-motion detection and no search inside the clips — SnapDisk copies files, and finding one clip among a thousand .mp4 files is your file manager's job, on the drive, after the copy.
  • It does not back up anything but photos and videos. No app data, no messages, no system state — and no browser tabs, notes, or clipboard history. Those live in the app's own storage, not in the photo folders, and they need their own backup.
  • It does not delete anything. SnapDisk only copies — the only delete calls in its source touch .part files, never your photos — so freeing space is a two-step flow you do yourself: copy to the drive, watch one clip play in slow motion on the phone, then delete the originals from your phone. That flow is covered step by step in How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive.
  • It does not promise the copy keeps running with the screen off under battery saver. No wake lock, no battery-saver handling in the repo, no slow-motion run ever tested — stated plainly above, not promised. Keep the phone awake for the run.
  • It does not restore or view your photos. There is no in-app viewer and no restore feature — you open the copied .mp4 files in a viewer or on your computer yourself. The SnapDisk vs VaultDrive comparison covers what the rivals add here, and what they charge for it.

What to avoid

  • Assuming slow motion is a special format. A slow-motion clip is an ordinary .mp4 with a note inside it. The backup does not need to understand slow motion — it needs to copy the file without changing it.
  • Sharing the clip when you meant to copy it. Sharing from the Gallery hands the file to another app, which may re-encode it and drop the metadata. Copy in the Files app; Share hands the file to another app.
  • Judging the copy by playing it on a computer. Most computer players do not read the slow-motion metadata, so a good copy plays at full speed there. The check is the phone's Gallery — copy one clip back and watch it play slowly.
  • Letting Storage saver re-encode the clips. Google Photos Storage saver uploads "may be compressed into a different image format, like a .jpg" — Google's own words. Copy to the drive first, at original quality.
  • Assuming the default run covers .mov slow motion. The default run is certain for .mp4; for .mov, pick DCIM/Camera as the From folder — .mov is in the recognised extension list, so the folder route copies it.
  • Using Move instead of Copy in the Files app. Move deletes the originals as the copy goes — an interrupted Move leaves clips split between phone and drive with no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete.
  • Formatting the drive NTFS. Most Android phones cannot open NTFS on their own. Use exFAT — DriveForge's guide is the format reference — and keep FAT32 only for a drive an older device must read.
  • Buying a drive before counting the library. Slow-motion files run large — a second of Super Slow-mo holds 960 frames. Count the DCIM/Camera folder first, then buy the size, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy — and for a slow-motion clip, the check is watching it play in slow motion on the phone. Copy, watch it play slowly, then delete from the phone — never the other way round.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Unverified — no wake lock, no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  • Starting a multi-gigabyte copy on a nearly dead battery. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Searching for the wrong thing. The pages the search returns are generic transfer guides, our own general articles, and two OTG walkthrough videos — none of them is a slow-motion backup guide. Search for the slow-motion videos, not the videos, and the gap is obvious.

Rule of thumb: a slow-motion video is an ordinary .mp4 with a note inside it — the copy has to be byte-for-byte, and the check has to prove the slow part is still there. Pick DCIM/Camera as From, copy, copy one clip back and watch it play in slow motion, then delete. That order is what keeps your slow-motion clips safe.
