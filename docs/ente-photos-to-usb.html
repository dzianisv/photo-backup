Save Ente Photos photos to USB from Android

Your photos on Ente live in your encrypted account, not on your phone. Download each one in the app — it lands in Pictures — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 15 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see in Ente is not on your phone yet. Ente is an end-to-end encrypted photo library that lives in your account — on Ente's hosted service, or on a server you run yourself — not a folder on your phone: you can see the photo, open it, preview it, share its link, browse it offline, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive except the one tap that does. The file appears only when you download it. In the Ente app on Android, open the photo, tap the three-dot menu, and tap Download — that is the app's own download action, labelled Download, with a download icon — or long-press photos in the timeline to select them and tap Download in the action menu — and the file lands in the Gallery, in the phone's Pictures folder. That is the one honest sentence in this whole job, and it carries two surprises: the button only appears for photos that live in your Ente account and are not already on the phone, and the folder is not one Ente names — the app's code passes no folder at all, so Android puts the file in its default place for the file type, Pictures for a photo and Movies for a video. If the original is still on the phone, there is no Download to tap — and no need for one, because the file is already in the Gallery and the backup covers it directly. From wherever the file landed, the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the downloaded Ente photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: Ente downloads nothing by itself — no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and the app's automatic feature runs the other way, sending your phone photos up to Ente, not bringing account photos down to your phone; downloading is not the same as opening a photo, and a previewed photo leaves no file behind; a downloaded photo sorts by the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken, because the file is written when you download it; and Free Up Space removes the local copies of photos you already backed up, so a photo you freed up is account-only until you download it back. The good news, and it is real: an Ente download is the original file as uploaded — the app downloads the encrypted file and decrypts it on the phone, and Ente stores originals with no compression — so what you back up is the full file; and there is no transfer quota to hit on your own files, because Ente publishes no download quota — the wall is the tapping, not a meter, and the storage plan is the only meter in sight, with 10 GB free. As of November 26, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated Ente-photos-to-USB how-to with no computer: the results are our own generic transfer article, our own storage-full and factory-reset articles, and a generic Dr.Fone guide that never mentions Ente — with no Ente-specific phone-to-USB guide at all.

The confusion first: Ente is an encrypted account — and Download is not a copy in Downloads

Ente sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. Ente is an end-to-end encrypted photo backup service: an account you sign in to, plus an Android app that talks to it — the app on Google Play is io.ente.photos, by Ente, version 1.3.70, and the same app is on F-Droid and as an APK from the project's own releases — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like a photo library, it behaves like a photo library when you open a photo in it, and it is not your library. It is a library in an account, and the photos in it are files that live there, encrypted, not on your phone. A photo library on your phone — the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. Ente shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Ente account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Ente either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no cloud account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of Ente and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a download. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion Ente's own design invites. Ente is where people keep their photos because it is theirs — free and open-source software, with the whole app and server in the public ente-io/ente repository, encrypted on the device before upload so that not even Ente can see them, with no storage plan to buy for the first 10 GB and no account to rent — and the account feels like an archive: your files, your albums, your shared links, all in the order you put them in, all yours, all reachable from any device. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy in an account, under that account's rules. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits in an account you stop paying attention to is a photo at risk twice over. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the account is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

The third confusion hides inside Ente itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. Downloading a photo copies it from your Ente account to a folder on your phone that other apps can open — Pictures, in the Gallery — and that is the tap this article is about. Making a photo available offline is not the same tap: the app keeps photos you already browsed available for offline viewing, and an offline photo is a copy inside the app, not a file in a folder the Gallery or a backup can see. Opening a photo to look at it is thinner still: it shows you the photo from the account without downloading it first, and a viewed photo leaves no file behind at all. Sharing a photo's link is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the file remaining available in the account. And Free Up Space is a fourth thing, different again: it removes the local copies of photos you already backed up, to reclaim space — the right tap when the phone is full, and the wrong tap to confuse with a download, because it takes files off the phone instead of putting them on it. The only tap that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive is the Download in the photo menu or the selection menu — and if you have been opening photos and wondering why none of them are in the Gallery, this is why: you have been viewing photos, and viewed photos are not downloads.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still in the Ente account. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The download is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the download is a step you have to take yourself, because Ente offers no download-them-all setting and no auto-download. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the download-one-and-check rule, because for Ente the date problem is a download problem: the file is written when you download it, and the day you downloaded it is the day it sorts by. And the one thing this article will not do, which the other guides do without saying so, is invent a folder: Ente names no folder in its own code — the download saves with no folder given, so Android puts the file in Pictures — and the folder section below is a check, not a claim.

The wall: Ente downloads nothing by itself — the download is yours to do

Ente does not put your account photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every description of the download describes a manual step — open the photo, open the menu, tap Download — because the manual step is the only step there is. Ente's own help pages walk through backup and sync, and say nothing about downloading to the phone at all — the download is verified here from the app's own code for version 1.3.70, the latest release, not from the help pages, and this article says so instead of pretending the help pages cover it. Ente's settings hold nothing about downloading to the phone automatically: the app has an Auto Backup toggle, which sends your phone photos up to Ente, and no auto-download toggle, and no sync-to-device option. Viewed photos sit in the app's offline store, which keeps photos you already browsed available when you have no connection, but an offline copy is not a Gallery folder: it is Ente's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's data is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you download it, and you download it one photo at a time — or one selection at a time, with the multi-select described below.

The download itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because the app's own code states it in the same words this article uses. Open the Ente app. Find the photo — in your library, in an album, in a shared album — and open it. Open the three-dot menu and tap Download. That is the app's own download action: a download icon, the label Download, offered for photos that live in your Ente account. The label is the word Download in the app's own English strings, and the code shows the action only when the photo is uploaded — the menu entry appears when the file is uploaded, and the tap downloads it. If the photo is already on the phone — backed up, original still on the device — there is no Download to tap, because there is nothing to download: the file is already in the Gallery, and the backup covers it directly. That is not a missing button. It is the app telling you the job is already half done. For a photo that is account-only, the tap downloads the encrypted file, decrypts it on the phone, and saves it to the Gallery at Pictures. The same action works from the timeline: long-press a photo to select it, tap the others to build a selection, and use Download in the action menu to save them together. In some app versions the same step may be reached from the photo's own menu or from the selection menu — if one of them shows no Download, try the other, because the two menus carry the same action.

There is one more thing the download does not let you choose, and it is worth knowing before you download a hundred photos, because it decides where the files land. Ente names no destination in its own code: the download saves the photo to the device gallery with no folder given — the save call passes a file and a title and nothing else — and that is the folder to check first, in the Gallery, if the photo is not where you expected. Android's media store puts a file saved with no folder in its default place for the file type: Pictures for a photo, Movies for a video. The folder is the one named thing in this whole job, and it is named by Android, not by Ente: the app's code names no folder, so the search is short — check the Gallery first, then Pictures in the Files app — and the folder-check section below walks the whole search in order. Do not waste an evening looking for the download in the Downloads folder. A Download in Ente does not go to Downloads. The web download does that, and the web is a different route, covered below. And do not look for an Ente folder, either: there is none. Pictures is the folder, and the Gallery is where it shows.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the download sometimes silently does nothing. The Ente app needs permission to save photos to the gallery, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the download tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first download, open the Gallery and look for the photo. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every download after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission in Settings, Apps, Ente Photos, Permissions, Photos and videos, and download the photo again. Do not download fifty photos and check at the end. Download one, check the folder, then download the rest.

What you need. Five things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the Ente app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is your Ente account, because the photos live in that account. The account itself, in good standing and reachable from the phone — on any connection, Wi-Fi or mobile data, because Ente is a hosted service, not a server on your home network; without a reachable account there is nothing to download, and a photo that does not load cannot be downloaded, which is a confusing way to discover the account has lapsed. The app itself is free to install, on Google Play and on F-Droid, and current — version 1.3.70, released November 13, 2026 — so update it before you start, because the menu wording varies between app versions. Enough free space on the phone to hold the downloads: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, download in batches and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the download steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a download guide.

One more check, on the Ente side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can download. If the photo is in your library or an album you can open, it is a photo you can try to download. If it is from a link that expired, or the file was deleted, or it sits in a shared album you were removed from, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be downloaded. And if the Download option is missing entirely, that is usually one of two things, not a bug: the photo is already on the device, or the photo is shared with you in a way that does not include downloading — the next sections cover both. Do not download first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then open the menu. A download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Download one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Ente app on your Android phone and sign in to your account. Find the photo — in your library, in an album, in a shared album, or through a link — and open it full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a download of a thumbnail is a download of a thumbnail. Open the three-dot menu and tap Download. If you want more than one, long-press a photo in the timeline to select it, then tap the others to build a selection, and use Download in the action menu to save them together — the same download action serves the selection as well as the single photo. Then find the file — and finding it is a short step, because Android names the folder. Open the Gallery first: if the photo is there, the download worked, whatever folder it sits in. If it is not in the Gallery, open Files by Google and look in Pictures — Internal storage, Pictures — and the folder-check section below walks the search in order, with the reason each place is worth checking.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the folder the file landed in is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo the Gallery shows is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of Pictures, rename it, or put it in DCIM/Camera. Leave it where Ente put it. The default run, described in the section after next, reads the media index with no folder filter, and Pictures is indexed like any other folder — it sits outside DCIM, under the default From folder's neighbour, and the index does not care. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo in Ente if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is a file on the phone, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the downloaded Ente photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the downloaded batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo — or one selection — at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one album, the photos from one trip. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand tap-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole album, use the selection route in Method 2, which gets the album in one step instead of one tap at a time. For a whole library, use Method 3, which works through the albums one by one instead of one selection at a time. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what was shared — Ente offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Download option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the photo is already on the device: search the Gallery and the Files app for the photo before anything else — it may already be a file, and the job may already be half done, because the Download button only appears for photos that are in your Ente account and not on the phone. Second, check that you are logged in to the right account: the photos live in the account you signed in with, and a second Ente account holds a different library. Third, update the Ente app: the menu wording varies between app versions — Download in current versions — and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Fourth, try the selection route instead of the single-photo route: long-press the photo in the timeline, select the photos, and download the selection — the same action serves both menus. Fifth, open the photo on the web instead of the app: sign in at photos.ente.com in Chrome, open the photo, and download it from there, and the file arrives in the browser's Downloads folder. Sixth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, Ente Photos, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and download again. One of those six fixes the download in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, before you download the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the folder the download landed in — Pictures, or Downloads if you used the web — select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole album — select them in the app, or download on the web

Ente is blunt about the one bulk step the app does offer, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the step every other cloud in this series offers in some form and Ente offers in its own: the app downloads a selection, and a selection download is one step on the selection itself. Long-press a photo in the timeline to select it, tap the others to build the selection — a whole album, a whole day, a whole trip — open the action menu, and tap Download. Each photo downloads as its own file to Pictures, the same folder as a single download, and the wait is the download, not the tap — use the batch-size advice below, and check the folder before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

For a batch on the web, the route is the browser, and it is worth following exactly, because the web route has one trap — the Downloads folder — that decides whether it works on a phone at all. Sign in at photos.ente.com in Chrome, open the album or the timeline view, select the photos you want, and use Download — or open a single photo and use Download in the viewer. One photo arrives as its own file, under its own file name; several photos arrive as files to the browser's Downloads folder. That is the trap, and it is a real one: the web download lands in Downloads, not in Pictures and not in DCIM, and a reader who looks for the batch in the Gallery's Pictures folder will not find it until the media index picks the files up — open one downloaded photo once, which prompts the media scanner, or use the From picker route, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. And a shared album or link works from the web too: the web download applies to shared files as well as your own, so a photo someone shared with you downloads the same way — but check each item, because sharing is per album and per link, and a file shared with you for viewing is not automatically a file you can download: check what the share allows before assuming the selection is yours to take.

The web route is also the route for the photos the app will not download, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the route for the edge cases. If a photo is in an album someone shared with you, the web view shows the same album, and the same Download works from there — but check each item, because sharing is per album and per link, and a file shared with you for viewing is not automatically a file you can download: check what the share allows before assuming the selection is yours to take. If a photo is in a shared link someone sent you, the same rule holds: the link shows you the file, and the download is a separate permission the owner controls. And if the Download option is missing for a single photo in the app but present on the web, the web is the fix — the app and the web do not always agree on what is already on the device, and the web download writes a fresh file either way.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. The download is the photos you selected, not the album as an object: the album name, the album order, and the album membership do not travel with the files, so keep the album context yourself — a dated folder on the drive, named for the Ente album, is the simplest way, and the checking section below says so again, because it is the step people skip. Each time more photos are added to the album, you need to download again, or the new photos will not be in the batch. And there is no quota wall to size the batch against: Ente publishes no download quota on your own files — the files live in your account, and the only meter in sight is the storage plan, with 10 GB free — so the limit is the phone's free space and your patience, not a meter. For ten photos, download them in the app. For a whole album, select them in the app, or use the web download. Use each route for the job it fits, and check Pictures — or the Downloads folder for a web batch — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole library — what Ente does and does not export

For a whole library, the honest answer is that Ente has no one-tap whole-library export in the app, and this article says so instead of inventing one. There is no settings page that zips your entire library, no download-all button that compiles everything you ever uploaded, and no archive that arrives by email with all your albums in it. The export unit in the app is the photo, the selection, or the album: one photo at a time, one selection at a time, or one album at a time. If you have ten albums, that is ten downloads. If you have a hundred, that is a hundred — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

The route that does exist for a whole library is the album-by-album download, and it is worth doing in order, because the order is what keeps it from becoming a mess. Start with the albums that matter most — the photos with no other copy anywhere — and work down the list. For each album, select the photos in the app and download the selection, or download it on the web, and back up the files it contains before downloading the next album. Keep each album's files in their own dated folder on the drive, so the drive ends up with the albums in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower than a single export would be, and it is the route that exists. The app's selection download is the faster route for a single album, and the web download is the route for the albums the app will not take. Use each one for the job it fits.

For the whole library at once, the route that exists is not on the phone at all, and the boundary is worth stating because it is specific to Ente in a way it is not for the other clouds in this series. Ente's own export lives on the computer: the desktop app's Settings, Export data, with Continuous export, which keeps a folder on the computer updated with your library, album by album — or the Ente CLI, whose photos export command pulls the whole library, or chosen albums, to a folder you name, incrementally, so running it again picks up only what changed. That is the honest boundary of this article — it is the computer route, and this article is the no-computer phone route, so it names the boundary instead of pretending the phone can do the same job. If you have a computer handy, the desktop export or the CLI is the faster route for a whole library by a long way, and the phone route is the one for the photos you actually want right now. Do not leave the job until the account lapses: the time to take the photos out is while the account is in good standing, not after you stop paying attention to it.

Two facts about the library downloads are worth stating because they decide who they work for. First, they are for photos you can download: your own photos, and other people's photos where the sharing allows it. They do not provide an export for someone else's library, and they do not include photos you viewed but did not download — a library of views is not a library you can export. Second, the download is not locked to you: a file you download is a file, and a file can be copied, shared, and forwarded by anyone who holds it, so keep the drive somewhere safe and do not forward a batch in a group chat and assume only the people you meant will open it. If the photos need protection, the protection is the drive in your drawer, not the account on the service.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what was shared. Ente offers no export for a library you do not control, no download-a-whole-library button, and no save-them-all in the app beyond a single photo, a selection, or an album. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take an account or an album link and fetch the files — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them, because the app's own Download does the job they claim to do, and a tool that asks for your Ente password to download a shared album is asking for something it does not need. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: download the photos you actually want one by one, or the whole album where the sharing allows it, or ask the person for the files. A thousand one-by-one downloads is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a downloaded Ente photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a downloaded photo copies with the rest of the library, whatever folder Ente put it in — Pictures, Movies, Downloads, or any other shared folder. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:23-36, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:60 takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the folder the Ente download landed in.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: download the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in Pictures and then in Downloads: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into Ente. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Ente for anything. A photo still in the Ente account is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still in the Ente account is invisible to the file manager. The download is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Ente; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Ente photos without downloading them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in Pictures came from Ente, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/BackupEngine.kt:126. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Ente route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the download that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the Ente photos, not the whole library — and because for Ente it is the one place the folder matters. Point the From picker at the folder the downloads landed in — /sdcard/Pictures, or Downloads if you used the web — and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:90-95, walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName, at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:125-128, keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/MainActivity.kt:62 and :71, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked Pictures for Ente downloads yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on these exact folders — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works. And the one thing to get right: point the picker at Pictures, not at DCIM. Ente downloads land in Pictures, outside DCIM, so the default From folder of /sdcard/DCIM does not contain them as a folder — the default run still covers them through the index, but the picker route needs the folder they actually landed in.

The date problem: a downloaded photo shows the download date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo downloaded from Ente shows the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:47-48, and the sort order is a CASE expression, at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:56-58: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a downloaded file is the download time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a downloaded Ente photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you downloaded it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For Ente, the taken date has a better chance than for the other photo services, and the reason is the quality section below: an Ente download is the original file as uploaded, byte for byte, so the taken date recorded inside the file survives the download — the file is the same file, and the date inside it is the same date. But the date inside the file and the date the media index records are two different things, and the index is what the default run sorts by: the scanner may read the taken date out of the file and record it, or it may record the download date, and no source this run tested which one a given phone does after an in-app download. This article states download-date order as the expected case, not as a certainty, because the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app — the file is written by the download, at download time, and its modified date is the download date — while noting the one way Ente differs: the original file survives, so the taken date is still inside it. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: download one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you download a hundred of them.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Ente names the downloaded file itself, after the original file name, and two downloads of the same photo — or two photos Ente named the same way — can arrive in Pictures under the same name, so the second download either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo in Ente if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to download a batch and copy it to the drive before downloading the next, or to rename the files in Pictures first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to download.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Download one album's photos, back up Pictures, clear it, download the next album's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the albums in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the downloaded copy is the original — and there is no quota wall

A photo downloaded from Ente is the original file as uploaded. That is the one place where Ente is better than every photo service in this series, and it is worth stating plainly, because it changes the whole quality question. Ente is a library in your account, not a photo service that re-encodes what you give it: it stores the file you uploaded, encrypted, and a download returns that same file — the app's download fetches the encrypted file and decrypts it on the phone, and the project's own Play listing says you own your originals, with no compression and no quality loss. There is no served copy, no resized version, no size menu to get wrong in the app — the app download offers no size choice because there is no size to choose. The downloaded copy is the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app download is the right tool, and this article says so instead of sending you to the web for a quality the app already gives you.

The one place a size choice could exist is the web, and the answer is the same there: the web download is the file as Ente holds it — the original — so the quality question has the same answer on both routes, and there is no resized copy to get by mistake. The same warning holds for a live photo in the app: the download saves the still and its motion video as two files, so check that both arrived before you call the batch done. In the app there is no size choice to get wrong: the download is the original, every time.

The limit, for Ente, is not the quality and not a quota — it is the tapping. Ente publishes no transfer meter on downloading your own files: there is no six-hour window, no published number, no message that the transfer quota is exceeded, because there is no quota to exceed — the files live in your account, not on a metered service. If your account is someone else's — a family plan you were invited to, a shared album on another account — their rules apply instead, and a plan that runs out of storage can still slow you down, so check with them if the downloads stall. But for your own account, the wall is the tapping, not a meter. Do not treat the absence of a quota as a reason to hand your account to a downloader that claims to be faster: the app's own Download does the job, and a tool that asks for your Ente password to do it is asking for something it does not need.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because Ente is a library that holds videos too — and videos are the one place the batch bites hardest. The app download covers videos the same way it covers photos: the same menu and Download that save a photo save a video — open the video in the Ente app, use the menu and Download, and the file lands on the phone, in Movies. The backup covers it from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:131-134, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, at :135-137, so a downloaded photo and a downloaded video both copy with the default run. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: download the video, open the folder, and look at what arrived. If it plays, it downloaded as a video; if it arrived as a single still frame, the download caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, download it again and check what arrived before you download the next twenty. And remember the free space while you are at it: a video-heavy album fills a phone long before a photo album does, and a download of a video you already have on the phone is a download you did not need.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: download one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the downloaded copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before downloading — wait for the photo to load crisp before you open the menu, because a download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the downloaded file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload. And check the metadata before you delete the account copy: a download should be checked for metadata, edits, and motion-photo packaging before the account copy is deleted — and a motion photo may arrive as a still, and the motion is gone for good if the account copy goes first.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the download is worth doing at all. A photo downloaded from Ente is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the tapping: the downloaded copy is the original, and no backup step will put the missing photos back if you never downloaded them. Download the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. Back up each batch before downloading the next, so a stopped batch is a finished batch, not a lost one.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest download, a middle one, and the most recent download. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against Pictures on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the folder holds the photos plus whatever else landed there — but the photo count should match the number you downloaded, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second download overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the folder before the backup ever saw it. Download one batch at a time and copy each batch before downloading the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Ente batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a downloaded photo shows the download date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the download, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, download that photo again, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in the download, not at upload — and because an Ente download is the original file, a date that does not survive is worth one more check in the file itself before you give up on it.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Ente login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The download needs no third party at all: the app's menu does the single photo, the selection download does the album, the web download does the batch, and the desktop export or the CLI holds the library for the computer route. A tool that asks for your Ente password to download a shared album is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours — the same account that holds your files, your photos, and your encrypted library, not just the photos you meant to download. The in-app download needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not open photos and expect them on the drive. Opening a photo shows it from the account without downloading it first, so a viewed photo leaves no file behind at all. Download the photo with the menu, check the Gallery, and then back it up. And do not confuse Download with offline viewing: Download writes the file to Pictures and puts it in the Gallery; offline viewing keeps a copy inside the app that no other app can see. They are two different things for two different purposes, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing new where they looked — because the photos were viewed, not downloaded, and viewing writes nothing anywhere. Know which one you did, and look in the folder that tap writes to.

Do not use Free Up Space before you copy the downloads to the drive. Free Up Space removes the local copies of photos you already backed up — that is its job, and it does it well — but a photo it removed is account-only until you download it back, and a backup that has not run yet has nothing left to copy. Download, copy, check, and only then free up the space. The same goes for clearing the app's data or uninstalling it: the downloaded files go with it. The drive copy is the one that survives, and it only survives if it was made first.

Do not download a whole library one photo at a time. A thousand tap-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Download the handful you actually want in the app, use the selection download for an album, and work through your own albums one by one. The app download is fast and the web download is fiddly, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the download to reach someone else's library: it is for photos you own or that were shared with download rights, and it will not contain your views either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the account as the only copy. Ente is a library in an account, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can lapse, and an account you stop paying attention to is an account you have stopped paying attention to. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the account is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
