What you need

A Nokia phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk); a USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into a modern Nokia phone, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter; older Nokia phones with a Micro-USB port need a Micro-USB OTG adapter instead; and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Three Nokia specifics: first, there is no OTG switch to hunt for. Nokia phones run near-stock Android — Android One on most models — so the drive mounts on plug-in: look for a "USB storage added" or "USB drive connected" notification. If the phone ignores the drive, check Settings > Storage: no documented Nokia OTG switch location was found, and whether a switch exists at all, or switches itself off, is unverified — so don't assume a missing switch means a broken phone. Not every Nokia phone has OTG at all — Seagate's compatibility list confirms the Nokia X71, 5.1 Plus, and 6.1 do, but for any other model check its specs — so if there is no OTG setting anywhere, the phone may not support it. Second, Nokia's stock file manager is Files by Google, and the stock gallery is Google Photos — Nokia phones ship with Google's apps handling the basics, and Nokia camera photos live in DCIM/Camera — and whether your model has a microSD slot varies by model, so check your own model's specs rather than assuming one either way; a USB drive is the plug-in storage that works on every model. Third, Nokia phones ship with Google Play, so SnapDisk installs from the v0.1.2 APK on the GitHub release page today and from Play once the listing is live — there is no sideload-only situation here. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. Most Nokia phones mount it straight away — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage; no documented Nokia OTG switch location was found, so there may be nothing to turn on.
  2. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Nokia camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Method 2: Files by Google (the stock route, no app needed)

Nokia phones ship with Files by Google as the stock file manager, and it can copy your photos to the USB drive: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage. 2. Open Files by Google and confirm the USB drive shows up under Storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until Files says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

It is a real backup, and it is the closest Nokia's own guides come to this article's job — but the backup lives in Google's cloud and restores to a phone, not a folder of photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.

What to avoid

  • Hunting for an OTG switch that may not exist. No documented Nokia OTG switch location was found, and near-stock Android mounts the drive on plug-in with a "USB storage added" notification. If your Nokia phone ignores a drive, check Settings > Storage — but a missing switch is not proof the phone is broken; not every Nokia phone has OTG at all. Seagate's list confirms the X71, 5.1 Plus, and 6.1 do; for any other model, check its specs.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google backup and Google One back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not Google backup.
  • Mistaking a PC backup for a photo copy. Nokia PC Suite and MobileTrans back your phone up to a PC — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy — that is Method 1 or 2, not a PC tool.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC, or Nokia PC Suite, or MobileTrans, works — but all three need the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 17, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On Nokia the cloud route is Google Photos or Google One — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
