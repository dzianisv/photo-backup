SnapDisk vs Photos to USB: back up Android photos to USB

Two Android apps that copy your photos to a USB drive — no cloud, no account, no upload. Here is how they actually differ, from the facts we could verify as of November 2026.

November 21, 2026 · 4 min read

TL;DR: SnapDisk and Photos to USB both back up Android photos to a USB drive offline, with no account and no cloud — and both only copy, never delete. The verified differences: Photos to USB is on Google Play today (first release, updated Oct 1, 2026) and SnapDisk is not yet (v0.1.2 — install the APK from GitHub); Photos to USB is free with ads and a one-time purchase removes them, but the listing shows no price for that purchase, so this article makes no price comparison — SnapDisk Pro is $9.99 one-time; Photos to USB's free tier copies the whole gallery in one tap with no size or file-count cap stated, while SnapDisk's free tier is 5 GB per run and repeatable — files already copied are never copied twice; Photos to USB keeps the photo's location, sorts copies into one folder per phone by month, and copies only new photos next time, while SnapDisk copies oldest-first with Pause and Stop in the progress notification, keeps your source folder structure, and checks the size of every file after copying; Photos to USB needs the app open while it copies — the screen stays on by itself — while SnapDisk copies in the background as a foreground service, though screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified. This is the third head-to-head comparison in the series, after SnapDisk vs VaultDrive and SnapDisk vs FotoSafe.

The two apps at a glance

SnapDisk is a free Android app (v0.1.2, not on Google Play yet) that backs up photos and videos to a USB disk. Plug in the disk, tap Backup in the notification, and photos copy oldest-first in the background, size-verified, with a progress notification showing percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, plus Pause and Stop. Free: manual backup, 5 GB per run. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited size. No cloud, no account, no ads, and no internet permission — your photos physically cannot leave the phone. It needs Android 7.0 or later.

Photos to USB: OTG Backup (com.arzdrz.photos_to_usb, developer Akshay C P) is on Google Play — first release, updated Oct 1, 2026. Plug a pen drive into your phone (a USB-C drive goes straight in, an older one needs an OTG adapter), it spots the drive the moment it is in, shows how much space it has, and copies your gallery onto it in one tap: photos and videos from your camera, WhatsApp, and every other folder. Next time, only new photos are copied. Copies land in one folder per phone, sorted by month, so the drive stays easy to browse on a computer. It works offline with no cloud and no account. Free, with ads; a one-time purchase removes the ads for good — the listing shows no price for that purchase. It works with pen drives formatted as FAT32 or exFAT; a drive formatted as NTFS opens read-only on many phones, and the app tells you if that happens.

Price: free with ads vs free without — and the price nobody publishes

Both apps are free to start, but free means different things on each side. Photos to USB is free with ads, and a one-time purchase removes the ads for good. That is the whole of what its listing says about money — and it shows no price figure for that purchase anywhere in the listing text we could read. SnapDisk is free with no ads at all, and its Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase, verified in the repo's billing code (product id pro_unlock).

So this article makes no price comparison: any number for the Photos to USB ad removal would be a guess, and a guessed price in a comparison is worse than no price at all. What can be compared is what the money buys. Photos to USB's purchase buys ad removal — its listing says nothing else about it. SnapDisk's Pro buys features: auto-backup when a disk is connected, and unlimited size instead of the 5 GB free cap. If the price is what decides it for you, open Photos to USB's Play listing and read the in-app purchase price there — it is the only source that can show it.

Neither app asks for a subscription at any point. Photos to USB's purchase is one-time, and SnapDisk Pro is one-time.

The free tier: the whole gallery vs 5 GB a run

Photos to USB's free tier is the whole gallery in one tap — its listing says "One tap copies everything" and states no size cap and no file-count cap. That is the free tier: the entire library, ads included, with no stated limit on how much it copies.

SnapDisk's free tier is 5 GB per backup run, with no file-count cap, and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. A phone that has never been backed up often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps — and that is the case Pro is for — but the free version can finish the whole library, run by run, without paying.

Which free tier goes further depends on your camera roll and your patience: Photos to USB copies the whole library in one run, free, if you accept the ads and keeping the app open; SnapDisk's free tier takes several runs for a big library, but each run is in the background with Pause and Stop, and there are no ads at all. If you want the whole library copied in one tap without paying, Photos to USB's free tier is the usable one — SnapDisk's is not, until you repeat the run.

Before the copy: one tap vs pick your folders

Photos to USB is the simpler start. Its listing lays out three steps: plug in the pen drive, allow the drive once (Android asks the first time only), tap Copy. It spots the drive the moment it is in and shows how much space it has. There is no folder picking and no preview: it copies the whole gallery — camera, WhatsApp, and every other folder — in one tap.

SnapDisk asks you to choose first. Pick the From and To folders in settings (the system folder picker, verified in the repo as OpenDocumentTree), plug the drive in, tap Backup in the notification — and the numbers appear once the copy is running, in the progress notification: percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left. The folders are your choice; the plan is not previewed.

Neither app shows a planned-backup summary before the run — FotoSafe does, in the previous comparison in this series, but neither of these two does. The difference here is control against convenience: Photos to USB decides what to copy (everything) and where it lands (one folder per phone, by month); SnapDisk lets you pick the folders, and mirrors your source folder structure on the drive.

During the backup: keep the app open vs background copy

This is the clearest behavioural difference, and it cuts both ways. Photos to USB says it plainly in its own listing: "Keep the app open while it copies. The screen stays on by itself." The copy runs while the app is open and the screen is on — that is the deal for the one-tap whole-gallery copy. Its listing mentions no Pause and Stop controls, and states no copy order.

SnapDisk copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first, by the date the photo was taken — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The progress notification shows real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, with Pause and Stop. If you unplug the disk mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the disk is back; the file in flight resumes from its .part file, and files already copied are never copied twice.

Photos to USB handles the unplug case in its own way: "Pulled the drive out by mistake? Nothing breaks. Plug it back in and it carries on where it stopped. Half copied files are never left looking finished." And on repeat runs it does what SnapDisk does: "Next time, only new photos are copied. Plug the same drive in next month and it skips everything that is already there." That is resume-by-skipping for finished files, and it works. What it does not advertise is the rest: no Pause and Stop, no copy order, and no background copy — the app stays open and the screen stays on until it is done.

After the copy: verification, location, and month folders

Photos to USB verifiably wins this section, on three points its listing states outright. First, location: "Location kept. Android normally strips where a photo was taken when an app reads it. Photos to USB asks for the original, so your copies keep it." Your copies keep where each photo was taken. Second, folders: "Tidy folders. Photos land in one folder per phone, sorted by month, so the drive stays easy to browse on a computer." Third, quality: "Original quality. Every file is copied byte for byte, nothing is compressed." And it adds a completion promise of its own: "Safe to unplug. Each file is fully written to the drive before the app says it is done."

SnapDisk's answer is a different check. It checks the size of every file after copying: each copy writes to a .part file next to the destination and is renamed to the final file only after the size verification passes, so a file that did not arrive whole never pretends it did. Its destination mirrors your source folder structure — Camera, Screenshots, and the rest land where they were on the phone — instead of reorganising by month. Files that cannot be copied — a single file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive, for example — are skipped and listed with their reason instead of failing the whole backup. Photos to USB handles the same FAT32 limit its own way: "FAT32 drives cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so very long videos need an exFAT drive. The app tells you which ones were skipped."

On location, this article does not score SnapDisk: its repo makes no location claim either way, so there is nothing verified to compare against Photos to USB's documented location-kept. Neither side replaces opening a few photos on the drive yourself — a backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy.

Freeing up space: neither app deletes for you

Here is the honest part. Neither app frees up space on your phone today. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes anything (the only delete calls in its source touch .part files, never your photos) — so freeing space is a two-step flow you do yourself: copy to the drive, open a few photos on the drive to check they arrived, then delete the originals from your phone. That flow is covered step by step in How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive.

Photos to USB is in the same place, by its own words. Its listing describes a copy — "copies your gallery onto it" — and does not say it deletes the originals. The "Move your photos to a pen drive and keep them safe" line in its listing is about the use case, not a delete claim: it is the reason you copy, not something the app does to your phone. So if freeing space is why you're choosing an app, the question is not which app deletes — neither does — but which app's copy you trust enough to delete after. SnapDisk's answer is the copy-first-then-delete flow above, with every file size-verified after copying; Photos to USB's answer is the same flow, with each file fully written before the app says it is done.

What to avoid

  • Paying before checking what the purchase buys. Photos to USB's purchase removes the ads — its listing says nothing else about it, and shows no price. SnapDisk's Pro buys auto-backup on connect and unlimited size for $9.99 once. Different money, different thing.
  • Assuming "free" means the same thing on both sides. Photos to USB is free with ads and copies the whole gallery in one tap; SnapDisk is free with no ads and 5 GB per run, repeatable. The trade is ads against the cap — and against keeping the app open.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. This applies to both apps.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Photos to USB tells you to keep the app open — the screen stays on by itself. SnapDisk's copy runs as a foreground service — the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no comparison run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified, and this article says so instead of promising it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. A single file over 4 GB does not fit on FAT32. Both apps skip those files and tell you which ones — use exFAT for a library with very long videos.
  • Plugging in an NTFS drive and expecting to write to it. Photos to USB warns that an NTFS drive opens read-only on many phones, and tells you if that happens. Use FAT32 or exFAT, which is how most drives come.
  • Choosing on whether an app is on Play. Photos to USB is on Google Play today and SnapDisk is not yet — that says where to download it, not how either app copies your photos.
  • Assuming the folder layout does not matter. Photos to USB sorts copies into one folder per phone by month; SnapDisk mirrors your source folders. If you browse the drive on a computer later, that layout is the difference between finding a month and finding a folder.

Rule of thumb: the app that wins is the one whose copy you trust enough to delete after. Compare the free tier, the preview, and the copy — then check the copy on the drive before you delete anything.

Which one fits you

If you want an app on Google Play today, a one-tap copy of the whole gallery, copies that keep their location and land in tidy month folders, and only-new-photos on the next run — and you do not mind ads or keeping the app open while it copies — that is Photos to USB: free with ads, one-time purchase to remove them (the listing shows no price for that purchase). If you want the repeatable free tier (5 GB per run), oldest-first order, Pause and Stop in the notification, per-file size verification, a background copy, and no ads at all, that is SnapDisk: free manual backup up to 5 GB per run, Pro $9.99 once for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size. Either way, back up first, verify the copy, then delete — that order is what keeps your photos safe, whichever app does the copying.
