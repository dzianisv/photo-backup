What you need

A Sharp Aquos (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk) — the Aquos R10, R9, and sense9 all have USB-C with OTG, so no adapter is needed for a USB-C flash drive; a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter — and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Three Sharp specifics: first, OTG is not a question on the current models — spec tables list USB Type-C 3.2 with OTG for the Aquos R10 and the Aquos sense9, and USB Type-C 2.0 with OTG for the Aquos R9 — and Sharp runs near-stock Android with no OTG switch, so the drive mounts on plug-in; if the phone ignores the drive, check Settings > Storage. Second, Sharp ships Files by Google as the stock file manager — Sharp's own FAQ pages point to the Files app for finding downloaded files — and the stock photo backup is Google Photos, which is the route Sharp's own FAQ pages present; Sharp camera photos live in DCIM/Camera — and every current Aquos has a microSDXC slot, so a card is an option, but the card stays in the phone and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is. Third, Sharp phones ship with Google apps including the Play Store, so SnapDisk installs from the v0.1.2 APK on the GitHub release page today and from Play once the listing is live. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. It mounts straight away — look for a "USB storage added" notification. Sharp runs near-stock Android with no OTG switch, so there is nothing to turn on first. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage. All three current models — Aquos R10, R9, and sense9 — have OTG, so the drive should appear on any of them.
  2. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Sharp camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  3. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. Method 2: Files by Google (the stock route, no app needed)

Sharp runs near-stock Android, so the stock file manager is Files by Google, and it can copy your photos to the USB drive: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage. 2. Open Files and confirm the USB drive shows up under external storage. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until Files says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: Sharp's own tools (Google Photos and Google backup — not phone-to-USB)

If you want Sharp's own route, know what each tool actually does — Sharp has no backup tool of its own; the routes on a Sharp Aquos are Google's, plus docomo Data Copy on Japan carrier models, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app, and it is the stock photo backup on Sharp — Sharp's own FAQ pages present Google Photos as the route for backing up photos and videos: open Photos, tap your profile picture, Photos settings, Backup, and turn Backup on — it automatically backs up photos to your Google account, with 15 GB free with every Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead. Google backup is the other Google route: Settings > System > Backup, Back up to Google Drive on — it backs the phone up to your Google account so you can restore app settings and data to a new phone. It is a real backup — but the backup lives in Google's cloud and restores to a phone, not a folder of photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. docomo Data Copy is the backup on Japan carrier models: it copies phonebook, mail, and media to a microSD card, or directly to another phone over a one-to-one connection — but that backs the phone up to a card or another phone, not to a USB drive. Data Storage Box is the same carrier route for call log and music. All of them work fine for what they are. What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • Older Sharp models use micro-USB. The Aquos Zeta SH-01H and other older models have a micro-USB port, so a USB-C drive or adapter will not fit — a micro-USB OTG adapter is the route there. SnapDisk still needs Android 7.0 or later.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into any current Aquos — the Aquos R10, R9, and sense9 all have USB-C with OTG — so an adapter is only needed for a regular USB-A drive.
  • Looking for an OTG switch that isn't there. Sharp runs near-stock Android with no OTG toggle — the drive mounts on plug-in, so if nothing appears, check Settings > Storage rather than hunting for a switch.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google backup and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. Drag-and-drop through a computer works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop tool.
  • Mistaking a PC backup for a USB backup. Sharp's own manuals back your Aquos up to a computer — a real backup, but it lands on the PC, not on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open.
  • Mistaking a carrier backup for a USB backup. docomo Data Copy backs your Aquos up to a microSD card or another phone — a real backup, but it lands on the card or the other phone, not on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open.
  • Mistaking a microSD copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a memory card is a real local copy — every current Aquos has a microSDXC slot — but the card stays in the phone, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 17, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On Sharp the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google backup — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
