Back up Kik photos to a USB drive

Kik photos are not on your phone until you save them — and Kik never saves them for you. Save first, one photo at a time, then the backup is a one-tap job.

November 25, 2026 · 12 min read

TL;DR: Kik does not save the photos you receive — not automatically, not ever. A photo you only viewed in a chat lives inside Kik, not in any folder on your phone, and no backup app, file manager, or USB drive can reach it, because there is nothing to reach. The save is manual and one photo at a time: open the photo full-screen, long-press it, tap Save — or tap the download arrow in the top-right corner — and it lands in your gallery. The exact folder is disputed between sources: the dedicated 2026 save guide says Internal storage/Pictures/Kik first and Internal storage/DCIM/Kik second, Wondershare's guides say the DCIM folder or the gallery, and a recovery guide says the gallery under an album likely called Kik — so check the gallery first, then Pictures/Kik, then DCIM/Kik, then DCIM. From there the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the saved Kik photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: the save is one tap per photo and there is no save-all button for a chat; Kik has no native cloud chat backup, so a photo you never saved is lost if you log out, delete the app, or sign in on another device; a stray .nomedia file in the Kik folder can hide saved photos from the gallery — and therefore from the default run — even though the files are on the disk, and the fix is to delete it and restart; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. As of November 25, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated Kik-photos-to-USB how-to: the results are the save guides that stop at the gallery, computer-backup tools that name Kik as a data type in a list, and generic OTG copy guides that never name Kik at all.

The wall: Kik saves nothing by itself — a photo you only viewed lives in the chat, not on the phone

Start with the fact that decides the whole job, because Kik is the quietest wall in the messaging-app series so far. WhatsApp at least has an auto-download switch to argue about, and Messenger has a Save button that puts the photo in a named folder. Kik has neither: Kik does not automatically save photos or videos you receive. The dedicated 2026 save guide states it plainly — this is a privacy and storage-saving feature, and you must manually tap on the image or video and select Save or the download icon for it to be saved to your device — and Wondershare's guides agree, in two places: the photos and videos you get in a chat will stay there, and you cannot open them outside the application unless you save them. If you only viewed a photo in the chat, it remains on Kik's servers and is not on your phone's local storage. That is the wall, and it is a total one: a chat full of photos you looked at and never saved is a chat full of photos that do not exist anywhere outside Kik, and running SnapDisk's default backup on that phone copies every photo in the gallery — and zero Kik photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no Kik photos to copy.

The step that puts the photos on the phone is the built-in save, one photo at a time, and it has two shapes that amount to the same tap. Open the chat, open the photo full-screen, press and hold it: a pop-up appears, tap Save, and the picture is saved to the camera roll of your phone. That is the route Wondershare's save guide gives, step by step. The other shape is the download arrow: locate the picture, press and hold it in full screen, then tap the Download arrow in the top-right corner of the Kik app. Either way the result is the same — one file, saved to the gallery — and either way it is one photo per tap. There is no save-all button for a chat, no select-all, no export conversation: a chat that has collected hundreds of photos over months is a long evening of tapping, and this article does not pretend otherwise. The bulk route is the PC tool in Method 3, which backs Kik up to a computer, not to the phone, and the phone route is the manual save. Download the ones that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting.

Two scope facts, because they decide what this article can and cannot promise. First, Kik has no native cloud chat backup — the 2026 guide says so in so many words, contrasting Kik with WhatsApp — which means there is no restore-from-the-cloud safety net behind the chat at all. Wondershare's save guide lists what deletes Kik photos: deleting the Kik app, logging out of the Kik account, logging in with a different device, the Kik app data folder getting full, deactivating the account, or the phone's internal storage filling up. Those are the in-chat photos — the ones you never saved. A photo already saved to the gallery is an ordinary file on the phone and survives all of that; a photo still only in the chat does not. That is the urgency in this article: the save is not housekeeping, it is the only copy that survives leaving Kik. Second, the save itself can fail silently if Kik was denied storage permission: the guide says Kik may then download the photo to a temporary, inaccessible cache folder that gets cleared later, and the folder may never appear at all. If you saved a photo and there is no Kik folder anywhere, permissions are the first suspect, and the fix is in the folder section below — Settings, Apps, Kik, Permissions, Storage or Files and media set to Allow — then save the photo again.

And a word on whose photos these are, because Kik photos are usually your own conversations, not someone else's catalogue. The photos this article covers are the ones people sent you — a picture a friend took, a photo from a group chat, an image someone shared that you want to keep — and the ones you sent, which you usually still have as originals. Saving them to your own drive for your own records is the job. They are also someone else's photos in a plain sense: a friend's picture of their own life, a photo someone sent you in confidence. Saving a file does not grant permission to reuse it — so re-posting a photo someone sent you in a private chat, or sharing it onward as if it were yours, is a different act, and the same courtesy applies here as anywhere: the person who sent it is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else. This article's methods copy the file to your drive; what you do with it afterwards is yours to decide, and asking is the honest step before anything leaves your phone. Only save photos you have permission to keep — and this article makes no claim about recovering photos from a chat you have already deleted, because Kik keeps no server copy to recover from.

Where saved Kik photos land — the guides disagree, and a hidden file can hide them

This section exists because the folder is the one fact the sources do not agree on, and a guide that picks one of them as certain would be guessing — and because Kik has a wrinkle the other messaging apps in this series do not: a stray hidden file can make saved photos invisible to the gallery even when they are on the disk. The dedicated 2026 save guide, the most specific source, gives a table: on Android the primary save location is Internal Storage > Pictures > Kik, and the alternative is Internal Storage > DCIM > Kik. Its own walkthrough says the same: open the file manager, go to Internal Storage, open Pictures, and inside Pictures there should be a folder named Kik; if it is not there, go back and look in DCIM, because some phones use that as the primary camera folder and Kik might place its folder there instead. Wondershare's save guide says something else: if you saved the picture from the Kik app itself, you can find your images in the DCIM folder or Gallery, and the downloaded picture can be viewed in the gallery section of your phone. Its recovery guide's FAQ says the same in one line: the Kik pictures you receive or share are stored in the DCIM folder, and you can also find them in your device's gallery. A third guide, a recovery walkthrough, says saved photos appear in the phone's gallery app under an album likely called Kik, and can also be reached through the file manager or by checking the DCIM folder. Those are three different answers — a named folder under Pictures, the DCIM folder, and a gallery album — and they disagree because the folder really does vary: by device, by Android version, and by Kik version. So this article does not state one path as the path. It gives the check order instead, and the check order is the same on every phone.

Check the gallery first. Open your gallery app and look for a “Kik” album. If it is there, the save works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see the photos, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If the album is not there yet, no photo has been saved: save one as in Method 1 and look again. Then check Pictures/Kik in the file manager: Internal Storage, Pictures, Kik — the 2026 guide's primary location. Then check DCIM/Kik: Internal Storage, DCIM, Kik — the same guide's alternative. Then check DCIM itself: Wondershare's guides name the DCIM folder flat, with no Kik subfolder, and the recovery walkthrough agrees the DCIM folder is worth checking. If the photo is in none of those places, check that Kik has permission to save photos — without it, the app cannot write to the gallery at all — and save one photo again, watching for the confirmation that it saved.

Then the wrinkle, because it is the one Kik-specific failure this series has not had to state before. A .nomedia file is a zero-byte file that tells Android's media scanner to ignore all media in its folder, and the 2026 guide names it as a cause of saved Kik photos not showing in the gallery: if one somehow ends up in your Kik folder, your gallery will hide all your saved photos and videos from view. The guide is careful about the word “somehow” — it does not claim Kik puts the file there itself — and this article keeps that care: a .nomedia file can land in the folder, and when it does, the photos are on the disk but invisible to the gallery, to the media index, and therefore to SnapDisk's default run. The fix is the guide's own, step by step: open the file manager, turn on Show hidden files or Show hidden system files in its settings, navigate back to the Kik folder, look for a file named exactly .nomedia, long-press it and delete it, then restart the phone — the restart forces the media scanner to run again, and with the .nomedia file gone it will discover the Kik photos and add them to the gallery. Two more causes from the same guide cover the rest of the missing-photo cases: storage permission denied, fixed in Settings, Apps, Kik, Permissions, Storage or Files and media set to Allow; and media-scanner lag, where Android simply has not picked the new file up yet and a restart forces the rescan. If the Kik folder does not exist at all, it is almost certainly the permissions cause: Kik never had a place to save to, so nothing was ever written.

One more scope fact for this section, because it decides what the drive copy holds: the saved file is the photo as Kik served it in the chat, not necessarily the original the sender took. Kik compresses photos in transit, the same way every messenger does, and the save keeps the copy Kik delivered — SnapDisk copies that file byte-for-byte, no conversion, no quality change — but it cannot recover quality Kik already removed. If the photo you want is one you sent, the original is usually still in your camera roll in DCIM/Camera at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file the default run already covers; back that up instead. The Kik route for a photo you sent is only needed when the original is gone. And the photos you took inside Kik's own camera are a separate case again: the guides say images and videos taken inside the app are placed in a folder in the phone's gallery, and a screenshot taken inside Kik lands in the Screenshots folder — so a photo you took in Kik is a gallery file already, and the save step in this article is for the photos other people sent you.

What you need

An Android phone — Android 7.0 or later, which is SnapDisk's minimum version — with the Kik app installed and signed in, plus one USB drive your phone can read: a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. SnapDisk itself is installed from the v0.1.2 APK — it is not on Google Play yet — so install it by allowing “install unknown apps” for the browser or Files app you download it with, which is standard Android. You need nothing else for the main route: no file manager with hidden files, no extra app, no account. The save step is inside Kik itself, and the backup is SnapDisk's default run. Check the drive's space first: Kik photos are small — a chat photo is a few megabytes at most — so space is almost never the problem here, but look anyway, because a whole-library default run copies everything, not just Kik. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit, and exFAT is the format to use for a photo drive.

Before any method, settle the one question that decides whether the phone route works at all: does your phone see the USB drive? Plug the drive in and open Settings > Connected devices or Settings > Storage — the drive should appear there. That check is the test, and it takes ten seconds. If the drive does not appear, try a different adapter first — cheap OTG adapters are the usual failure — then a different drive, and a powered hub if the drive is a spinning hard drive rather than a flash drive. And one warning up front, because it applies to every route below: do not delete anything from the phone until the USB copy is done and verified. The photos in the chat are Kik's copy, and the photos in the gallery are yours — but until the drive copy is checked, the gallery is the only copy outside Kik, and deleting from the gallery before the check is how a photo ends up nowhere at all. And do not log out of Kik, delete the app, or sign in on another device until the save and the copy are both done: Kik has no cloud backup, and the in-chat photos are lost when the account leaves the phone.

Method 1: Save the photos, then back up with SnapDisk

This is the route to use, because it is the only one that joins the whole job — the Kik photo, out of the chat and into the gallery, onto a USB drive plugged into the phone, no computer — using only the apps already on the phone plus SnapDisk. It has two halves, and they have to happen in order: the save, which puts the photos on the phone as files, and the SnapDisk copy, which puts them on the drive. The first half is the work. The second half is the backup. Skipping the first half is the mistake this article exists to prevent: running SnapDisk's default backup with nothing saved copies every photo in the gallery — and zero Kik photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no Kik photos to copy.

  1. Save the photos from inside the chat. Open the chat, open the photo full-screen, press and hold it, and tap Save when the pop-up appears. The other shape of the same tap is the download arrow: press and hold the picture in full screen, then tap the Download arrow in the top-right corner. Either way the photo is saved to the camera roll. There is no save-all button — every photo is one tap — so a chat with a hundred photos is a long job, and it is worth doing in sittings rather than all at once. Save the ones that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting. If Save does nothing, check Kik's storage permission first — Settings > Apps > Kik > Permissions, Storage or Files and media, Allow — and consider updating the Kik app, because Wondershare's guide warns the save can fail on an outdated version. If a video will not save, screen-record it instead: open the video full-screen, start the phone's built-in screen recorder, play the video with the sound on, stop the recording, and trim the start and end in the gallery — the 2026 guide's own fallback, and a recording is a copy of the screen, not the video file, so it is lower quality and carries the app chrome around it.
  2. Check the Kik album appeared. Open your gallery app, go to Albums, and look for “Kik”. If it is there, the save works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see the photos, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If the album is not there, work through the check order in the section above: Pictures/Kik, then DCIM/Kik, then DCIM itself. If the files are in the folder but not in the gallery, the .nomedia wrinkle is the suspect: open the file manager, turn on Show hidden files, open the Kik folder, delete the .nomedia file if there is one, and restart the phone to force a rescan. If the Kik folder does not exist at all, the permissions cause is the suspect: allow storage permission and save one photo again, watching for the confirmation that it saved.
  3. Plug in the drive and run SnapDisk. Plug the USB drive into the phone. SnapDisk offers the backup in a notification — tap Backup — or open the app and tap Start. The run copies oldest-first, verifies every file by size after copying, and shows progress with Pause and Stop in the notification. The free run covers up to 5 GB, and Kik photos are small, so one run is almost always enough for the Kik half — though a whole-library run copies everything else too, and that is the point: the saved Kik photos come along with the rest, with no folder to pick and no step to remember. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  4. Do not plan around the photos you never saved. A photo you only viewed in the chat is still inside Kik, and no step in this article changes that: Kik does not auto-save, there is no save-all, and there is no export. If a photo matters, save it from the chat now, while the chat is still on this phone and the account is still signed in — logging out, deleting the app, or signing in on another device can delete the in-chat photos, and Kik keeps no server copy to recover from. A screenshot is the only copy left for a photo the built-in save will not give you — and a screenshot is a picture of the screen, not the photo: it is lower quality, it carries the app chrome around it, and it lands in the Screenshots folder, not with the saved photos. Do not plan a backup around saving what Kik will not let you save.

Two honest limits. First, the save is not retroactive in the other direction either: saving a photo today does not put the photos from a chat you already deleted onto the phone — a deleted chat is gone, and Kik keeps no server copy to recover from. That is step 1's job, and it is a manual job: one tap per photo, or the bulk route in Method 3 for the ones you are willing to take to a computer. Second, the 5 GB free cap is per run — a Kik album will never reach it, but a whole-library default run might, and running it again picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Third, SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the photos from the gallery after the check is always your step, never the app's — and deleting them from the chat is Kik's, not SnapDisk's, and it does not free the drive copy. The drive copy is the backup; the gallery copy is the original until you decide otherwise.

Copying only some Kik photos? If you would rather copy just a few photos than the whole album, the From folder picker still takes a folder, not single files — so copy the ones you want into their own folder first (in the file manager, or by saving them to the gallery and selecting them there), then point the picker at that folder. The picker route keeps only recognised photo and video extensions. If the drive is unplugged mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

And the check that matters for this article: after the run, compare the count against the album. A green backup that copied the whole library has still copied zero Kik photos if nothing was ever saved from the chat — which is exactly what happens when the photos were only viewed. Open the drive and look for the Kik folder by name: the default run mirrors the source tree, so the album should be there as its own folder. If it is missing, the save is the suspect, not the run — save the photos from the chat first, then run again. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — the only files it ever deletes are its own half-finished .part files — so deleting the originals after the check is always your step, never the app's.

Method 2: Point the From picker at the Kik folder

If you would rather copy just the Kik photos and nothing else — and the photos are already saved — point SnapDisk's From folder picker at the folder that holds them. Open SnapDisk, tap the From folder picker, and navigate to the folder you found them in, using the check order in the folder section above: the gallery's Kik album first, then Pictures/Kik, then DCIM/Kik, then DCIM — and tap Backup. To get everything in Pictures at once, pick the parent Pictures folder instead: SnapDisk walks the folder recursively, so one run copies the Kik folder and any other album that lives under Pictures together. The same 5 GB free cap applies, and the same oldest-first order, size verification, Pause and Stop.

The honest limit on this route is that it is the code-supported route, not the test-proven one — and this article says so rather than claiming a test that never ran — and the folder itself is the second honest limit. The picker is the system's folder picker, and the code has no Kik path handling anywhere — zero matches for “kik” in the whole source tree — so the navigation is yours, step by step, through folders whose names are ordinary. But the exact folder name is disputed between sources — the 2026 guide says Pictures/Kik first and DCIM/Kik second, Wondershare's guides say the DCIM folder or the gallery — so this article does not state one folder path as the path, and no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet. Neither candidate name starts with a dot, so neither is hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and the folder sits at the top of internal storage, not under Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks the Telegram folder does not apply here at all. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the parent Pictures folder reaches every album under it. But the end-to-end pick is untested, and this article marks it as such. If the picker will not show the folder on your phone, fall back to Method 1: save the photos to the gallery first, then run the default backup, which is the route that is proven to work. And one more scope fact for this route: the folder walk keeps only recognised photo and video extensions — a document in the folder is simply skipped, and a chat is never copied, because a chat is not a photo or a video. For a whole chat, use Method 3 — this article is only about the photo and video files.

One Kik-specific reason Method 2 matters more here than in the other messaging-app articles: the .nomedia wrinkle. The default run reads the media index, and a .nomedia file in the Kik folder keeps saved photos out of the index — so the default run cannot see them, even though the files are on the disk. The From picker route does not read the media index at all: it walks the folder tree directly and lists the files it finds, so a .nomedia file does not hide them from it. That makes the picker the fallback when the gallery will not show the Kik album and the .nomedia fix has not worked yet — pick the Kik folder and copy it directly, then fix the .nomedia file afterwards so the default run sees the photos next time. This is a code-grounded difference, not a guess: the picker path lists files from the document tree, while the default path queries MediaStore — and .nomedia only affects the media scanner, not a direct folder walk. The pick itself is still untested end to end on a real phone, and this article marks it as such — but the reason it should work where the default run cannot is the code's own two paths, stated as such.

Method 3: Bulk Kik backup on a PC — and why it is not a phone-to-USB job

Kik has no bulk save of its own, and the bulk route that exists runs off the phone — and it is worth naming honestly, because it is the first result for every Kik-backup search — and it is not this job. Wondershare's MobileTrans backs up Kik data on a Windows or Mac computer: launch the software, connect the phone, select Kik under Backup and Restore, click Backup, and it backs up all the Kik data, including messages, photos, and videos, to the computer. Dr.Fone's computer-backup guide names Kik the same way, as a data type in a list of things its PC software can back up. This article names both without endorsing either of them: they are third-party tools with their own terms and their own permissions, and neither of them was tested this run.

But neither of them is a phone-to-USB backup, and the difference is where the files land. The PC tools put the Kik data on a computer: the files are in a backup on that computer, and they still need a second copy — computer to USB drive — before they are a backup you can open anywhere. The backup is also a backup file, not plain photo files: it holds messages and photos together in the tool's own format, and getting the photos out as files you can open is a second step inside the tool. So use the bulk route for what it is: the way to get a whole Kik history off the phone in one sitting, for the day you need the whole chat at once, including the messages. For plain photo files on a drive you can open anywhere, save the photos to the gallery first — Method 1 — and copy them to a USB drive with SnapDisk, no computer. The two routes are not rivals; they are different shapes, and this article is about the plain-files one. And the same permission rule applies to both of them: only save photos you have permission to keep, and do not use a backup tool to pull someone else's photos out of a chat you were never meant to keep.

Can SnapDisk read the Kik folder directly?

This is the question the whole article turns on, and it deserves a straight answer in three parts — what the code proves it can do, what is untested, and what would prove the rest. The first part is proven, from the code at the current base and from how Android's media index works — and it is the same shape as the Messenger and Viber cases, with one Kik-specific condition. SnapDisk's default run reads the phone's media index — it queries the MediaStore images and videos tables, oldest-first by date taken, with no folder filter. A Kik photo saved to the gallery is an ordinary indexed file: it shows in the gallery as a “Kik” album, and gallery apps read the media index. The album's existence in the gallery is the evidence the folder is indexed — if a .nomedia file sat in the folder, the media scanner would skip it and the album would not appear in the gallery at all. The query is in PhotoQuery.kt, the no-folder-filter shape is the code's own design — the query call passes no selection at all — and the gallery evidence is the platform fact that closes the loop. So the default run sees saved Kik photos that show in the gallery, and it sees them with no step beyond the save. That is not an assumption: it is the code's query shape plus the gallery evidence, stated as such. And the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only — the query reads every indexed image and video on the phone, whatever folder it sits in, so the Kik album is covered the same way DCIM is.

The condition is the .nomedia wrinkle, and it is the part that makes Kik different from Pinterest in this series: the default run's coverage is conditional on the photos showing in the gallery. If a stray .nomedia file hides the Kik folder from the gallery, the photos are on the disk but not in the media index, and the default run cannot see them — not because the code is wrong, but because the index it reads has nothing to return. The 2026 guide names this exact failure, and the fix is its own: show hidden files, delete the .nomedia file, restart the phone to force a rescan. Until that is done, the default run copies zero Kik photos while the files sit in the folder. This article states the condition in the TL;DR, in the folder section, in Method 1 step 2, and here — because a guide that promised the default run covers every saved Kik photo, without the condition, would be wrong on exactly the phones where the save looks broken.

The second part is untested, and this article marks it as such rather than guessing in either direction: whether SnapDisk's From folder picker reaches the Kik folder end to end on a real phone. The picker is the system's folder picker — ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree, in MainActivity.kt — and it opens with no folder pre-selected: there is no initial-folder hint anywhere in the code, and no Kik path handling anywhere in the app — zero matches for “kik” in the whole source tree. The navigation chain is ordinary folders — the gallery album, Pictures/Kik, DCIM/Kik, or DCIM — none of them dot-prefixed, so none of them is hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and the folder sits at the top of internal storage, outside Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks the Telegram folder does not apply here. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the parent Pictures folder should reach every album under it. But the exact folder name is disputed between sources, and no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first. The picker has one code-grounded advantage the default run does not, stated in Method 2: it walks the folder tree directly instead of reading the media index, so a .nomedia file does not hide the photos from it — but that advantage is itself untested end to end, and this article marks it as such too.

The third part is what the code does prove about the rest of the job, so the limits above are not mistaken for a wider doubt. The manifest requests the media-read permissions (READ_MEDIA_IMAGES and READ_MEDIA_VIDEO, plus the older READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE capped at Android 12), the notification permission, and foreground-service permissions — and no INTERNET permission at all, zero occurrences, so the photos physically cannot leave the device. It does not request all-files access — no MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE anywhere — which is the permission a file manager uses to browse another app's folders freely, and the absence is the reason SnapDisk cannot simply walk into the Kik folder the way a file manager can; the folder picker is the route instead, and it needs your tap. The backup itself is the verified core: oldest-first by date taken with a date-modified fallback (PhotoQuery.kt), every file size-verified after copying (BackupEngine.kt), Pause and Stop in the notification (NotificationHelper.kt, BackupService.kt), files of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive skipped with their reason instead of stalling the job (BackupService.kt), and the free run capped at 5 GB with Pro a one-time $9.99 purchase (ProPrefs.kt). None of that changes the picker verdict — it bounds it. The backup engine is proven; the end-to-end pick of this exact folder is not yet tested.

Photos you sent vs photos you received

The route is the same for both, but the starting point is not, and it is worth saying which one you are saving before you start. Photos you received — the case this article is really about — are the ones the save governs: with a save they land in the gallery, and without it they are not on the phone at all, and the methods above are written for them. Photos you sent are different, because you usually have a better original: a photo you sent on Kik is almost certainly still in your camera roll, in DCIM/Camera, at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file that SnapDisk's default run already covers — and the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only, so it is covered either way. Back that up instead. The copy in the chat is a version Kik made for sending — smaller, compressed — and the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping. That matters more than it sounds, because the photos you send are often the ones you care about most: a picture of the finished work for a client, a photo of the goods before dispatch, the shot you took to show someone what something looks like. The original is the one they will ask for again. The Kik route for a photo you sent is only needed when the original is gone — you deleted it from the camera roll, or it was a photo you took inside Kik's camera and never saved — and then the chat copy is the only one left: save it from the chat the same way as a received photo, and the default run picks it up like any other indexed photo, or point the From picker at the folder and copy it directly.

One related corner is worth a line, because Kik users hit it and it looks like a shortcut that is not one: Kik keeps its working data under Android/data, and a guide may tell you to dig in Android/data for a cached photo. That is a different thing from saved photos — the cached copies are fragments keyed to the installation, not your photos in any usable sense — and Android/data is guarded by scoped storage, so a file manager may not even show it. Wondershare's recovery guide names the cache folder outright — Internal Storage, Android, data, kik.android, cache — as a place its deep scan looks for orphaned photo fragments, which is a recovery tool's job, not a backup route. Do not go digging in Android/data for your Kik photos: they are not there. The photos are in the gallery once saved, or nowhere at all when they are not, and those are the only two places this article's methods touch.

Checking the drive after the copy

The check has two halves, and they have to happen in order: first that the save actually produced a file in the gallery, then that the drive holds it. After the SnapDisk run finishes, open the drive on a computer or on the phone itself and count: the drive should hold the Kik photos you saved, in a Kik folder of their own — the default run mirrors the source tree, so the album should be there by name — and a few opened at random should render fully, videos included. Open a Kik photo itself — not just any photo on the drive — because a run that covered the rest of the library would still look complete on a random check while the one album you cared about was missing. If the count is short, the run is not the first suspect — the save is. Check the gallery: is the “Kik” album actually there, and does it hold the photos you expected? If the album is empty, the photos were never saved — save the ones that matter from the chat, and run again. If the album holds them and the drive does not, the run is the suspect, and the run needs repeating, not a different folder. If the files are in the Kik folder but not in the gallery, the .nomedia wrinkle is the suspect: delete the file, restart, and run again — or pick the folder directly with the From picker, which does not depend on the gallery.

If the drive's free space ran out mid-run, the run stops with the full error shown; free space by moving the drive's contents to a bigger drive first, or back up to a second drive. If the run stopped at the 5 GB free cap — unlikely for a Kik album alone, possible on a whole-library default run — run it again and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. And the check that matters most for a photo that only existed inside Kik: confirm the drive copy before you delete anything from the phone. The copy in the gallery is an ordinary file and will survive; the copy in the chat depends on Kik keeping it, and a logged-out account, a deleted app, or a new phone ends that. The drive is a plain folder of plain files. Anyone who picks up the drive can open them. That is the price of a copy you can open anywhere, and it is worth stating before the photos leave the phone.

What to avoid

  • Assuming Kik saves the photos you receive. It does not — Kik does not auto-save photos or videos, and a photo you only viewed lives in the chat, not in any folder. A one-tap backup copies zero Kik photos until you save them from the chat first.
  • Assuming the save covers a whole chat. It does not — the built-in save is one photo at a time, and there is no save-all button. A chat with a hundred photos is a long job of one tap each; the bulk route runs on a PC, not on the phone.
  • Assuming a deleted chat can be recovered. It cannot — Kik has no native cloud chat backup, and the guides say a deleted chat and its unsaved photos are gone. Save the photos that matter while the chat is still on this phone.
  • Assuming logging out or deleting the app is safe. It is not — the save guide lists logging out, deleting the app, and signing in on another device among the things that delete Kik photos. Save first, copy second, and only then change anything about the account.
  • Assuming one folder path for saved Kik photos. UNVERIFIED — the guides disagree: the 2026 guide says Pictures/Kik first and DCIM/Kik second, Wondershare's guides say the DCIM folder or the gallery, and a recovery guide says a gallery album likely called Kik. Check the gallery first, then Pictures/Kik, then DCIM/Kik, then DCIM — do not plan a backup around a single path no source agrees on.
  • Assuming saved photos always show in the gallery. They do not always — a stray .nomedia file in the Kik folder hides them from the gallery and from the media index, even though the files are on the disk. Show hidden files, delete the .nomedia file, and restart the phone to force a rescan — or pick the folder directly with the From picker, which does not depend on the gallery.
  • Assuming the default SnapDisk run covers photos you only viewed. It cannot — proven from the code and the platform: the default run reads the media index, and a photo that was never saved never enters the index. A green backup copies zero Kik photos from chats you never saved from.
  • Assuming the default run covers saved photos hidden by .nomedia. It cannot — the .nomedia file keeps them out of the media index, and the default run reads the index. Delete the file and restart, or use the From picker, which walks the folder directly.
  • Assuming the From picker route is test-proven. Untested — no SnapDisk test has picked the Kik folder yet, and the exact folder name is disputed between sources. The code supports it and the folder names are ordinary, but do not plan a backup around it as the only route; Method 1's save-to-gallery-first workflow is the proven one.
  • Assuming the chat copy is the original. A photo you sent is usually still in DCIM/Camera at full quality. The chat copy is a smaller, compressed version Kik made for sending; back the original up, and use the chat copy only when the original is gone.
  • Looking in Android/data for your Kik photos. That folder holds Kik's working data — cached fragments, not your photos — and scoped storage guards it. The photos are in the gallery once saved, or nowhere at all when they are not.
  • Using a PC backup tool and thinking the USB job is done. The tool puts the Kik data on a computer, not on a drive — the files land in a backup on that computer and still need a second copy to reach a USB drive. The USB leg is still SnapDisk's job — the tool replaces nothing in this article.
  • Using a screenshot and thinking it is the photo. It is not — a screenshot is a picture of the screen, lower quality, carrying the app chrome around it, and it lands in the Screenshots folder, not with the saved photos. Save the photo itself when the save works, and screenshot only when it does not.
  • Assuming screen-off continuation under battery saver. UNVERIFIED — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for a library with long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, and a library with long 4K clips will hold one. SnapDisk skips those files and lists them — use exFAT.
  • Assuming the free 5 GB cap covers the whole library. It is per run. A library over 5 GB needs several free runs — run it again and it picks up where it stopped — or one $9.99 Pro purchase.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. An unplug mid-copy leaves a half-copied library that looks finished until you count it.
  • Checking the drive by opening any photo. Open a Kik photo itself — not just any photo on the drive. A random check passes even when the one album you cared about was never copied, because the photos that did copy render fine.

Rule of thumb: Save first, then back it up — Kik saves nothing by itself, the save is one tap per photo, and the gallery is the bridge to the drive. The save is one tap per photo; the backup after it is one tap for all of them.
