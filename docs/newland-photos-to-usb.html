Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Check your model can run SnapDisk. The MT90 Orca runs Android 7.0 in its distributor spec, the MT90 Orca II and Orca III run Android 8.1, the MT90 Orca Pro runs Android 10, the MT65 Beluga IV runs Android 8.1 and the Beluga V runs Android 11, the MT67 Sei runs Android 11 (one listing says Android 13) and the Sei II runs Android 15, the N7 Cachalot Pro runs Android 10 and the Pro II runs Android 13 with an Android 15 upgrade released, the MT93 Megattera Pro runs Android 13 with an Android 15 upgrade released, the MT95 Kambur Pro runs Android 11, the Pro II runs Android 13, and the Pro III runs Android 15 — every handheld listed clears the Android 7.0 minimum except the MT65 Beluga II, which runs Android 5.1 and cannot run SnapDisk at all, so Method 4 is the route on that model. Check Settings > About phone if you are unsure which Android version your unit runs — and on the MT90 Orca, check it first: one source lists the original MT90 on Android 5.1, below the minimum.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into the USB-C port on the N7, MT93 and MT95 models, and a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter there; on the MT90, MT65 and MT67 models the port is Micro-USB, so a regular USB-A drive needs a Micro-USB OTG adapter. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. One Newland wrinkle: no source found confirms OTG or host support on twelve of the fifteen handhelds at all, so on those models the drive mounting is the test — if the drive never appears in Settings > Storage, the device does not host drives and Method 4 is the route.
  3. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Newland camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  4. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  5. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  6. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: a file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

A file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — the generic guides' route is an OTG connection with the built-in file manager, and it is the manual route for external storage: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a USB-C drive plugs straight into the USB-C port on the N7, MT93 and MT95 models, a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter there, and on the MT90, MT65 and MT67 models a regular USB-A drive needs a Micro-USB OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. On the twelve models where no source confirms OTG, if the drive never appears, the device does not host drives and Method 4 is the route. 2. Open the file manager (Files by Google on Newland handhelds) and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option. One note: the file-manager route works on the MT65 Beluga II too — it needs no app install, so the Android 7.0 minimum does not apply to it — but that unit still needs to host the drive, so the drive mounting is the test there as well.

Method 3: Newland's own tools (Google Photos, Google Drive, or a microSD card — not phone-to-USB)

If you want Newland's own route, know what each tool actually does — there is no Newland backup page pointing at a USB drive at all, and none of the stock tools writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app, and it is the stock photo backup on Newland handhelds: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead. 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — "on Android devices the default built-in service is Google Drive. With Google Drive you have 15Gb free" — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 15 GB of free storage. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. 4. A microSD card is the local option on most Newland handhelds — the MT90 Orca II takes a card up to 32 GB, the MT90 Orca Pro up to 128 GB, the MT65 Beluga IV and V up to 128 GB, the N7 Cachalot Pro up to 128 GB, and the MT95 Kambur Pro III up to 2 TB. It is a real local copy — but the card stays in the device, and opening the card anywhere else needs a card reader. 5. A computer copy is the other way to move files off the device — connect the handheld to a computer with a USB cable and copy DCIM from the device to the computer. It is a real backup — but it needs a PC in between, and the backup lands on the PC, not on a USB drive as plain files straight from the device. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.

Method 4: through a computer (if you have one)

If a PC or Mac is handy, the old route still works: plug the handheld into the computer with a USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. This works fine, and it is the only route above that needs a second device in between. It is also the only route that works at all on the MT65 Beluga II, which runs Android 5.1 and cannot run SnapDisk, and the only route that works if no Newland model you own mounts a drive. If the device and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.

  • Anything below Android 7.0 cannot run SnapDisk at all. Older Newland handhelds exist on Android 5.1, so check Settings > About phone if you are unsure: below Android 7.0, SnapDisk cannot install, and the computer copy in Method 4 is the route.
  • The adapter differs by drive and by model, not by brand. The N7, MT93 and MT95 lines have a USB-C port, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into any of them and a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter; the MT90, MT65 and MT67 lines have a Micro-USB port, so a regular USB-A drive needs a Micro-USB OTG adapter there. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the device to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into the N7, MT93 and MT95 models — so no adapter is needed on any of them for a USB-C drive — while a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter there and a Micro-USB OTG adapter on the MT90, MT65 and MT67 models. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the device to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • Assuming a model hosts drives because another Newland model does. The MT90 Orca, the MT90 Orca II and the MT95 Kambur Pro all list OTG in their spec interfaces — but no source found confirms it on the other twelve handhelds, so test with a drive on your model, and use Method 2 or 4 on it if the drive never mounts.
  • Installing SnapDisk on a Newland unit below Android 7.0. Older Newland handhelds run Android 5.1 — the MT65 Beluga II runs Android 5.1, and one source lists the original MT90 on Android 5.1 too — check Settings > About phone first: on an Android 5.1 unit the computer copy in Method 4 is the route, not this article's one-tap method.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your device up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. A computer copy with a USB cable moves files to a computer — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The Newland spec sheets and product pages are real Newland pages — but the spec sheets only list an Android version, a chipset, or a USB connector type, and the product pages only describe the hardware. Neither of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking a manual for a phone-to-USB guide. The MT90 Orca Pro manual and the MT95 Kambur Pro user guide are real Newland documents — but neither describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking OTG support on one model for OTG support on the other. The MT90 Orca, the MT90 Orca II and the MT95 Kambur Pro all list OTG in their spec interfaces — but no source found confirms it on the other twelve handhelds, so test with a drive on your model, and use Method 2 or 4 on it if the drive never mounts.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — the MT90 Orca II takes a card up to 32 GB, the MT90 Orca Pro up to 128 GB, the MT65 Beluga IV and V up to 128 GB, the N7 Cachalot Pro up to 128 GB, and the MT95 Kambur Pro III up to 2 TB — but the card stays in the device, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Newland handheld the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the device.
