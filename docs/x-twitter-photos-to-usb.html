Save X (Twitter) photos to USB from Android

Your photos on X live on X's servers, not on your phone. Save each one in the app — it lands in the Twitter folder — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 25, 2026 · 13 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see on X is not on your phone yet. X — formerly Twitter — is a social feed, not a photo library: you can see the photo, open it, like it, and bookmark the post, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you save it, one photo at a time. In the X app on Android, tap the photo to open it full-screen, tap the three-dot menu in the top-right corner, and tap Save — the app shows an "Image saved" message, and the photo lands in a folder called Twitter, visible in your Gallery. That is the whole first half of the job, and it is the half every other guide skips: the generic Android-to-USB guides start from photos already on the phone, and the X guides that do exist stop at the phone — save to the phone, done — with no next step to a drive. From the Twitter folder the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the saved X photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: X saves nothing by itself — no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and a bookmark is a link inside your account, not a file, so if the author deletes the post your bookmark points at nothing; on Android you cannot pick where the photo lands, unlike the iPhone app, which offers a save-to-files choice; a saved photo sorts by the day you saved it, not the day it was posted, because X serves you its own copy and the taken date does not survive it; the saved copy is X's resized copy, not the photographer's original file; GIFs and videos are not photos — a GIF is a short looping video, and video download on X is a Premium feature for certain posts only; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. As of November 26, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated X-photos-to-USB how-to with no computer: the results are generic phone-to-USB guides that never mention X, save-to-phone guides that stop at the phone, and downloader sites that take a post link and hand you a file, with no USB step at all.

The confusion first: X is not a photo library — and Save is not a bookmark

X sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name. The app is X now, and it was Twitter before — same app, same account, same photos, new name and new icon. The guides written before the rename say Twitter, the guides written after it say X, and they describe the same menu: tap the photo, tap the three dots, tap Save. If a guide says Twitter and your phone says X, you are reading the right guide. The folder on your phone still says Twitter, too — the app saves into a folder called Twitter, and the rename did not rename the folder. Do not go looking for a folder called X. The folder is Twitter, and the rest of this article calls the app X and the folder Twitter, because that is what each one is actually called on your phone.

The second confusion is X versus a photo library, and it is the one that decides whether the job works at all. X is a social feed. Photos live in posts, in timelines, in accounts, in direct messages — and they live on X's servers, not on your phone. A photo library — Google Photos, iCloud Photos, the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and album, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. X is not that. It shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your X account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into X either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no social account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of X and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a save. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The third confusion hides inside X itself, between the two ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. A bookmark saves a link. Tap the bookmark icon on a post and the post is saved to your X account, in a private list you can scroll later. It is the fastest option, and it is not a download: you are saving a link, not the photo. You cannot send it in a chat as a file, you cannot copy it to a drive, and if the author deletes the post or their account, it disappears from your bookmarks too — the bookmark points at the post, and the post is gone. Two independent guides say it in the same words: bookmarks keep a link, which breaks if the post is deleted; a bookmark saves a reference to the post in your account, and it does not create a file on your phone. Liking a post is even less: a like is a signal to the algorithm, not a save of any kind, and it writes nothing anywhere. The only tap that writes a file is Save, in the photo viewer's three-dot menu — or a long press on the full-screen photo, which opens the same choice. If you have been bookmarking photos and wondering why none of them are on your phone, this is why: you have been saving links, and links are not files.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on X's servers. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The save is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the save is a step you have to take yourself, one photo at a time, because X offers no save-them-all button and no auto-save setting. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the save-one-and-check rule, because for X the date problem is a save problem: the file is written when you save it, and the day you saved it is the day it sorts by.

The wall: X saves nothing by itself — the save is yours to do

X does not put timeline photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the save describes a manual step — tap the photo, open the menu, tap Save — because the manual step is the only step there is. The only media settings X offers sit under Settings and privacy, then Data usage: Data Saver, which loads lower-resolution images and stops videos from playing on their own; video autoplay; and image quality on mobile data versus Wi-Fi. None of them saves anything. Data Saver is about how much data the timeline uses, not about where photos go, and turning it on makes the saved copy smaller, not automatic — it loads the lower-resolution image, and the photo you save is the photo you loaded. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache, which saves data when you scroll back over posts you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is X's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's cache is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you save it, and you save it one photo at a time.

The save itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because the guides agree on it in the same words across five independent sources. Tap the photo attached to a post to open it full-screen. On the image viewer, tap the three-dot menu in the top-right corner. From the options that appear, tap Save. You will see a message at the bottom of the screen that reads "Image saved". That is candid.technology's version, and dignited, itgeared, and useqwitter give the same three steps in the same order — tap, three dots, Save — and the same destination: you will find your saved photo in your Gallery under the Twitter folder. Two of them add the same detail independently: by default, a new folder called Twitter is created specifically for storing images from the platform. If the three-dot menu looks bare, or the save option does not appear, press and hold the full-screen photo instead: the save option appears in the sheet that slides up, and the same Save tap applies. That long-press route is the one sekin.in documents for current app versions, and it is the fallback this article gives when the menu does not show Save — try the menu first, then the long press, then the browser route in the fixes below.

There is one more thing the save does not let you do, and it is worth knowing before you save a hundred photos, because it decides how you organise them afterwards. On Android, you cannot choose where the photo lands. Candid.technology states it plainly: while iOS has the option to choose a custom location for pictures from X, you can only save a picture from X's Android app to the default folder assigned to the app in your device storage. The photo goes to the Twitter folder, every time, with no picker, no prompt, and no setting to change it. That is a limit, and it is also a convenience: every X photo you save is in the same folder, and the folder is the thing you point a backup at. Do not waste an evening looking for a save-location setting in the X app. There is not one, on Android, and the guides that describe one are describing the iPhone app.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the save sometimes silently does nothing. The X app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the Save tap still shows "Image saved" on some versions and writes nothing — or shows nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the toast: after the first save, open the Gallery and look for the Twitter folder. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every save after it works the same way. If it is not there, open the phone's Settings, go to Apps, find X, open Permissions, and grant Photos and videos — then save the photo again. Do not save fifty photos and check at the end. Save one, check the folder, then save the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the X app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the X account, because the photos live in that account's timeline and messages. Enough free space on the phone to hold the saves: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, save in batches and copy each batch to the drive before saving the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the save steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a save guide.

One more check, on the X side: open the post and confirm the photo is actually there. If the photo is in a post you can open, it is a photo you can save. If the post is from a protected account you do not follow, or it was deleted, or it is age-restricted or region-unavailable, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be saved, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. Do not save first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then tap Save. A save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Save one photo from the timeline, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the X app on your Android phone and sign in. Scroll through your timeline — or open the account that posted the photo, and check under the Media tab, which lists the account's photos in one place — and tap the photo you want, so it opens full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a save of a thumbnail is a save of a thumbnail. Tap the three-dot menu in the top-right corner and tap Save. The app shows "Image saved" at the bottom of the screen. Then open the Gallery and look for the Twitter folder: the photo should be there, at full size, and the size should be plausible for a photo, not a few kilobytes.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the Twitter folder is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo in the Twitter folder is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of the Twitter folder, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where X put it. The default run, described in the next section but one, reads the media index with no folder filter, and the Twitter folder is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the post on X if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is in the Twitter folder, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the saved X photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the saved batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the thread of twenty from a trip, the photos from one account's Media tab. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand three-tap saves is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole account, use Method 3, which gets the account's photos in one archive instead of one save at a time. For a whole X history, the archive is the only honest route. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Save option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, update the X app: the menu wording varies between app versions, and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Second, try the long press: open the photo full-screen, press and hold it, and take the save option from the sheet that slides up. Third, open the post in Chrome instead of the app: open the post at x.com in the browser, tap the picture to enlarge it, press and hold it, and choose the download or save-image option the browser offers — the file lands in Downloads, and the gallery shows it from there. Fourth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, X, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and save again. One of those four fixes the save in almost every case, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, in the Twitter folder, before you save the next one.

Method 2: Save a photo from a DM — the same gesture, and the video limit

A photo someone sent you in a direct message saves with the same gesture as a timeline photo, and the guides agree on that too. Tap the Messages icon — the envelope — and open the conversation. Tap the photo in the DM to open it full-screen. Tap the three-dot menu in the top-right corner and tap Save. The image is saved to your phone's gallery, in the same Twitter folder as a timeline save. That is gadgetstouse's version, tested on the DM flow itself: tap the image in the DM, tap the three-dotted icon at the top right, click Save, and the image is saved to the phone gallery. The newer guides describe the same flow with the labels left open — look for Save, Download, or Share, and if you see Share, choose the phone's option to save the item to Photos, Gallery, or Files — because the exact labels differ between app versions, but the gesture is the same: open it full-screen, find the save control, tap it, and check the Gallery.

Two things are worth knowing before you save a DM photo, and both are about the message, not the menu. The first is that X does not tell the sender. X does not notify anyone when you save, download, or screenshot a picture sent in a DM: you can press and hold, download, or screenshot the image, and the person who sent it will not be told. There is no such feature, and nothing has been announced publicly that suggests one is coming. That is a fact about the app, not a permission: a private image is still private, and the right thing is to get the sender's consent before saving or sharing it, even though the app does not stop you. One guide puts it as a plain privacy note — DM images are private, always get the sender's consent before saving or sharing — and this article repeats it, because the absence of a notification is not the presence of a permission.

The second is the video limit, and it is a hard one. There is no official way to save a video shared in a DM. Gadgetstouse tested the DM flow and says so directly: the Save button trick works for a photo, and it does not work for videos — and the third-party extensions they tried for the web did not work either. The workaround the guides name is a screen recording: record the phone's screen while the video plays, then trim the start and the end and crop out the interface. It works, and the result is lower quality than the original, with the interface cropped out of the frame — a copy of a playing video, not the video file. The other workaround is the archive in Method 3: X says an archive can include Direct Messages and the photos, videos, and GIFs attached to them, so a video in a DM you want to keep is an archive job, not a save job. Do not spend the evening hunting for a save button on a DM video. There is not one, and the guides that tested it say so.

The same video limit applies, in a different form, to the timeline. A GIF on X is a short looping video file, not a photo, so the photo gesture does not apply to it — the save that works on a still image does nothing useful on a GIF. And a video in a post has no universal one-tap save button either: X's own video help says Premium subscribers can download videos from certain posts, and the eligibility rules the guides quote are narrow — an eligible paid X subscription, a video posted on or after July 25, 2023, and an uploader who allowed downloads when publishing. If the video's three-dot menu shows Download video, take it; if it does not, the post is not eligible, and the download is not available to you. This article covers photos. Videos are a different job, with a different gate, and the photo save does not open it.

Method 3: A whole thread or account — the Media tab, and the archive for bulk

There is no save-them-all button in the X app, and that is not a guess: every guide that describes the save describes one photo at a time, and none of them describes a batch command, a select-all, or a download-an-account button. The workaround for a thread or an account is to make the finding fast, not the saving: open the account that posted the photos and check under the Media tab, which lists the account's photos in one place instead of scattered through the timeline. Then save them one by one, in order, with the same three taps — open full-screen, three dots, Save — and check the Twitter folder after the first few, so a permission or menu problem shows up on photo three, not photo three hundred.

For a whole account, or a whole history of DMs, the honest route is the archive, and it is X's own route, not a third-party tool. Open Settings and privacy, choose Your account, select Download an archive of your data, confirm your password, and choose Request archive. When X sends an email or an in-app notification, download the archive and open the media files it includes. X says an archive can include media attached to posts, Direct Messages, and Moments, along with the rest of your account data — so the archive is the route that reaches the DM videos Method 2 cannot save, and the photos from an account you have already lost access to, in the sense that the archive is yours once it is downloaded. The limits are worth stating plainly: the archive is for your own account — it is not a general method for downloading another person's photos, and it will not contain every deleted or unavailable item. It is also slow: the archive is prepared on X's side, and the email or notification arrives when it is ready, not when you ask. Request it, wait for the notification, download it, and then back up the files it contains — and unzip it first if it arrives as a ZIP, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the Twitter folder, select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

One more thing about the archive, because it changes how you should think about the batch. Downloading the archive writes a set of files through the browser, with no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-download, and no verification that every file actually arrived. It is the right route for a whole account, and it is the wrong route for a handful of photos, because a handful is three taps each in the app and an archive is a wait for an email. For ten photos, save them in the app. For a thousand, request the archive. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Twitter folder — or the archive's folders — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a saved X photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a saved photo in the Twitter folder copies with the rest of the library. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the Twitter folder in shared storage.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: save the photo, open the gallery, and look for it in the Twitter folder. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in the Twitter folder: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into X. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask X for anything. A photo still on X's servers is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on X's servers is invisible to the file manager. The save is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at X; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up X photos without saving them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Twitter folder came from X, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the X route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the save that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the X photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the Twitter folder and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Twitter folder yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on this exact folder — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a saved photo shows the save date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo saved from X shows the day you saved it, not the day it was posted — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a saved file is the save time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a saved X photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you saved it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For X, the taken date almost certainly did not survive, and the reason is the quality section below: X serves you its own copy of the photo, resized and re-encoded for the timeline, and a re-encode is the step most likely to rewrite or drop the metadata. This article states that as the expected case, not as a certainty, because no source this run tested the taken date after an in-app save — but the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app: the file is written by the save, at save time, and its modified date is the save date. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: save one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its posted date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you save a hundred of them.

There is one way in which X is better than the other clouds on the date, and it is worth stating fairly. No source this run says X rewrites the taken date when the photo is uploaded — X stores the upload and serves a copy of it, and the upload is not the step that loses the date. That is the opposite of OneDrive, where Microsoft's own support forum says the taken date can be replaced by the upload date, especially by camera upload or a bulk upload, before you ever tap Save. For X the risk is at the save, not at the upload, and the risk is a property of the copy X serves, not a choice you make — there is no Unmodified Originals option in the X app, and no format choice before you tap Save. The date you get is the save date, and the defence is to know it before you save the batch.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. X names the saved file itself, and two saves of the same photo — or two photos X named the same way — can arrive in the Twitter folder under the same name, so the second save either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the post on X if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to save a batch and copy it to the drive before saving the next, or to rename the files in the Twitter folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to save.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Save one account's photos, back up the Twitter folder, clear it, save the next account's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the accounts in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the saved copy is X's copy, not the original

A photo saved from X is not the photographer's original file. It is the copy X serves for the timeline, and the difference is measurable. One guide measured it on a real file, checked on October 6, 2026: the default save came back at 1200 by 675 pixels, and the original X holds came back at 1920 by 1080. Same picture, different file — the saved copy is smaller, because X resizes the upload into renditions that fit the view, and the save hands you the rendition that fits. The saved copy is a real photo, and it is good enough for the gallery, the drive, and a print at ordinary sizes. It is not the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app Save is the wrong tool, and this article says so instead of letting you discover it on the drive.

The route to the original is a browser trick, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the only route that gets the full file — and it is a browser route, not an app route. Open the photo full-screen on x.com in a desktop browser, open the image in its own tab so the address bar shows a pbs.twimg.com address, and change the end of the address to ?format=jpg&name=orig, then save the image that loads. On the file the guide checked, the large rendition and the original returned the same bytes — which happens whenever the upload was no bigger than X's large rendition — so the trick does not always buy you pixels, and on a phone it is a fiddly address-bar edit for a gain that is often zero. The honest advice is to use the trick only when the photo matters at full size, and to use the in-app Save for everything else: the in-app Save is three taps, and the browser trick is a URL edit per photo. Use each one for the job it fits.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: save one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the saved copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before saving — X's Data usage settings hold the image-quality controls, and Data Saver loads the lower-resolution image, so turn Data Saver off and wait for the photo to load crisp before you tap Save. A save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the saved file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the save is worth doing at all. A photo saved from X is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the size: the saved copy is X's resized copy, and no backup step will put the missing pixels back. Save the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. If it is not, take the browser route for that one photo, and save the rest in the app.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest save, a middle one, and the most recent save. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Twitter folder on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the Twitter folder holds the photos plus whatever else X saved there — but the photo count should match the number you saved, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you saved, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second save overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the Twitter folder before the backup ever saw it. Save one batch at a time and copy each batch before saving the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you saved, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the X batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a saved photo shows the save date, not the posted date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the save, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, save that photo again from the browser at full size, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in X's copy, not at upload — and no save route will recover it, because the file X holds is the original, and the file you saved is the copy.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your X login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The public-link downloader sites need no sign-in: copy the post link, paste it in, and save the file — that is the whole flow, and the guides that cover them say so plainly. A tool that asks for your X password to download a public photo is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours. The in-app Save needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not bookmark photos and expect them on the drive. A bookmark is a link inside your X account, not a file, and a bookmarked post the author deletes is a bookmark that points at nothing. Bookmark the post to read it later, and save the photo to keep it. They are two different taps for two different jobs, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing in the Twitter folder — because the photos were never saved, only bookmarked.

Do not save a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand three-tap saves is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Save the handful you actually want in the app, and request the archive for the account. The archive is slow and the app is fast, and each one is the right route for the job it fits.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the cloud as the only copy. X is a feed, not a backup: posts can be deleted, accounts can be closed or protected, and shares can be revoked, and any one of those turns a photo on X into a photo that is gone. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the feed is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
