Why lending your phone is the deadline

Lending a phone is not a reminder to back up — it is a deadline, and the deadline is before you hand the phone over, not when it comes back:

  • The borrower can delete a photo by accident. A tap on the wrong button, a swipe in the gallery, a free-up-space prompt that clears photos the phone thought were already backed up — and a deletion has no undo. Photos that live only on the phone are gone with it. The borrower does not have to mean it: an accident deletes just as well as a choice.
  • The borrower can look. A phone handed over unlocked, with the photos app one tap away, is a phone someone can scroll through. That is not malice — it is curiosity, or a bored minute, or the wrong app opened by mistake. The backup does not stop it: the backup protects the photos from being lost, it does not keep anyone out of them.
  • The phone leaves your hands. Lending is not selling and it is not repair — the phone comes back, and that is exactly why the risks are smaller and easier to miss. A shop wipe is dramatic enough to plan for; a borrower's accidental tap is not, and it is the one that actually happens.
  • The handover is a hard deadline. Once the phone is in someone else's hand, the copy is over — the phone is either backed up or it is not. A copy still running when the borrower walks off is not a backup, because the photos that never copied are still only on the phone, and after a deletion there is no undo.
  • The borrower is the wrong place to start. The borrower has their own errand, their own time pressure, and no reason to wait for your copy — and one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. The lend is where a backup gets interrupted, not where it gets done.
  • Home is the right place. Start the copy at home, with power and time to let it finish, check a few photos on the drive, and put the drive somewhere safe — then the lend is just a lend, and the backup is already done.
  • If the phone is already lent out, the backup is already too late. After the phone is handed over, the photos on it are out of your reach until it comes back — and if it comes back with photos deleted, they are out of reach for good. The fix is a copy that finishes before the handover, oldest-first, verified on the drive, and kept at home.
  • The drive and the phone travel apart. The drive stays at home and the phone goes to the borrower — two copies in two places — because a drive handed over with the phone is a copy you no longer hold, and one lost bag takes both if they travel together.

None of this is fixed by backing up after the phone comes back — after a photo is deleted with no copy anywhere else, it is gone, and the backup is already too late. The fix is a copy that finishes before the handover, oldest-first, verified on the drive, and kept at home while the phone is away.

What you need

  • An Android phone running Android 7.0 or later that still works normally, with a dry, undamaged USB port — SnapDisk needs minSdk 24 in the repo. Never plug anything into a damaged port: a bent, loose, or wet port can short the board, and power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos. A dead phone is a repair-lab job only — no software route. On an iPhone, use Method 2: the Files-app copy has no version floor, and SnapDisk is Android only.
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. For a whole library, a portable SSD holds more — but a portable SSD may need a powered hub, because the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • A charged phone, or a powered hub — one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. Charge first, then plug the drive in — or use a powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do it at home before the handover, not at the borrower's door, and do not delete from the phone until the check is done.
  • No Wi-Fi needed — the drive copy runs entirely offline, and SnapDisk has no internet permission at all. Wi-Fi matters only if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the phone will keep filling it — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job, one evening at home. The phone's photos need only the drive; the charger needs to be done before the drive goes in.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive, and it needs no Wi-Fi at all. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start. Two honest limits first: the copy has to finish and be checked before you hand the phone over — a copy still running when the borrower walks off is not a backup — and the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-lending run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified. And one plain fact: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes and it never locks — the backup protects the photos, it does not keep the borrower out of them.

  1. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos on the phone are the ones a borrower's accidental tap puts most at risk.
  2. Charge the phone first. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — so charge to full, then unplug the charger and plug the drive in. A powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  3. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  4. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  5. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  6. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  7. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  8. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it, before the handover. The backup has to complete before you hand the phone over, and a lend cannot wait for a backup. If the copy is interrupted, resume it — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  9. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  10. Unplug the drive and put it somewhere safe at home — not in the bag with the phone, and never handed to the borrower. A drive handed over with the phone is a copy you no longer hold. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A phone that has never been backed up often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size, so the next lend is covered the moment you plug the drive in. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes, and it never locks.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every phone-to-USB guide above describes — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when you want the backup done now, before the handover:

  1. Charge the phone first — one port holds the drive or the charger, not both — then plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  2. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  3. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  4. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder a borrower's tap takes with it.
  5. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big and the evening is short.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  7. Unplug the drive and put it somewhere safe at home — never in the bag with the phone, and never handed to the borrower — and only then think about deleting anything from the phone, after the check.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to go to someone else, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time, with the evening you have left.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step most people skip before a lend — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. And the check has a catch of its own: the borrower may delete a photo, so the cloud copy has to be finished and checked before the handover, not after the phone comes back:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone, and before the handover, not after. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and keep the drive at home.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones a borrower's tap takes with it.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the Wi-Fi, the account, or the subscription the borrower does not have.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, before the handover — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on Wi-Fi, an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round — and why, before a lend, the drive copy is the backup that is finished when you need it, not the upload that is still running.

Before you hand it over: the borrower, the lock, and the handover

The copy is done at home — the handover is where the backup has to survive, not where it gets made. And one thing to be plain about: the backup is only half the handover. It protects the photos from being lost. It does not keep the borrower out of them — SnapDisk copies, it never deletes and it never locks — so the lock-down is your step, on the phone itself, before the phone leaves your hand:

  • Agree what the phone is for, and for how long. A camera for an afternoon, a call, a map on a trip — say it out loud, and say when it comes back. A borrower who knows the job does not go wandering through the gallery, and a borrower who knows the return time does not keep the phone overnight.
  • Keep the drive at home. The borrower does not need your backup to use the phone, and a drive handed over with the phone is a copy you no longer hold — lost, left in a bag, or wiped by mistake. The drive stays in your keeping until the phone comes home, and then the photos come back from the drive if any were deleted.
  • Lock the borrower out of your photos with the phone's own tools. A guest profile, on phones that offer one, gives the borrower a clean phone with none of your photos, apps, or accounts — the usual route is Settings, then System, then Multiple users, then Guest, though the exact menu names vary by phone. Screen pinning locks the phone to the one app the borrower needs — the usual route is Settings, then Security, then Screen pinning — so they can use the camera or the map and nothing else. An app lock or a secure folder, on phones that have one, locks the photos app itself behind a PIN or a fingerprint. Not every phone offers all three, and the names of the menus are different on different phones — so set it up and test it before the handover, and hand the phone over locked down, not open.
  • Lock the phone before you hand it over. A screen lock is the minimum: agree the passcode or the unlock steps with the borrower at the handover if they need to unlock it themselves, and change the passcode when the phone comes home. A phone handed over unlocked, with no backup, is a phone someone can scroll through — and a phone that comes back with photos deleted takes the photos with it.
  • Decide about the SIM before the handover. The borrower does not need your SIM to take a photo or use Wi-Fi — and a SIM left in the phone can run up calls or data, or receive your messages. Pull it before the handover and keep it with you, unless the lend is for calls, in which case agree that plainly. If the photos live on the microSD card itself, back the card up too — the drive copy in Method 1 covers the phone's folders, so copy the card's folders the same way before you pull it.
  • Do not start a big copy at the handover. The borrower has their own errand and no reason to wait for your copy, and one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. A copy still running when the borrower walks off is not a backup — finished files stay on the drive and SnapDisk resumes where it stopped after you get the phone back, but anything that never copied is still only on the phone, and a borrower's tap can delete it.
  • When the phone comes home, check it before you delete anything from the drive. Open the phone, check the photos are still there or copy them back from the drive, and only then think about clearing the drive for next time. The drive is the copy that saved the photos — it stays until the phone is whole again.

The borrower does not make the backup — they test it. A drive at home, a phone locked down at the handover, and a copy that finished before the phone left your hand is the whole plan, and the handover is where you stop worrying about it, not where you start it.

What this does not do

  • Does not stop the borrower from deleting a photo. The backup copies photos — it does not keep anyone from deleting them from the phone. After the copy, the phone still needs the lock-down at the handover, and the drive still needs to stay at home.
  • Does not undo a deletion. Once a photo is deleted with no copy anywhere else, it is gone. The backup has to finish before you hand the phone over, not after it comes back with photos missing.
  • Does not lock the phone or hide your photos. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes and it never locks — there is no lock, no hidden album, and no guest mode in the app. The guest profile, screen pinning, and app lock are the phone's own tools, and they are your steps before the handover.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Back those up in Settings, Google, Backup, too — and export 2FA tokens and transit passes separately, because a phone that is lent out takes those with everything else.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com before the handover. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the lend, not after the phone comes back.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-lending run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not charge the phone while the drive is in the port. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, then plug the drive in.
  • Does not power a portable SSD from the phone alone. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. A spinning hard drive may need its own power too.
  • Does not work on a damaged port or a dead phone. Never plug anything into a damaged USB port — power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos — and a dead phone is a repair-lab job only, not a free app.
  • Does not lock the phone for you. The guest profile, screen pinning, and app lock are your steps at the handover — the backup only makes them safe to take, because the photos are already on the drive at home.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only. On an iPhone the before-lending route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive — and the same rules apply: copy at home, verify on the drive, keep the drive at home, and lock the phone down before it leaves your hand.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Starting the copy at the handover. The borrower has their own errand and no reason to wait for your copy. The copy runs at home, with time to finish and be checked.
  • Handing the phone over before the copy finishes. A copy still running when the borrower walks off is not a backup — and after a deletion, there is no undo. Copy, check, then hand it over.
  • Handing the drive to the borrower with the phone. A drive handed over is a copy you no longer hold — lost, left in a bag, or wiped by mistake. The drive stays at home, and the phone goes to the borrower.
  • Handing the phone over unlocked with no backup. Someone with an unlocked phone full of photos can scroll through them — and a phone that comes back with photos deleted takes the photos with it. Back up first, then lock it down.
  • Assuming the backup keeps the borrower out of your photos. It does not — the backup protects the photos from being lost, it does not lock the phone. The guest profile, screen pinning, or app lock is a separate phone step, and it has to be set up and tested before the handover.
  • Leaving the SIM in the phone without deciding. The borrower does not need your SIM to take a photo or use Wi-Fi, and a SIM left in the phone can run up calls or data, or receive your messages. Pull it before the handover unless the lend is for calls, and agree that plainly.
  • Plugging anything into a damaged port. A bent, loose, or wet port can short the board, and power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos. A damaged port or a dead phone is a repair-lab job only.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want — before the handover, not after the phone comes back.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder a borrower's tap takes with it.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to go to someone else, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then decide.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then decide — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it, not after the copy fails.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole library in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole library is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.
  • Assuming the general guides cover the lending moment. The Techsith, Alibaba, MobileTrans, MakeUseOf, RottenWifi, and Android Central guides back up photos to the cloud, a computer, or a drive — but none of them copies the phone's own library to USB in one tap, none of them keeps the drive at home while the phone goes to a borrower, and none of them says how to lock the phone down before the handover. The cloud and the computer are the general guides' job; the phone's library, before the lend, is this article's.

Rule of thumb: a lend is a deadline, not a reminder — copy the whole library oldest-first at home, check a few photos on the drive, keep the drive at home and the phone locked down, and hand it over knowing the backup is already done.
