Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Check your model can run SnapDisk. Every XCover from the XCover4 (2017) onward runs Android 7.0 or later — the XCover4 launched on Android 7.0, exactly the minimum — so SnapDisk installs on all eight covered models. The original XCover (Android 2.3), the XCover2 (Android 4.1) and the XCover3 (Android 4.4, upgradable to 5.1) run below Android 7.0, so none of those can run SnapDisk, and Method 4 is the route on them. If you are unsure which version your unit runs, check Settings > About phone.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. On the XCover4 use a micro-USB OTG adapter; on the other seven use a USB-C OTG adapter for a USB-A drive. A USB-C dual drive plugs straight into the seven USB-C models with no adapter. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive, and on One UI 8.0 check that the USB mode is not still "Charging phone only". On the five models whose OTG is unconfirmed — the XCover7, the XCover Pro, the XCover4s, the XCover4 and the FieldPro — this mount notification is the test: if the drive mounts, the phone hosts drives and the backup works; if it never appears, the phone may not support USB storage at all. Start with a charged battery, or a charged spare in your pocket: the drive occupies the phone's only USB port while it copies.
  3. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — XCover camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  4. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  5. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  6. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: My Files (the stock route, no app needed)

Samsung's own file manager, My Files, can copy your photos to the USB drive — this is the route Samsung's own community answer and Samsung's support pages describe: 1. Plug in the USB drive — on the XCover4 with a micro-USB OTG adapter, on the other seven with a USB-C OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive, and on One UI 8.0 check that the USB mode is not still "Charging phone only". 2. Open My Files and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until My Files says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos — and the YouTube guide above recommends Move, which is the one step in it not to follow. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option. One note: Samsung says files cannot be transferred directly to the USB drive from apps such as Gallery — use My Files instead, not Gallery.

Method 3: Samsung's own tools (Smart Switch, Google Photos, Google Drive, or a microSD card — not phone-to-USB plain files)

If you want Samsung's own route, know what each tool actually does — Samsung's own USB backup guide points at Smart Switch, and none of the stock tools writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Smart Switch is Samsung's own backup app, and it is the tool Samsung's support page points at for USB: launch Smart Switch, tap the External Storage icon, connect the drive with an OTG or Type-C adapter, tap USB Storage, select the data, and let the phone back it up. It is a real backup with no computer — but it writes a structured backup meant mainly for transfer to another Galaxy phone, not a folder of plain photo files you can open on any computer, and it does not preserve everything (draft messages, some apps and Secure Folder content among them). 2. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos. 3. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — "on Android devices the default built-in service is Google Drive. With Google Drive you have 15Gb free" — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 15 GB of free storage. 4. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. 5. A microSD card is the local option on every covered model — up to 1 TB on the XCover6 Pro, the XCover7 and the XCover5, up to 512 GB on the XCover4s and the FieldPro, up to 256 GB on the XCover4. It is a real local copy — but the card stays in the device, and opening the card anywhere else needs a card reader, and no source found states a microSD capacity for the XCover7 Pro or the XCover Pro. 6. Samsung Cloud is Samsung's own cloud backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and it needs a Samsung account and storage beyond the free tier. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

Method 4: through a computer (if you have one)

If a PC or Mac is handy, the old route still works: plug the phone into the computer with a USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. This works fine, and it is the only route above that needs a second device in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • The adapter differs by model, not by brand. The XCover4 has micro-USB, so it needs a micro-USB OTG adapter; the other seven covered models have USB-C, so a USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter. A plain USB-C charging cable is not a host connection: it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • OTG is confirmed on only three of the eight. The XCover6 Pro, the XCover5 and the XCover7 Pro have OTG confirmed in spec sources; the XCover7, the XCover Pro, the XCover4s, the XCover4 and the FieldPro have no source that labels their USB port OTG. On those five, plug in a drive and look for the mount notification — or run a USB OTG Checker app — before buying an adapter. And two sources contradict each other on the three confirmed ones: PhoneBunch says "Not Supported" for the XCover7 Pro, the XCover5 and the XCover6 Pro, while Three, neoteo, Smartprix, Kimovil, PhoneDB and hard-reset.org list OTG on them. PhoneBunch's OTG column is not trusted here, because it also denies OTG on the XCover6 Pro, which three other sources confirm.
  • Two spec details differ by source. The XCover6 Pro is USB Type-C 3.2 in GSMArena and neoteo, and USB Type-C 2.0 in a reseller listing — the port speed is unsettled, though OTG itself is not. The XCover Pro's microSD capacity is up to 512 GB in one comparison and up to 1 TB in a manual listing — so this article states no capacity for that model. The XCover6 Pro launched on Android 12 and is upgradable — one comparison says to Android 13, GSMArena says to Android 16 — but every reading clears the minimum either way.
  • One covered model sits exactly on the minimum. The XCover4 launched on Android 7.0, exactly SnapDisk's minimum: anything older cannot install the app, and the original XCover, the XCover2 and the XCover3 all run below Android 7.0.
  • One rival needs Android 9. FotoSafe needs Android 9 or later, so it cannot run on an XCover4 still on its launch Android 7.0 — only on one updated to Android 9.0.
  • No source found states a microSD capacity for the XCover7 Pro or the XCover Pro. The XCover7 Pro has a dedicated microSDXC slot and the XCover Pro takes a microSD card, but no source found states how large a card either takes — the XCover Pro's capacity is disputed between sources — so this article states no capacity for either of them.
  • No source found states where the port sits on any of the eight models. No source found states the port position on the XCover7 Pro, the XCover6 Pro, the XCover7, the XCover5, the XCover Pro, the XCover4s, the XCover4 or the FieldPro, so check your unit before buying a drive or a cable that assumes a port position.
  • Three older XCover models cannot run SnapDisk at all. The original XCover runs Android 2.3, the XCover2 runs Android 4.1, and the XCover3 runs Android 4.4, upgradable to 5.1 — all below SnapDisk's Android 7.0 minimum, so the computer copy in Method 4 is the route on them.
  • Anything below Android 7.0 cannot run SnapDisk at all. No XCover model in the sources found runs between Android 5.1 and Android 7.0 — but check Settings > About phone if you are unsure: below Android 7.0, SnapDisk cannot install, and the computer copy in Method 4 is the route.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. A USB-C dual drive plugs straight into the seven USB-C models with no adapter at all. The XCover4 needs a micro-USB OTG adapter — a USB-C adapter does not fit a micro-USB port.
  • Assuming OTG because the phone has USB-C. The XCover7, the XCover Pro, the XCover4s and the FieldPro all have USB-C, and no source found labels any of their ports OTG. Plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter — USB-C is a connector shape, not a promise the phone hosts drives.
  • Trusting one spec site's OTG column. PhoneBunch says "Not Supported" for OTG on the XCover7 Pro, the XCover5 and the XCover6 Pro — and it is wrong on the XCover6 Pro, which Three, neoteo and Kimovil all confirm has OTG. Check a second source, or just plug in a drive and look for the mount notification.
  • Assuming your XCover4 is on Android 9 because the line goes that high. The XCover4 launched on Android 7.0 and is upgradable to Android 9.0 — check Settings > About phone on your unit instead of trusting a version printed for another model. FotoSafe needs Android 9, so it cannot run on an XCover4 still on its launch version.
  • Assuming the phone charges while the drive is connected. The drive occupies the phone's only USB port while it copies, so a long backup on a low battery will stop when the battery dies. Every covered XCover has a removable battery — carry a charged spare instead of counting on charging mid-backup.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup. And Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Mistaking Smart Switch for a photo copy. Smart Switch is Samsung's own backup and its support page points at it for USB — but it writes a structured backup meant for another Galaxy phone, not a folder of plain photo files. If the drive and the phone are all you have and you want files you can open anywhere, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not Smart Switch.
  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The carrier and comparison spec pages are real documents — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port type. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the phone. The hard-reset.org XCover6 Pro page is the closest thing to an XCover-specific result, and it is a two-sentence FAQ note, not a guide.
  • Mistaking Gallery for a file manager. Samsung says files cannot be transferred directly to the USB drive from apps such as Gallery — use My Files instead. Gallery is for viewing photos, not for copying them to a drive.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — every covered XCover takes one — but the card stays in the device, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On an XCover the cloud routes are Google Photos, Google Drive and Samsung Cloud — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. My Files' Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
