What a motion photo actually is

A motion photo is one file. Google's published Motion Photo format 1.0 defines it as a single file that contains a still photographic image and a short video, which includes audio recording: the primary still image — JPEG, HEIC or AVIF — with the video file appended to it, and XMP metadata in the still image describing where the video starts. The clip is usually about three seconds, roughly a second and a half either side of the shutter. On a Pixel the camera calls it Motion or Top Shot, and the files are named MVIMG_… on older Pixels or PXL_….MP.jpg on Pixels from 2021 on. On a Samsung Galaxy the camera calls it Motion photo, the file keeps a plain date name like 20230712_154210.jpg, and the clip is marked with a MotionPhoto_Data tag before the video, followed by a SEF trailer. Two different markers, one shape: a still image with a video appended after it, inside a single .jpg file. Viewers that do not know about motion photos stop reading at the end of the JPEG image and show the still — which is why a motion photo opens as an ordinary photo on a computer, and why the clip can be lost without anyone noticing.

That single-file shape is the opposite of the iPhone's. An iPhone Live Photo is two files: the still and a separate .mov clip with the same name, and the backup has to copy both — that is the WL-191 article, where the .mov companion copies as a second file because .mov is in the video extension list. On Android there is no second file to miss. The risk runs the other way: the one file gets re-saved — by a cloud upload, a share, an editor, a converter — and the re-saved file is a still only, with the clip gone and nothing to show for it. The backup's job, then, is not to find a second file. It is to never touch the first one.

Where do they live? In DCIM/Camera, alongside the ordinary stills — a motion photo is a camera photo, saved as a .jpg in the camera folder, and every transfer guide names DCIM/Camera as where camera photos live. That decides the backup route: a DCIM pick covers them, because the folder walk recurses into the Camera subfolder and copies every .jpg in the tree. Two things to check before you start. First, whether motion is actually on: Samsung switches Motion photo off by itself in high-resolution modes such as 50MP and 200MP, and in Portrait, Panorama and Hyperlapse modes, and it can stop saving clips when storage is almost full — so if DCIM/Camera holds no motion photos, check the camera setting before assuming the backup missed them. Second, what format yours are in: the format spec allows HEIC and AVIF primaries as well as JPEG, and Pixel motion photos can be .heic in some configurations — if yours are .heic, the HEIC rule applies instead of the JPEG one, and the folder route is the certain one.

What you need

  • An Android phone with motion photos on it. On a Pixel, Motion (Top Shot) in the camera settings; on a Samsung Galaxy, Motion photo in the camera settings. Check it is actually on — Samsung turns it off in 50MP and 200MP, Portrait, Panorama and Hyperlapse modes, and when storage is almost full. Then check the files: open the Files app, look in DCIM/Camera, and look for MVIMG_… or ….MP.jpg files on a Pixel, or any recent .jpg on a Samsung — open one in Google Photos or Samsung Gallery and see whether it plays. If nothing plays, motion was off when the photos were taken, and no backup can put it back.
  • A USB drive with enough free space. Count first: a motion photo is bigger than a still alone, because each file carries a few seconds of video as well as the photo — so add up the DCIM/Camera folder in the Files app before you buy the drive, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • A drive formatted exFAT. The format reference for this article is DriveForge's exFAT-vs-FAT32-vs-NTFS guide: exFAT has no 4 GB per-file limit and is read and written by current Android phones, Windows, and Mac. FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more — a single motion photo never reaches that, but the long 4K videos sitting next to your motion photos can, and exFAT is the practical format for the whole folder. NTFS is a Windows format that most Android phones cannot open on their own — do not use it for a phone backup drive.
  • An OTG adapter if your drive is USB-A. A dual USB-C drive plugs straight in; a standard USB-A stick needs a data-capable OTG adapter. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for the drive.
  • SnapDisk v0.1.2. Not on Google Play yet — install the APK from the GitHub release page. It needs Android 7.0 or later (minSdk 24 in the repo). No account, no cloud, no ads, and no internet permission — your photos physically cannot leave the phone.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

This is the route this article is for, and the certain route is the folder route. Plug the drive into the phone and wait for Android to detect it. Open SnapDisk, tap the From picker, browse to the phone's storage, and choose DCIM — or DCIM/Camera, for motion photos only. The From picker is the system folder picker (verified in the repo as OpenDocumentTree), and on that route SnapDisk walks the folder you picked — subfolders included — and copies every file whose extension is in the list. Both .jpg and .heic are in that list, verified in the repo's PhotoQuery.kt, so a DCIM pick copies every motion photo in the tree whatever its primary format. Then pick the To folder on the drive, in the same picker, and tap Backup. The copy runs in the background: oldest-first by the date the photo was taken, with a progress notification showing percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, plus Pause and Stop.

The default run — leave From unpicked — covers JPEG motion photos too, and for most people that is enough: with no From folder picked, SnapDisk reads the phone's MediaStore Images table and the Video table with no folder filter and no MIME filter, verified in the repo as PhotoQuery.queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, called from BackupService when no From folder is picked — and a JPEG motion photo is a .jpg file, which every Android MediaStore indexes, so it is in the result set by construction. But the default route is the uncertain one for .heic motion photos: whether a given phone's MediaStore indexes .heic is device-dependent, which is the HEIC article's finding. So the rule is the same as the HEIC article's: if you do not know the format, pick the folder. The folder route is certain in every case; the default route is certain only for JPEG.

The motion survives because the copy never touches the file's bytes. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — there is no transcoding step anywhere in its copy path — and it checks the size of every file after copying, renaming the .part file to the final file only after the size verification passes. A motion photo that arrives on the drive is the same .jpg file that left the phone, clip and all. And here is the check that matters, because it is the one most people get wrong: open a copied motion photo on a computer and it shows only the still — that does not mean the motion is gone. Most computer viewers stop at the end of the JPEG image and never look for the clip. To prove the clip survived, copy one file back to the phone and open it in Google Photos or Samsung Gallery — it plays as a motion photo again — or drop it on one of the free in-browser extractor tools, which read the embedded clip and save it as an MP4 without uploading the file. Either way, check the sizes match first: a motion photo that lost its clip is smaller than the phone original by the size of the video.

Two honest limits on this route. First, there is no motion-only mode on the default run: it copies the whole library, oldest-first, motion photos mixed in with stills in date order. If you want motion photos only, that is what the DCIM/Camera pick is for. Second, screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified: SnapDisk copies as a foreground service — the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no motion-photo run has ever been tested, so this article says so instead of promising it. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it. On Android 15 and later a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours, so a very long copy can be paused by Android itself, and opening the app resets the timer (WL-34).

One fair comparison, because the search surfaced a rival that does part of this right: VaultDrive is on Google Play today, it shows the size before you start, it has backup reminders, folders by date or by album, an App Lock, and incremental backups on Pro — and its Live Photos section promises both parts of an iPhone Live Photo, saved side by side. What it does not promise, on its page, is anything about Android motion photos at all: its Android section lists the OTG route, the background copy, and the progress notification, with no motion-photo line. If those features are what you want — a size preview before you start, reminders, folders by date — VaultDrive is the honest pick, at $19.99 once for Pro Lifetime against SnapDisk's $9.99 once. What SnapDisk adds is the oldest-first order across the whole library, the detailed progress notification with Pause and Stop, per-file size verification with .part resume, a free tier of 5 GB per run against VaultDrive's 200 files or 2 GB per backup, and this article's rule: the folder route that provably keeps the clip, whatever the format.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you would rather not install anything, the Files app does the same copy by hand — and for motion photos it has one advantage: you see exactly which files you selected. Plug the drive in, open Files by Google (or your phone's file manager), browse to DCIM/Camera, long-press one motion photo, tap the rest, tap Copy, navigate to the USB drive, and tap Paste. Check the drive afterwards: compare the sizes against the phone, and copy one file back to the phone and open it in the Gallery to confirm it still plays.

Three warnings specific to motion photos on this route. First, copy — do not share. Sharing a motion photo through WhatsApp or a social app hands the file to that app, and most of them keep only the still when you share, edit or upload a motion photo — the clip is stripped at the share, and the file that arrives is a still only. Copy in the Files app moves the file itself; Share hands it to another app, which may hand you back a still. Second, do not edit and re-save the original. An editor that exports a fresh still strips the clip — edit a copy, and keep the original file intact. Third, do not use Move. The guides that suggest Move are telling you to delete the originals as the copy goes — and a Move that is interrupted halfway leaves some motion photos on the drive, some on the phone, and no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete — that order is what keeps your motion photos safe.

What you give up is everything SnapDisk automates. There is no oldest-first order — you copy in whatever order you selected. There is no progress notification with speed and time left, no Pause and Stop, and no resume: if the drive is unplugged mid-copy, you start that file again and work out yourself which files already arrived. There is no per-file size verification, and no skipped-file list — a file that does not fit, or a copy that silently stops, fails quietly. For a dozen motion photos that is fine. For a library of them, the manual route is an afternoon of tapping and checking, and the one-tap route is the honest recommendation. The manual route is also the fallback for the photos MediaStore does not index: the default run reads the MediaStore Images table with no format filter, so a .heic motion photo is included like any .jpg on a phone whose MediaStore indexes .heic — but whether a given phone's MediaStore indexes .heic is device-dependent, the HEIC article's finding, so on a phone that does not index it the folder route is the fallback; and a photo in a folder MediaStore skips, for example a folder containing a .nomedia file, never reaches the default run either, and the way to copy it is to select it by hand in DCIM/Camera and copy it the same way.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

Before you trust the cloud with motion photos, check what it actually holds. Google Photos backs up the camera folder, and a motion photo uploaded at Original quality keeps its clip — the extractor sources say motion photos downloaded in original quality keep their video. But the default is not Original quality. Google's own help page says Storage saver uploads "may be compressed into a different image format, like a .jpg" — and a motion photo re-saved as a plain JPEG is a still only, the clip gone. The extractor sources say the same thing in plainer words: Google Photos in storage-saver quality usually removes the clip. So the cloud copy of a motion photo is only as good as the quality setting it was uploaded at, and the setting is one tap in Photos settings — easy to have wrong for years without noticing, because the still looks identical.

One more cloud detail, because it confuses people: a motion photo downloaded from Google Photos in original quality sometimes arrives as a ZIP with a separate MP4 already extracted. That is Google splitting the file for you on download, not a second file you missed — the clip was inside the .jpg all along, and the ZIP is how Google hands it back. It is not a reason to worry, and it is not a reason to trust the cloud copy either: the ZIP proves the clip survived that one download, not that every upload kept it.

The cloud is not the enemy — it is a fine second copy once the originals are safe on a drive you hold. The order matters: copy to the USB drive first, verify one motion photo still plays, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. And never delete the phone originals on the strength of a cloud badge alone. Check the drive copy, then delete — that order is what keeps your motion photos safe.

The 5 GB free cap, and why motion photos feel it

SnapDisk's free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — verified in the repo as FREE_BACKUP_BYTES_LIMIT, 5 GiB — and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. For motion photos, 5 GB does not go as far as it does for stills: every motion photo is a still plus a few seconds of video, so each file is bigger than the still alone, and a library of them adds up faster than a library of stills. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (product id pro_unlock, verified in the repo's billing code) that auto-starts the backup when a disk is connected and removes the size limit, so a whole season copies in one run.

The cap is per run, not per library, and that distinction is what matters here: the free version can finish any motion-photo library if you run it again, because files already copied are never copied twice. The count to do before you start is the library size: open the Files app, look at the DCIM/Camera folder size, divide by 5 GB, and that is how many free runs the library needs. Do the arithmetic before you plug the drive in, not halfway through the copy.

One more size note: a file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped with its reason and the job continues — verified in the repo's BackupService. A single motion photo is never 4 GB or more, so this never fires for a .jpg. What it does catch is the long 4K video that sits next to your motion photos in the same folder — that file is skipped and listed, the motion photos around it copy normally, and the fix is the same exFAT format recommended above.

What this does not do

  • It does not convert your motion photos — to anything. SnapDisk copies the .jpg exactly as it is. Converting it to a plain JPEG, or to any other format, strips the clip — and that is your editor's or your computer's job, on a copy, never on the only original. The backup's job is to keep the original intact.
  • It does not extract the clip to a separate .mp4. The clip stays inside the .jpg, the way the phone saved it. If you want the video as its own file, that is a separate tool's job: the free in-browser extractors read the embedded clip and save it as an MP4 without uploading the file. SnapDisk keeps the original; extraction is what you do with a copy.
  • It does not copy motion photos only on the default run. The default run copies the whole library, oldest-first, motion photos mixed in with stills in date order. Motion photos only is the DCIM/Camera pick in Method 1.
  • It does not keep the motion if you edit and re-save. An editor that exports a fresh still strips the clip. Edit a copy, and keep the backed-up original intact.
  • It does not read or index your motion photos. There is no clip extraction and no search inside the videos — SnapDisk copies files, and finding one motion photo among a thousand .jpg files is your file manager's job, on the drive, after the copy.
  • It does not back up anything but photos and videos. No app data, no messages, no system state — and no browser tabs, notes, or clipboard history. Those live in the app's own storage, not in the photo folders, and they need their own backup.
  • It does not delete anything. SnapDisk only copies — the only delete calls in its source touch .part files, never your photos — so freeing space is a two-step flow you do yourself: copy to the drive, copy one motion photo back to the phone and check it still plays, then delete the originals from your phone. That flow is covered step by step in How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive.
  • It does not promise the copy keeps running with the screen off under battery saver. No wake lock, no battery-saver handling in the repo, no motion-photo run ever tested — stated plainly above, not promised. Keep the phone awake for the run.
  • It does not restore or view your photos. There is no in-app viewer and no restore feature — you open the copied .jpg files in a viewer or on your computer yourself. The SnapDisk vs VaultDrive comparison covers what the rivals add here, and what they charge for it.

What to avoid

  • Letting Storage saver strip the clip. Google Photos Storage saver uploads "may be compressed into a different image format, like a .jpg" — Google's own words — and storage-saver quality usually removes the clip. Copy to the drive first, at original quality.
  • Sharing a motion photo when you meant to copy it. Most apps keep only the still when you share, edit or upload a motion photo. Copy in the Files app; Share hands the file to another app.
  • Editing and re-saving the original. An editor that exports a fresh still strips the clip. Edit a copy, never the only original.
  • Assuming the still-only view on a computer means the motion is gone. Most computer viewers show only the still — the clip is still inside the file. Copy one file back to the phone and open it in the Gallery to prove it.
  • Assuming a .jpg is "just a photo". A motion photo is a still with a video appended inside it. Anything that re-saves the .jpg — a converter, an editor, a cloud upload at reduced quality — can drop the clip without changing how the still looks.
  • Converting a motion photo to "clean it up". Any converter that re-encodes the still drops the clip. If a file must be converted, convert a copy, and keep the original.
  • Assuming the default run covers .heic motion photos. MediaStore indexing of .heic is device-dependent. If your Pixel saves motion photos as .heic, pick DCIM as the From folder — the folder route is certain whatever the format.
  • Using Move instead of Copy in the Files app. Move deletes the originals as the copy goes — an interrupted Move leaves motion photos split between phone and drive with no record of which is which. Copy, verify, then delete.
  • Formatting the drive NTFS. Most Android phones cannot open NTFS on their own. Use exFAT — DriveForge's guide is the format reference — and keep FAT32 only for a drive an older device must read.
  • Buying a drive before counting the library. A motion photo is bigger than a still alone. Count the DCIM/Camera folder first, then buy the size, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy — and for a motion photo, the check is that one file still plays. Copy, check one file plays, then delete from the phone — never the other way round.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Unverified — no wake lock, no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  • Starting a multi-gigabyte copy on a nearly dead battery. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Searching for the wrong thing. The pages the search returns are generic transfer guides and one rival product page — none of them is a motion-photo backup guide. Search for the motion photos, not the photos, and the gap is obvious.

Rule of thumb: a motion photo is a still with a video hidden inside it — the copy has to be byte-for-byte, and the check has to prove the clip is still there. Pick DCIM as From, copy, copy one file back and watch it play, then delete. That order is what keeps your motion photos safe.
