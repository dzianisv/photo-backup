Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Check your model can run SnapDisk — and host a drive. Both covered Elo models run Android 10 or Android 14, so SnapDisk installs on both. The drive is the open question on both: no source found labels either Elo USB-C port OTG — the M50 data sheet says only "USB Type-C for display, charging and data" and the M51 support page says only "USB Type-C" — so plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter. A separate search for Elo M50 USB OTG host support returned only generic OTG explainers, no Elo result at all.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. A USB-C dual drive plugs straight into the USB-C port with no adapter; a USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive.
  3. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — handheld camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else — on an Elo handheld, the photos are the 8 MP shots from the M50's rear camera or the 13 MP shots from the M51's rear camera, plus whatever image files sit in the folders you pick — tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  4. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  5. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  6. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: the built-in file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

The built-in file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — on near-stock Android that is Files by Google, and no source found names the stock file manager on an Elo device, so the stock route is the Android one: 1. Plug in the USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. 2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Elo offers no backup tool of its own at all — its own software is EloView, a device-management platform for zero-touch deployment, kiosk lockdown and remote configuration, not a photo backup — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 5 GB of free storage by default since May 2026, with the full 15 GB available only after verifying a phone number on the account. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new handheld when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What to avoid

  • Assuming OTG because the port is USB-C. Both Elo models have a USB-C port, and no source found labels either one OTG — a USB-C port is not a promise the device hosts drives. Plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter.
  • Assuming your China-model M50 has the Play Store. The China-model M50 runs Android 10 AOSP with no Google services. Check for the Play Store on your unit and sideload the APK if it is absent.
  • Assuming your M50C has a camera. No source found states what the M50C is or how it differs from the M50. Check Settings > About phone and the camera app on your unit before assuming a camera roll exists.
  • Assuming the M51 is Samsung's. "M51" collides with Samsung's Galaxy M51 — a different brand's phone with a 64 MP quad camera. The M51 here is Elo's 6-inch rugged handheld with a 13 MP rear camera.
  • Assuming the M50 is still sold new. The M50 is end-of-life; remaining stock is best suited to expanding an existing fleet. New deployments get the M51, whose accessories do not fit the M50.
  • Assuming a microSD card is a USB backup. Both models take microSD, but a card is not a drive you can plug into any computer. If what you want is a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive, the card is a second copy, not the backup.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the handheld — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. A file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the handheld. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your handheld up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup. And Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost — and since May 2026 Google accounts get only 5 GB free by default, with the full 15 GB only after verifying a phone number.
  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a handheld-to-USB guide. The data sheets and the product pages are real documents — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device. The generic Android guides in the search results are the closest thing to a handheld-to-USB guide, and they say nothing about Elo.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a handheld-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On an Elo device the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the handheld.
