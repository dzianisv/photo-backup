Save Nextcloud photos to USB from Android

Your photos on Nextcloud live on your server, not on your phone. Download each one in the app — then find it in the gallery — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 14 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see in Nextcloud is not on your phone yet. Nextcloud is file storage that lives on a server — yours, or your provider's — not a folder on your phone: you can see the photo, open it, preview it, share its link, mark it available offline, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive except the one tap that does. The file appears only when you download it. In the Nextcloud app on Android, navigate to the file's directory, open the three-dot menu on the file you want to download, and select Download — that is Nextcloud's own user manual, word for word — and a green checkmark appears on the file's icon, marking it available offline. That is the one honest sentence in this whole job, and it carries a surprise: the file does not land in your Downloads folder. It lands in the Nextcloud app's own folder on the phone, Android/media/com.nextcloud.client, under your account — a real file in shared storage, visible in a file manager, and the manual says the download makes the file accessible to other applications via the media gallery, so check the Gallery first and the app folder second. From wherever the file landed, the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the downloaded Nextcloud photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: Nextcloud downloads nothing by itself — no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and the app's automatic feature runs the other way, sending your phone photos up to the server, not bringing server photos down to your phone; downloading is not the same as opening a photo, and a previewed photo leaves no file behind; a downloaded photo sorts by the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken, because the file is written when you download it; and removing your Nextcloud account from the app deletes the files the app had downloaded to your phone, so copy them to the drive before you remove the account. The good news, and it is real: a Nextcloud download is the file as you uploaded it — Nextcloud is file storage that keeps the file you put there, with no re-encoding and no size menu to get wrong — so what you back up is the full file; and there is no transfer quota to hit, because the files live on your own server, so the wall is the tapping, not a meter. The confusion first: Nextcloud is cloud storage — and Download is not a copy in Downloads

Nextcloud sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. Nextcloud is file storage — files and folders on a Nextcloud server, where every file is stored under your account and synced across your devices — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like a photo library, it behaves like a photo library when you open a photo in it, and it is not your library. It is storage on a server, and the photos in it are files that live there, not on your phone. A photo library on your phone — the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. Nextcloud shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Nextcloud account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Nextcloud either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no cloud account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of Nextcloud and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a download. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion Nextcloud's own design invites. Nextcloud is where people keep their photos because it is theirs — free and open-source software, running on a server they control or a provider they chose, with no storage plan to buy and no account to rent — and the account feels like an archive: your files, your folders, your shared links, all in the order you put them in, all yours, all reachable from any device. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy on a server, under that server's rules. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits in an account you stop paying attention to is a photo at risk twice over. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the server is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

The third confusion hides inside Nextcloud itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. Downloading a file copies it from the Nextcloud server to a folder on your phone that other apps can open — the Nextcloud app's own folder, Android/media/com.nextcloud.client, which the manual and the app's own users both name outright. Making a file available offline is the same tap in the Nextcloud app: the three-dot menu's Download marks the file available offline with a green checkmark — and here Nextcloud differs from the other clouds in this series, because that downloaded file is a real file in shared storage, visible in the Gallery and in a file manager, not a copy hidden inside the app. Opening a photo to look at it is thinner still: it shows you the photo from the server without downloading it first, and a viewed photo leaves no file behind at all. Sharing a file's link is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the file remaining available in the account. And Export is a fourth tap, different again: it saves a purely local copy to the Downloads folder, with no green checkmark in the app and no sync back to the server — the right tap when you want a plain copy in Downloads, and the wrong tap to confuse with Download. The only tap that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive and keeps it in the Gallery is the three-dot menu's Download — and if you have been opening photos and wondering why none of them are in the Gallery, this is why: you have been viewing photos, and viewed photos are not downloads.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on the Nextcloud server. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The download is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the download is a step you have to take yourself, because Nextcloud offers no download-them-all setting and no auto-download. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the download-one-and-check rule, because for Nextcloud the date problem is a download problem: the file is written when you download it, and the day you downloaded it is the day it sorts by. And the one thing this article will not do, which the other guides do without saying so, is invent a folder: Nextcloud names the app folder, so the folder section below is a check plus a short search, not a claim.

The wall: Nextcloud downloads nothing by itself — the download is yours to do

Nextcloud does not put your account photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the download describes a manual step — navigate to the file, open the three-dot menu, tap Download — because the manual step is the only step there is. Nextcloud's settings hold nothing about downloading to the phone automatically: the app has an Auto Upload toggle, which sends your phone photos up to Nextcloud, and no auto-download toggle, and no sync-to-device option. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache, which saves data when you scroll back over photos you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is Nextcloud's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's data is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you download it, and you download it one photo at a time — or one selection at a time, with the multi-select described below.

The download itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because Nextcloud's own user manual states it in the same words this article uses. Open the Nextcloud app. Navigate to the file's directory. Open the three-dot menu on the file you want to download. Select Download from the menu. A green checkmark will appear on the file's icon, marking the file as available offline. That is Nextcloud's own user manual, first-hand, quoted in full by a guide that links the manual page — the manual's old "latest" web address for that page no longer loads as of this run, but the steps are corroborated on Nextcloud's own forum, where users describe the same three-dot Download and the same green checkmark, and by the app's own behaviour in current versions. If the icon changes to a red X, the file is out of sync with the server — download it again to fetch the latest version. The same manual page says the download makes the file accessible to other applications via the media gallery, and that local and downloaded Nextcloud files are directly accessible from the phone's media gallery. In some app versions the same step may be reached by tap-and-hold instead of the three-dot menu — one forum thread describes marking one or several files and choosing Download from the toolbar — so if the three-dot menu shows no Download, press and hold the file and look for Download in the toolbar instead. The same steps cover a file in any folder you can open. A shared folder is a download source like any other: open the folder, and download from there — though a file shared with you for viewing is not automatically a file you can download, so check what the share allows before assuming the selection is yours to take.

There is one more thing the download does not let you choose, and it is worth knowing before you download a hundred photos, because it decides where the files land. Nextcloud names the destination outright: the app's own folder, Android/media/com.nextcloud.client, under your account — internal storage, Android, media, com.nextcloud.client — and that is the folder to check first, in the Files app, if the photo is not in the Gallery. The manual and the app's own users agree on the path: users on Nextcloud's own forum report finding their downloaded files at /storage/emulated/0/Android/media/com.nextcloud.client, visible in a file browser. That folder is the one named thing in this whole job: Nextcloud names it, so the search is short — check the Gallery first, then the app folder in the Files app — and the folder-check section below walks the whole search in order. Do not waste an evening looking for the download in the Downloads folder. A Download in Nextcloud does not go to Downloads. Export does that, and Export is a different tap, covered below.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the download sometimes silently does nothing. The Nextcloud app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the download tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first download, open the Gallery and look for the photo. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every download after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission in Settings, Apps, Nextcloud, Permissions, Photos and videos, and download the photo again. Do not download fifty photos and check at the end. Download one, check the folder, then download the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the Nextcloud app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the Nextcloud account, because the photos live in that account, and a shared link is the one case where no sign-in is needed at all. The app itself is free, on Google Play and on F-Droid, and current — version 34.0.1 in June 2026, version 35.0.1 in October 2026 — so update it before you start, because the menu wording varies between app versions. Enough free space on the phone to hold the downloads: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, download in batches and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the download steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a download guide.

One more check, on the Nextcloud side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can download. If the photo is in a folder you can open, it is a photo you can try to download. If it is from a link that expired, or the file was deleted, or it sits in a shared folder you were removed from, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be downloaded, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. And if the Download option is missing entirely, that is usually one of two things, not a bug: the photo is already on the device in some form, or the photo is shared with you in a way that does not include downloading — the next sections cover both. Do not download first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then tap the three-dot menu. A download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Download one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Nextcloud app on your Android phone and sign in. Find the photo — in your account, in a folder, in a shared folder, or through a link — and open it full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a download of a thumbnail is a download of a thumbnail. Go back to the file list, open the three-dot menu on the file you want to download, and select Download. If you want more than one, press and hold a file to select it, then tap the others to build a selection, and use the download option in the toolbar to save them together — Nextcloud's own forum confirms that marking one or several files offers Download for the whole selection. Then find the file — and finding it is a short step, because Nextcloud names the folder. Open the Gallery first: if the photo is there, the download worked, whatever folder it sits in. If it is not in the Gallery, open Files by Google and look in the Nextcloud app folder — Internal storage, Android, media, com.nextcloud.client, then your account — and the folder-check section below walks the search in order, with the reason each place is worth checking.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the folder the file landed in is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo the Gallery shows is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of the Nextcloud folder, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where Nextcloud put it. The default run, described in the section after next, reads the media index with no folder filter, and the Nextcloud app folder is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo in Nextcloud if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is a file on the phone, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the downloaded Nextcloud photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the downloaded batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo — or one selection — at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one folder, the photos from one trip. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand press-hold-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole folder, use the Synchronize route in Method 2, which gets the folder in one step instead of one tap at a time. For a whole account, use Method 3, which works through the folders one by one instead of one selection at a time. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what was shared — Nextcloud offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Download option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the photo is already on the device: search the Gallery and the Files app for the photo before anything else — it may already be a file, and the job may already be half done. Second, check that you are logged in to the right account: the photos live in the account you signed in with, and a second Nextcloud account holds a different drive. Third, update the Nextcloud app: the menu wording varies between app versions — Download in current versions, and the tap-and-hold route in some others — and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Fourth, try the folder route instead of the photo route: open the folder, select the photos inside it, and download the selection — one forum thread gives the folder the same tap-and-hold Download as a single file. Fifth, open the photo on the web instead of the app: sign in on the Nextcloud website in Chrome, select the file, and download it from there, and the file arrives in the browser's Downloads folder. Sixth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, Nextcloud, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and download again. One of those six fixes the download in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, before you download the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the folder the download landed in — the Nextcloud app folder, or Downloads if you used Export — select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole folder — Synchronize it, or tap and hold in the app

Nextcloud is blunt about the one bulk step the app does offer, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the step every other cloud in this series offers in some form and Nextcloud offers in its own: the app synchronizes a folder, and a folder synchronize is one step on the folder itself. Navigate to the parent directory, open the three-dot menu for the directory you want to synchronize, and select Synchronize in the menu. A syncing icon will briefly appear on the file's icon, and disappear when done. That is the manual again, word for word — and it comes with the manual's own warning: this is a one-time action, and new files will not be continuously synchronized. For a folder of twenty photos it is one minute. For a folder of five hundred it is one selection plus a wait, and the wait is the download, not the tap — use the batch-size advice below, and check the folder before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

For a batch on the web, the route is the browser, and it is worth following exactly, because the web route has two traps — the ZIP and the Downloads folder — that decide whether it works on a phone at all. Sign in on the Nextcloud website in Chrome, open the folder or the drive view, select the files you want, and use the Download button that appears at the top — or the three-dot overflow menu on a single file, which offers Download. The files arrive in the browser's Downloads folder, on the phone, in Internal storage/Download. When several files or a folder are downloaded together, Nextcloud compresses them into a ZIP archive before downloading — that is the first trap, and it is a real one: SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives, so a ZIP sitting in Downloads is a file the backup will skip, and the photos inside it are still zipped. Open the ZIP in the Files app and tap Extract first, and back up the files it contains. The second trap is the folder: the web download lands in Downloads, not in the Nextcloud app folder and not in DCIM, and a reader who looks for the batch in the Gallery's Pictures folder will not find it until the media index picks the extracted files up — open one extracted photo once, which prompts the media scanner, or use the From picker route, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. One more web note, and it is an honest one: if the Download button is not visible on the web, the server administrator has disabled it — the manual says so outright — and the fix is the app route, not a setting on your phone.

The web route is also the route for the photos the app will not download, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the route for the edge cases. If a photo is in a folder someone shared with you, the web view shows the same folder, and the same Download works from there — but check each item, because sharing is per folder and per link, and a file shared with you for viewing is not automatically a file you can download: check what the share allows before assuming the selection is yours to take. If a photo is in a folder link someone sent you, the same rule holds: the link shows you the file, and the download is a separate permission the owner controls. And if the Download option is missing for a single photo in the app but present on the web, the web is the fix — the app and the web do not always agree on what is already on the device, and the web download writes a fresh file either way.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. The download is the photos you selected, not the folder as an object: the folder name, the folder order, and the folder membership do not travel with the files, so keep the folder context yourself — a dated folder on the drive, named for the Nextcloud folder, is the simplest way, and the checking section below says so again, because it is the step people skip. The ZIP is prepared by the browser, and the download arrives when it is ready, not when you ask — select, download, wait, unzip, and then back up the files it contains. Each time more photos are added to the folder, you need to download again, or the new photos will not be in the batch. And there is no quota wall to size the batch against: Nextcloud publishes no download quota, because the files live on your own server — the limit is the phone's free space and your patience, not a meter. For ten photos, download them in the app. For a whole folder, use Synchronize in the app, or the web download. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Nextcloud app folder — or the unzipped batch folder — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole account — what Nextcloud does and does not export

For a whole account, the honest answer is that Nextcloud has no one-tap whole-account export in the app, and this article says so instead of inventing one. There is no settings page that zips your entire drive, no download-all button that compiles everything you ever uploaded, and no archive that arrives by email with all your folders in it. The export unit in the app is the file or the folder: one photo at a time, one selection at a time, or one folder at a time. If you have ten folders, that is ten downloads. If you have a hundred, that is a hundred — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

The route that does exist for a whole account is the folder-by-folder download, and it is worth doing in order, because the order is what keeps it from becoming a mess. Start with the folders that matter most — the photos with no other copy anywhere — and work down the list. For each folder, synchronize it in the app or download it on the web, unzip the batch if it arrived as a ZIP, and back up the files it contains before downloading the next folder — unzip first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives. Keep each folder's files in their own dated folder on the drive, so the drive ends up with the folders in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower than a single export would be, and it is the route that exists. The app's Synchronize is the faster route for a single folder, and the web download is the route for the folders the app will not take. Use each one for the job it fits.

For the whole account at once, the route that exists is the computer: the Nextcloud desktop client syncs whole folders to a computer, where you can copy them to a USB drive like any other folder, and that is the honest boundary of this article — it is the computer route, and this article is the no-computer route, so it names the boundary instead of pretending the phone can do the same job. If you have a computer, even borrowed for an afternoon, the desktop client plus a USB drive is the fastest way to empty a Nextcloud account: install it, sign in, wait for the folders to sync, and copy them to the drive. If you do not have a computer, the phone route is the folder-by-folder download, and the honest time to budget is days, not an evening — not because of a quota, but because a hundred folders is a hundred downloads, and each one needs its own check. Do not leave the job until the account lapses: the time to take the photos out is while the account is in good standing, not after you stop paying attention to it.

Two facts about the account downloads are worth stating because they decide who they work for. First, they are for photos you can download: your own photos, and other people's photos where the sharing allows it. They do not provide an export for someone else's account, and they do not include photos you viewed but did not download — a library of views is not a library you can export. Second, the download is not locked to you: a file you download is a file, and a file can be copied, shared, and forwarded by anyone who holds it, so keep the drive somewhere safe and do not forward a batch in a group chat and assume only the people you meant will open it. If the photos need protection, the protection is the drive in your drawer, not the account on the server.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what was shared. Nextcloud offers no export for an account you do not control, no download-a-whole-drive button, and no save-them-all in the app beyond a single file or folder. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take a username or a folder link and fetch the files — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them, because the app's own Download does the job they claim to do, and a tool that asks for your Nextcloud password to download a shared folder is asking for something it does not need. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: download the photos you actually want one by one, or the whole folder where the sharing allows it, or ask the person for the files. A thousand one-by-one downloads is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a downloaded Nextcloud photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a downloaded photo copies with the rest of the library, whatever folder Nextcloud put it in — the Nextcloud app folder, Downloads, Pictures, or any other shared folder. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:23-36, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:60 takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the folder the Nextcloud download landed in.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: download the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in the Nextcloud app folder and then in Downloads: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into Nextcloud. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Nextcloud for anything. A photo still on the Nextcloud server is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on the Nextcloud server is invisible to the file manager. The download is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Nextcloud; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Nextcloud photos without downloading them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Nextcloud app folder came from Nextcloud, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/BackupEngine.kt:126. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Nextcloud route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the download that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the Nextcloud photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the folder the downloads landed in — the Nextcloud app folder, or Downloads if you used Export — and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:90-95, walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName, at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:125-128, keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/MainActivity.kt:62 and :71, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Nextcloud app folder for Nextcloud downloads yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on these exact folders — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a downloaded photo shows the download date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo downloaded from Nextcloud shows the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:47-48, and the sort order is a CASE expression, at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:56-58: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a downloaded file is the download time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a downloaded Nextcloud photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you downloaded it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For Nextcloud, the taken date has a better chance than for the other clouds, and the reason is the quality section below: a Nextcloud download is the file as uploaded, byte for byte, so the taken date recorded inside the file survives the download — the file is the same file, and the date inside it is the same date. But the date inside the file and the date the media index records are two different things, and the index is what the default run sorts by: the scanner may read the taken date out of the file and record it, or it may record the download date, and no source this run tested which one a given phone does after an in-app download. This article states download-date order as the expected case, not as a certainty, because the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app — the file is written by the download, at download time, and its modified date is the download date — while noting the one way Nextcloud differs: the original file survives, so the taken date is still inside it. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: download one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you download a hundred of them.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Nextcloud names the downloaded file itself, and two downloads of the same photo — or two photos Nextcloud named the same way — can arrive in the app folder under the same name, so the second download either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo in Nextcloud if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to download a batch and copy it to the drive before downloading the next, or to rename the files in the app folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to download.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Download one folder's photos, back up the Nextcloud app folder, clear it, download the next folder's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the folders in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the downloaded copy is the file you uploaded — and there is no quota wall

A photo downloaded from Nextcloud is the file you uploaded. That is the one place where Nextcloud is better than every photo service in this series, and it is worth stating plainly, because it changes the whole quality question. Nextcloud is file storage, not a photo service: it stores the file you uploaded, and a download returns that same file. There is no served copy, no resized version, no size menu to get wrong — the app download offers no size choice because there is no size to choose. The downloaded copy is the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app download is the right tool, and this article says so instead of sending you to the web for a quality the app already gives you.

The limit, for Nextcloud, is not the quality and not a quota — it is the tapping. Nextcloud publishes no transfer meter on downloading your own files: there is no six-hour window, no published number, no message that the transfer quota is exceeded, because there is no quota to exceed — the files live on your own server, not on a metered service. If your server is someone else's — a provider, a friend's box, a workplace instance — their rules apply instead, and a provider that meters traffic can still slow you down, so check with them if the downloads stall. But for your own server, the wall is the tapping, not a meter. Do not treat the absence of a quota as a reason to hand your account to a downloader that claims to be faster: the app's own Download does the job, and a tool that asks for your Nextcloud password to do it is asking for something it does not need.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because Nextcloud is file storage that holds videos too — and videos are the one place the batch bites hardest. The app download covers videos the same way it covers photos: the manual gives the download steps for any file, and the same three-dot menu and Download that save a photo save a video — open the video in the Nextcloud app, use the three-dot menu and Download, and the file lands on the phone. The backup covers it from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:131-134, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, at :135-137, so a downloaded photo and a downloaded video both copy with the default run. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: download the video, open the folder, and look at what arrived. If it plays, it downloaded as a video; if it arrived as a single still frame, the download caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, download it again and check what arrived before you download the next twenty. And remember the free space while you are at it: a video-heavy folder fills a phone long before a photo folder does, and a download of a video you already have on the phone is a download you did not need.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: download one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the downloaded copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before downloading — wait for the photo to load crisp before you tap the three-dot menu, because a download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the downloaded file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload. And check the metadata before you delete the server copy: a download should be checked for metadata, edits, and motion-photo packaging before the server copy is deleted — and a motion photo may arrive as a still, and the motion is gone for good if the server copy goes first.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the download is worth doing at all. A photo downloaded from Nextcloud is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the tapping: the downloaded copy is the original, and no backup step will put the missing photos back if you never downloaded them. Download the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. Back up each batch before downloading the next, so a stopped batch is a finished batch, not a lost one.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest download, a middle one, and the most recent download. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Nextcloud app folder on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the folder holds the photos plus whatever else landed there — but the photo count should match the number you downloaded, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second download overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the folder before the backup ever saw it. Download one batch at a time and copy each batch before downloading the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Nextcloud batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a downloaded photo shows the download date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the download, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, download that photo again, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in the download, not at upload — and because a Nextcloud download is the original file, a date that does not survive is worth one more check in the file itself before you give up on it.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Nextcloud login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The download needs no third party at all: the app's three-dot menu does the single photo, the folder Synchronize does the folder, the web download does the batch, and the desktop client does the account on a computer. A tool that asks for your Nextcloud password to download a shared folder is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours — the same account that holds your files, your photos, and your contacts and calendar, not just the photos you meant to download. The in-app download needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not open photos and expect them on the drive. Opening a photo shows it from the server without downloading it first, so a viewed photo leaves no file behind at all. Download the photo with the three-dot menu, check the Gallery, and then back it up. And do not confuse Download with Export: Download writes the file to the Nextcloud app folder and marks it available offline with a green checkmark, kept in sync with the server; Export writes a purely local copy to the Downloads folder, with no green checkmark and no sync. They are two different taps for two different things, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing new where they looked — because the photos were downloaded to the app folder while they were looking in Downloads, or exported to Downloads while they were looking in the Gallery. Know which tap you used, and look in the folder that tap writes to.

Do not remove the Nextcloud account from the app before you copy the downloads to the drive. Removing an account from the app also deletes the files the app had downloaded to your phone — everything stays in place in your Nextcloud, but the local copies are gone, and a backup that has not run yet has nothing left to copy. Download, copy, check, and only then touch the account. The same goes for clearing the app's data or uninstalling it: the downloaded files go with it. The drive copy is the one that survives, and it only survives if it was made first.

Do not download a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand press-hold-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Download the handful you actually want in the app, use Synchronize for a folder, and work through your own folders one by one. The app download is fast and the web download is fiddly, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the download to reach someone else's account: it is for photos you own or that were shared with download rights, and it will not contain your views either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the server as the only copy. Nextcloud is storage on a server, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can go over quota, and a server you stop paying attention to is a server you have stopped paying attention to. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the server is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
