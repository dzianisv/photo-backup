Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Check your model can run SnapDisk — and host a drive. Every covered DT Research Android model runs Android 13 or Android 14, so SnapDisk installs on all of them. The drive is the open question on all of them: no source found labels any DT Research Android USB port OTG — the DA323EP guide says only "USB 3.0 Port" and the DA381ND and DA361ND data sheets say only "1 x latest USB/charging" — so plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter. On the DT361ND, the DT381ND and the DA573LF, no source found even states the USB port types, so the connector is part of the test there too.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. Use a USB-C OTG adapter for a USB-A drive. A USB-C dual drive plugs straight in with no adapter. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. The DA381ND and the DA361ND also offer an optional 24-pin Fischer MiniMax connector with a USB 3.2 / USB 2.0 line — but that is a military connector, not a standard USB port, so do not plan a backup around it unless your unit was ordered with it and the cable to match.
  3. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — tablet camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else — on a DT Research tablet, the image files are whatever sits in the folders you pick, since only the DA381ND states a camera pair — tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  4. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  5. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  6. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: the built-in file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

The built-in file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — on near-stock Android that is Files by Google, and no source found names the stock file manager on a DT Research device, so the stock route is the Android one: 1. Plug in the USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. 2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • OTG is unconfirmed on all covered models — and on any DT Research device the drive may not attach at all. The DA323EP guide lists only a "USB 3.0 Port" and the DA381ND and DA361ND data sheets list only "1 x latest USB/charging" — no source found labels any of those ports OTG — so plug in a drive and look for the mount notification — or run a USB OTG Checker app — before buying an adapter. If nothing mounts, use the computer copy in Method 4. On the DT361ND, the DT381ND and the DA573LF, no source found even states the USB port types, so the connector is part of the test there too. The DA361ND's guide does say its USB 3.0 port is for data storage among other uses, which is the closest any DT Research source comes to saying the port hosts a drive — but it still does not label the port OTG.
  • No source found states whether the DA361ND, the DA381ND, the DT361ND, the DT381ND or the DA573LF ship Google Play. Only the DA323EP is Google Mobile Services certified, so the Play Store is present there — but the other five covered models say only "Android 13" or "Android 14", with no Google Play statement — so check for the Play Store on your unit and sideload the v0.1.2 APK if it is absent.
  • No source found states a camera megapixel count for the DA323EP, or any camera for the DA361ND, the DT361ND or the DT381ND. The DA323EP's back camera is optional with no megapixel count stated; the DA381ND states a 5 MP front camera and a 13 MP back camera; the DA573LF's sources state no camera at all. If your DT Research tablet holds no photos at all, there is nothing to back up — the backup still works the same way for any image files that are there.
  • No source found states microSD support for any covered model. What to avoid

  • Assuming OTG because the port is USB 3.0. The DA323EP has a USB 3.0 port, and no source found labels it OTG — a USB 3.0 port is not a promise the device hosts drives. Plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter.
  • Assuming your DT361ND or DT381ND has the same ports as the DA381ND. The DA381ND's port is stated — 1 x latest USB/charging, with an optional Fischer MiniMax connector — but no source found states the DT361ND's or the DT381ND's USB ports by type at all. Do not plan a backup around a port your tablet may not have.
  • Assuming your DA361ND, DA381ND, DT361ND, DT381ND or DA573LF has the Play Store. Only the DA323EP is Google Mobile Services certified. Check for the Play Store on your unit and sideload the APK if it is absent.
  • Assuming the DT311 is an Android tablet. The DT311 runs Windows 10 IoT Enterprise — the earlier work-log note that named it as an Android tablet was wrong. Same DT Research name, different operating system, and it cannot run SnapDisk. The DT301, the DT313, the DT312RP, the DT323RP, the DT323PA, the DT362DN and the DT382DN are Windows too.
  • Assuming the DT361DM runs Android. The DT361DM and the DT361SC ship Windows 10 IoT Enterprise or Android 10/12 depending on configuration — check Settings > About tablet on your unit before assuming either.
  • Assuming the DA573LF is a handheld tablet. The DA573LF is a 17.3-inch medical-cart computer that runs Android 14 — it is cart-mounted, not carried — so the photo premise on it is whatever image files sit in the folders you pick, and no source found states its USB ports or camera.
  • Assuming the DA323EP has a rear camera roll. The DA323EP's back camera is optional with no megapixel count stated in any source found, and no source found states any camera for the DA361ND, the DT361ND or the DT381ND. If your DT Research tablet holds image files, back those folders up; if it holds none, there is nothing to back up.
  • Assuming a microSD card is a second copy. No source found states microSD support for any covered DT Research Android model. Do not plan a backup around a card slot your tablet may not have.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your tablet up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup. And Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost — and since May 2026 Google accounts get only 5 GB free by default, with the full 15 GB only after verifying a phone number.
  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a tablet-to-USB guide. The data sheets and the product pages are real documents — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device. The generic Android guides in the search results are the closest thing to a tablet-to-USB guide, and they say nothing about DT Research.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a tablet-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a DT Research device the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the tablet — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. A file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the tablet. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the tablet.
