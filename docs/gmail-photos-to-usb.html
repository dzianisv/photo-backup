The wall: a Gmail photo is not on your phone until you download it — and Save to Drive is not Download

Start with the two mistakes this job turns on, because they look like the same tap and they are not. The first is the quiet one: opening a message in Gmail and seeing the photo does not put the photo on your phone. The photo you are looking at lives on Google's server — Gmail shows it to you over the network, the same way a web page shows you a picture — and until you download it there is no file in any folder for a backup app to copy. A mailbox full of photos you have opened and looked at is, from the phone's point of view, a mailbox full of nothing: the files are in Google's storage, not yours, and the day the message is deleted, the account is closed, or the sender's copy is gone, the photos go with it. If you have never downloaded an attachment, there is no Gmail folder on your phone at all, and pointing SnapDisk at a folder that does not exist copies nothing. That is the wall, and it is quieter than in the messaging-app cases: WhatsApp at least saves the photo to the phone when it arrives, and the work there is finding the folder. Gmail saves nothing on arrival — the download is a step you take, one attachment at a time, and the step is the whole first half of this article.

The second mistake is the button with the wrong name for this job. Gmail offers two actions on an attachment, and they do opposite things for a USB backup. Download creates a local file on the phone — in the Downloads folder — and that file is what SnapDisk copies to the drive. Save to Drive puts a copy in Google Drive instead: the photo ends up in your Drive account, in the cloud, reachable from anywhere with internet and from nowhere without it. It is a real copy and a useful one for sharing, but it is not a file on your phone, and no USB drive plugged into the phone can reach it. A photo you saved to Drive is still a photo that lives on Google's servers, one step removed, and the USB leg still has not happened — you would have to download it from Drive afterwards, which is a second download for a file you could have downloaded from Gmail in the first place. The guides agree on the two buttons and on which one is which, and Google's own help documents both: the Download control for a local copy, Add to Drive — Save to Drive on the phone — for the cloud copy. For this article, Download is the only button that counts, and every time this article says download, it means the local file, not the Drive copy.

Two scope facts, because they decide what this article can and cannot promise. First, the download is one attachment at a time, and there is no download-all button in the Gmail app: you open each message, tap each attachment, tap Download, and repeat. A mailbox that has collected hundreds of photos over years is a long evening of tapping, and this article does not pretend otherwise — the bulk routes are the computer routes in Method 3, which download to a computer, not to the phone, and the auto-download setting in Method 1, which covers recent messages on Wi-Fi only. Second, not every photo in a message can be downloaded the same way, and some cannot be downloaded at all. A photo sent inside the message body instead of as an attachment has no attachment card and no Download button — it needs the touch-and-hold save, which is a different step with its own section below. A photo that arrives as a Google Drive link instead of an attachment — Gmail replaces attachments over 25 MB with a Drive link — is a Drive file, and it has to be downloaded from Drive. And a message sent in confidential mode cannot be downloaded at all: Google's own help says you won't be able to copy or download the message text and attachments, and there is no route around that on the phone. A screenshot is the only copy left then — a picture of the screen, not the photo, lower quality, carrying the app chrome around it.

And a word on whose photos these are, because Gmail photos are usually yours in a way Pinterest pins are not. The photos this article covers are the ones people sent you — a parent's scan of a family photograph, a friend's pictures from a trip, a seller's photos of a thing you bought, the images that pile up in an inbox over years — and the ones you sent yourself, which is the other half of the same mailbox. Downloading them to your own drive for your own records is the job. They are also, in the plain sense, photos someone else took: a photographer's work, a family member's scan, a shop's product shot. Downloading a file does not grant permission to reuse it — so re-posting a photo someone sent you as if it were yours, or sharing it onward as your own work, is a different act, and the same courtesy applies here as anywhere: the person who sent it is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else. This article's methods copy the file to your drive; what you do with it afterwards is yours to decide, and asking is the honest step before anything leaves your phone. Only download content you have permission to keep — and this article makes no watermark-removal claim anywhere: SnapDisk copies the file exactly as Gmail saved it.

Where saved Gmail photos land — the Downloads folder, and the gallery check

This section exists because the folder is the one fact a guide can get wrong in a way that wastes an evening — and because the folder decides which SnapDisk route works. The answer is short: a photo downloaded from a Gmail attachment lands in the Downloads folder on the phone's internal storage, and you find it in the Files app under Downloads. Every independent guide agrees on that — the file lands in Downloads, visible in Files by Google or the phone's own file manager — and Google's own help says the same in its own words, that the browser saves attachments to a download folder. The path is the standard Android one, /sdcard/Download, and it is the same folder every Android download lands in, whatever app started it. That agreement is the honest centre of this article: unlike the Pinterest case, where the guides disagreed about the folder, the Gmail guides agree, and the folder is named by Gmail's own help as well as by the independent guides.

Check the gallery first anyway, because the gallery check is what proves the default run will see the photo. Open your gallery app and look for the photo — a downloaded photo that shows in the gallery is an ordinary indexed file, and the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index, so a photo the gallery shows is a photo the default run sees. If it is not in the gallery, look in the Files app under Downloads: the file is still a file on the phone, and Method 2 reaches it. Do not look in DCIM/Camera — that is where the camera puts its own photos, not where Gmail puts downloads, and a Gmail download will never be there. The one honest uncertainty in this section is whether a given phone's gallery shows Downloads photos: most gallery apps show a Downloads album, and Google Photos shows them under On this device, but the gallery is the check, not the promise — if the photo is in Downloads and not in the gallery, the default run may not see it, and Method 2 is the route. That is the same check the whole series uses, and it is stated here rather than assumed.

One more scope fact for this section, because it decides what the drive copy holds: the download is the file exactly as it was attached — byte-for-byte, not a re-encoded copy — so whatever the sender attached is what lands in Downloads. A photo emailed as an attachment keeps its original file and its metadata, including the date it was taken when the file carries it, which is why SnapDisk's oldest-first order puts it where it belongs: by the date taken when the file has one, falling back to the download date when it does not. The download does not shrink the photo, does not strip it, and does not convert it — Gmail's attachment download is a file transfer, not an image pipeline. If a downloaded photo looks smaller than the original, the sender sent the smaller file: some mail apps offer to shrink a photo before attaching it, and the small file is the one that arrived. SnapDisk copies the file exactly as Gmail saved it — byte-for-byte, no conversion, no quality change — but it cannot improve a file that arrived small.

What you need

An Android phone — Android 7.0 or later, which is SnapDisk's minimum version — with the Gmail app installed and signed in, plus one USB drive your phone can read: a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. SnapDisk itself is installed from the v0.1.2 APK — it is not on Google Play yet — so install it by allowing "install unknown apps" for the browser or Files app you download it with, which is standard Android. You need nothing else for the main route: no file manager with hidden files, no extra app, no account, no third-party downloader. The download step is inside Gmail itself, and the backup is SnapDisk's default run. Check the drive's space first: Gmail photos are small — a photo attachment is a few megabytes at most — so space is almost never the problem here, but look anyway, because a whole-library default run copies everything, not just Gmail. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit, and exFAT is the format to use for a photo drive.

Before any method, settle the one question that decides whether the phone route works at all: does your phone see the USB drive? Plug the drive in and open Settings > Connected devices or Settings > Storage — the drive should appear there. That check is the test, and it takes ten seconds. If the drive does not appear, try a different adapter first — cheap OTG adapters are the usual failure — then a different drive, and a powered hub if the drive is a spinning hard drive rather than a flash drive. And one warning up front, because it applies to every route below: do not delete anything from the phone until the USB copy is done and verified. The photos in the inbox are Google's copy, and the photos in Downloads are yours — but until the drive copy is checked, Downloads is the only copy outside Gmail, and deleting from Downloads before the check is how a photo ends up nowhere at all. The same goes for the messages themselves: do not delete the emails until the drive copy is checked, because the message is the only route back to a photo you have not downloaded yet.

Method 1: Download the photos, then back up with SnapDisk

This is the route to use, because it is the only one that joins the whole job — the Gmail photo, out of the attachment and into Downloads, onto a USB drive plugged into the phone, no computer — using only the apps already on the phone plus SnapDisk. It has two halves, and they have to happen in order: the download, which puts the photos on the phone as files, and the SnapDisk copy, which puts them on the drive. The first half is the work. The second half is the backup. Skipping the first half is the mistake this article exists to prevent: running SnapDisk's default backup with nothing downloaded copies every photo in the gallery — and zero Gmail photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no Gmail photos to copy.

  1. Download the photos from inside the Gmail app. Open the message, tap the attachment — it opens in a preview — and tap the Download icon, the down arrow. The file lands in the Downloads folder. Do it one attachment at a time: the app has no download-all button, and a message with five photos is five taps. Work through the messages that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting. If the attachment opens a preview with no Download icon, tap the three-dot menu in the preview — the download control is there on some versions. If Gmail asks for storage permission the first time, allow it, or the download cannot write anything. For an inline photo — one sent inside the message body, with no attachment card — touch and hold the photo, tap View image, tap the image again, then More, then Save: that is the inline save, not the attachment download, and it is the only route for a photo that was never attached.
  2. Check the photo is on the phone. Open your gallery app and look for the photo. If it is there, the download works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see it, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look under Downloads: the file should be there by name. If it is in neither place after a download, check that Gmail has storage permission — Settings > Apps > Gmail > Permissions, Files and media — and download one photo again, watching for the download notification that says the file has saved. A download that never completes is usually a connection problem, not a Gmail problem: the file is coming from Google's server, and a weak connection stalls it the same way it stalls any download.
  3. Turn on auto-download for the messages still to come. Gmail's Settings has a Download attachments option under your account, in Data usage: switched on, it auto-downloads attachments to recent messages over Wi-Fi. It helps only for the messages it covers — recent messages, on Wi-Fi — and it does not go back and download the attachments in old messages for you. The old ones are still one tap each. But switched on now, it means the photos people send you next week arrive as files without the tap, and the next backup picks them up without a download session first. It is the one setting in this article that makes the job smaller over time instead of bigger.
  4. Plug in the drive and run SnapDisk. Plug the USB drive into the phone. SnapDisk offers the backup in a notification — tap Backup — or open the app and tap Start. The run copies oldest-first, verifies every file by size after copying, and shows progress with Pause and Stop in the notification. The free run covers up to 5 GB, and Gmail photos are small, so one run is almost always enough for the Gmail half — though a whole-library run copies everything else too, and that is the point: the downloaded photos come along with the rest, with no folder to pick and no step to remember. Android 15 and later give a dataSync foreground service 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  5. Skip the Drive links and the confidential messages — or screenshot them. A message that carries a Google Drive link instead of an attachment is not a Gmail download at all: open the link, download the photo from Drive, and then it is a file on the phone like any other. A confidential-mode message cannot be downloaded — Google's own help says so — and no step in this article changes that. If a confidential photo matters, a screenshot is the only copy left on the phone — and a screenshot is a picture of the screen, not the photo: it is lower quality, it carries the app chrome around it, and it is not the file. Do not plan a backup around downloading what Gmail will not let you download.

Two honest limits. First, the download is not retroactive in the other direction either: downloading a photo today does not put the photos you only looked at yesterday onto the phone — a photo you opened in the inbox is still on Google's server until you download it. That is step 1's job, and it is a manual job: one tap per attachment, or the auto-download setting for the messages still to come. Second, the 5 GB free cap is per run — a Gmail album will never reach it, but a whole-library default run might, and running it again picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Third, SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the photos from Downloads after the check is always your step, never the app's — and deleting them from the inbox is Gmail's, not SnapDisk's, and it does not free the drive copy. The drive copy is the backup; the Downloads copy is the original until you decide otherwise.

Copying only some Gmail photos? If you would rather copy just a few photos than the whole library, the From folder picker still takes a folder, not single files — so copy the ones you want into their own folder first (in the file manager, or by downloading them and selecting them there), then point the picker at that folder. The picker route keeps only recognised photo and video extensions. If the drive is unplugged mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

And the check that matters for this article: after the run, compare the count against Downloads. A green backup that copied the whole library has still copied zero Gmail photos if nothing was ever downloaded from the app — which is exactly what happens when the photos were only looked at in the inbox. Open the drive and look for the Download folder by name: the default run mirrors the source tree, so the folder should be there as its own folder. If it is missing, the download is the suspect, not the run — download the photos from the app first, then run again. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — the only files it ever deletes are its own half-finished .part files — so deleting the originals after the check is always your step, never the app's.

Method 2: Point the From picker at the Downloads folder

If you would rather copy just the Gmail photos and nothing else — and the photos are already downloaded — point SnapDisk's From folder picker at the Downloads folder. Open SnapDisk, tap the From folder picker, navigate to Downloads — it sits at the top of internal storage, under its own name — and tap Backup. The same 5 GB free cap applies, and the same oldest-first order, size verification, Pause and Stop. The folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking Downloads reaches every file under it in one run.

The honest limit on this route is that it is the code-supported route, not the test-proven one — and this article says so rather than claiming a test that never ran — and the folder itself is the second honest limit. The picker is the system's folder picker, and the code has no Gmail path handling anywhere — zero matches for "gmail" in the app's source — so the navigation is yours, step by step, through folders whose names are ordinary. But no SnapDisk test has picked the Downloads folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first. Two things are in this route's favour, and they are worth stating because they are the reason the route is code-supported rather than blocked: the Downloads folder is not hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and it sits at the top of internal storage, outside Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks some app folders does not apply here at all. And the folder walk keeps only recognised photo and video extensions — a document in Downloads is simply skipped, and a message is never copied, because a message is not a photo or a video. For a whole mailbox, use Method 3 — this article is only about the photo and video files.

Method 3: Bulk Gmail backup on a PC — and why it is not a phone-to-USB job

Gmail has no bulk download of its own on the phone, and the bulk routes that exist all run off the phone — and they are worth naming honestly, because they are the first results for every bulk-download search — and none of them is this job. On Gmail on the web, one message offers a Download all attachments button that arrives as a ZIP: open the message, click it, and every attachment in that one message downloads together to the computer's Downloads folder. For the whole account, Google Takeout exports everything — messages and attachments together — as one MBOX file, to a download link or to Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, or Box. This article names both without endorsing either as a phone job: they are Google's own tools, they work, and they are not this article.

But neither of them is a phone-to-USB backup, and the difference is where the files land. The web download puts the ZIP on a computer: the files are in the browser's Downloads folder, and they still need a second copy — computer to USB drive — before they are a backup you can open anywhere, and the ZIP has to be opened first. The Takeout route puts the whole account in one MBOX file, with the attachments still inside it — not separated into photo files you can open — and it takes longer to generate, from minutes to hours depending on the size of the account. Neither route touches the phone at all. So use the bulk routes for what they are: the way to get a whole mailbox off Gmail in one sitting, for the day you need the whole mailbox at once. For plain photo files on a drive you can open anywhere, download the photos to the phone first — Method 1 — and copy them to a USB drive with SnapDisk, no computer. The two routes are not rivals; they are different shapes, and this article is about the plain-files one. And the same permission rule applies to both: only download content you have permission to keep.

Can SnapDisk read the Downloads folder directly?

This is the question the whole article turns on, and it deserves a straight answer in three parts — what the code proves it can do, what is untested, and what would prove the rest. The first part is proven, from the code at the current base and from how Android's media index works — and it is the same shape as the Pinterest and Kik cases. SnapDisk's default run reads the phone's media index — it queries the MediaStore images and videos tables, oldest-first by date taken, with no folder filter. A Gmail photo downloaded to the phone is an ordinary indexed file: it shows in the gallery, and gallery apps read the media index. The photo's presence in the gallery is the evidence the file is indexed — if the media scanner had skipped it, the gallery would not show it at all; it does show, so there is no such skip. The query is in PhotoQuery.kt, the no-folder-filter shape is the code's own design — the query call passes no selection at all — and the gallery evidence is the platform fact that closes the loop. So the default run sees downloaded Gmail photos, and it sees them with no step beyond the download. That is not an assumption: it is the code's query shape plus the gallery evidence, stated as such. And the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only — the query reads every indexed image and video on the phone, whatever folder it sits in, so the Downloads folder is covered the same way DCIM is.

The second part is untested, and this article marks it as such rather than guessing in either direction: whether SnapDisk's From folder picker reaches the Downloads folder end to end on a real phone. The picker is the system's folder picker — ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree, in MainActivity.kt — and it opens with no folder pre-selected: there is no initial-folder hint anywhere in the code, and no Gmail path handling anywhere in the app — zero matches for "gmail" in the app's source. The navigation chain is an ordinary folder — Downloads, at the top of internal storage — not dot-prefixed, so not hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and outside Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks some app folders does not apply here. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking Downloads should reach every photo under it. But no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first.

The third part is what the code does prove about the rest of the job, so the limits above are not mistaken for a wider doubt. The manifest requests the media-read permissions (READ_MEDIA_IMAGES and READ_MEDIA_VIDEO, plus the older READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE capped at Android 12), the notification permission, and foreground-service permissions — and no INTERNET permission at all, zero occurrences, so the photos physically cannot leave the device. It does not request all-files access — no MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE anywhere — which is the permission a file manager uses to browse another app's folders freely, and the absence is the reason SnapDisk cannot simply walk into the Downloads folder the way a file manager can; the folder picker is the route instead, and it needs your tap. The backup itself is the verified core: oldest-first by date taken with a date-modified fallback (PhotoQuery.kt), every file size-verified after copying (BackupEngine.kt), Pause and Stop in the notification (NotificationHelper.kt, BackupService.kt), files of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive skipped with their reason instead of stalling the job (BackupService.kt), and the free run capped at 5 GB with Pro a one-time $9.99 purchase (ProPrefs.kt). None of that changes the picker verdict — it bounds it. The backup engine is proven; the end-to-end pick of this exact folder is not yet tested.

Photos you received vs photos you sent

The route is the same for both, but the starting point is not, and it is worth saying which one you are saving before you start. Photos you received — the case this article is really about — are the ones the download governs: with a download they land in Downloads, and without it they are not on the phone at all, and the methods above are written for them. Photos you sent are different, because you usually have a better original: a photo you sent by Gmail is almost certainly still in your camera roll, in DCIM/Camera, at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file that SnapDisk's default run already covers — and the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only, so it is covered either way. Back that up instead. The copy in the sent message is the file you attached — the same file, byte-for-byte — and the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping. That matters more than it sounds, because the photos you send are often the ones you care about most: a picture of the finished work for a client, a photo of the goods before dispatch, the shot you took to show someone what something looks like. The original is the one they will ask for again. The Gmail route for a photo you sent is only needed when the original is gone — you deleted it from the camera roll, or it was a photo you took inside another app and never saved — and then the sent copy is the only one left: open the sent message, download the attachment the same way as a received photo, and the default run picks it up like any other indexed photo, or point the From picker at Downloads and copy it directly.

One related corner is worth a line, because Gmail users hit it and it looks like a shortcut that is not one: Gmail keeps its working data under Android/data, and a guide may tell you to dig in Android/data for a cached attachment. That is a different thing from downloaded photos — the cached copies are fragments keyed to the installation, not your photos in any usable sense — and Android/data is guarded by scoped storage, so a file manager may not even show it. Do not go digging in Android/data for your Gmail photos: they are not there. The photos are in Downloads once downloaded, or nowhere at all when they are not, and those are the only two places this article's methods touch.

Checking the drive after the copy

The check has two halves, and they have to happen in order: first that the download actually produced a file on the phone, then that the drive holds it. After the SnapDisk run finishes, open the drive on a computer or on the phone itself and count: the drive should hold the Gmail photos you downloaded, in a Download folder of their own — the default run mirrors the source tree, so the folder should be there by name — and a few opened at random should render fully. Open a Gmail photo itself — not just any photo on the drive — because a run that covered the rest of the library would still look complete on a random check while the one folder you cared about was missing. If the count is short, the run is not the first suspect — the download is. Check Downloads: is the photo actually there, and does it hold the photos you expected? If Downloads is empty, the photos were never downloaded — download the ones that matter from the app, and run again. If Downloads holds them and the drive does not, the run is the suspect, and the run needs repeating, not a different folder.

If the drive's free space ran out mid-run, the run stops with the full error shown; free space by moving the drive's contents to a bigger drive first, or back up to a second drive. If the run stopped at the 5 GB free cap — unlikely for a Gmail album alone, possible on a whole-library default run — run it again and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. And the check that matters most for a photo that only existed inside Gmail: confirm the drive copy before you delete anything from the phone. The copy in Downloads is an ordinary file and will survive; the copy in the inbox depends on Gmail keeping it, and a deleted message or a replaced phone ends that. The drive is a plain folder of plain files. Anyone who picks up the drive can open them. That is the price of a copy you can open anywhere, and it is worth stating before the photos leave the phone.

What to avoid

  • Assuming looking at a photo in Gmail puts it on your phone. It does not — opening the message shows you the photo from Google's server. A photo you only looked at lives on Google's server, not in any folder. A one-tap backup copies zero Gmail photos until you download them from the attachment first.
  • Assuming Save to Drive is the same as Download. It is not — Save to Drive puts a copy in Google Drive, which is the cloud, not a file on your phone. A USB drive plugged into the phone cannot reach a Drive copy. Download is the button that creates the local file; Save to Drive is the button that does not.
  • Assuming the download covers a whole mailbox. It does not — the Gmail app has no download-all button, and every attachment is one tap. A mailbox with hundreds of photos is a long job of one tap each; the bulk routes run on a computer, not on the phone.
  • Assuming every photo in a message has a Download button. It does not — a photo sent inside the message body has no attachment card and no Download button. Touch and hold it, tap View image, tap the image again, then More, then Save: that is the inline save, not the attachment download.
  • Assuming a Google Drive link is an attachment. It is not — Gmail replaces attachments over 25 MB with a Drive link, and the photo is a Drive file. Download it from Drive, not from Gmail, and do not wait for a Download button that will never appear on a link.
  • Assuming confidential-mode messages can be downloaded. They cannot — Google's own help says you won't be able to copy or download the message text and attachments. A screenshot is the only copy left then, and it is a picture of the screen, not the photo.
  • Assuming the downloaded photo lands in DCIM. It does not — Gmail downloads land in the Downloads folder, visible in the Files app. DCIM/Camera is where the camera puts its own photos. Looking in DCIM for a Gmail download is looking in the wrong folder.
  • Assuming the downloaded file is a smaller or re-encoded copy. It is not necessarily — the download is the file exactly as it was attached, byte-for-byte. If a downloaded photo looks smaller than the original, the sender sent the smaller file: some mail apps offer to shrink a photo before attaching it, and the small file is the one that arrived.
  • Assuming the default SnapDisk run covers photos you only looked at in the inbox. It cannot — proven from the code and the platform: the default run reads the media index, and a photo that was never downloaded never enters the index. A green backup copies zero Gmail photos from messages you never downloaded from.
  • Assuming the From picker route is test-proven. Untested — no SnapDisk test has picked the Downloads folder yet. The code supports it and the folder name is ordinary, but do not plan a backup around it as the only route; Method 1's download-to-gallery-first workflow is the proven one.
  • Assuming the sent copy is the one to back up. A photo you sent is usually still in DCIM/Camera at full quality. The sent copy is the same file you attached, but the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping; use the sent copy only when the original is gone.
  • Looking in Android/data for your Gmail photos. That folder holds Gmail's working data — cached fragments, not your photos — and scoped storage guards it. The photos are in Downloads once downloaded, or nowhere at all when they are not.
  • Installing a third-party Gmail downloader plugin. The 2013 plugin era is over — Gmail's own Download does the job now, and a thirteen-year-old plugin is a permission you do not need to grant. This article names the plugin only as history, and does not endorse it.
  • Using Google Takeout and thinking the USB job is done. Takeout puts the whole account in one MBOX file, with the attachments still inside it — not separated into photo files you can open — and it lands on a computer, not on a drive. The USB leg is still SnapDisk's job — Takeout replaces nothing in this article.
  • Using the web Download all attachments button and thinking the phone job is done. The button exists on Gmail on the web only, and it puts a ZIP on a computer, not a file on the phone. The ZIP has to be opened first, and the files still need a second copy to reach a USB drive.
  • Downloading someone else's photo to re-post it as your own. Downloading a file does not grant permission to reuse it. The person who sent it is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else — and this article makes no watermark-removal claim anywhere.
  • Assuming screen-off continuation under battery saver. UNVERIFIED — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for a library with long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, and a library with long 4K clips will hold one. SnapDisk skips those files and lists them — use exFAT.
  • Assuming the free 5 GB cap covers the whole library. It is per run. A library over 5 GB needs several free runs — run it again and it picks up where it stopped — or one $9.99 Pro purchase.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. An unplug mid-copy leaves a half-copied library that looks finished until you count it.
  • Checking the drive by opening any photo. Open a Gmail photo itself — not just any photo on the drive. A random check passes even when the one folder you cared about was never copied, because the photos that did copy render fine.

Rule of thumb: Download first, then back it up — looking at a photo in Gmail is looking at Google's server, Download is the step that puts a file on your phone, and Downloads is the bridge to the drive. The download is one tap per attachment; the backup after it is one tap for all of them.
