Where TikTok keeps your photos and videos

TikTok videos live inside the TikTok app, on TikTok's servers — not as files on your phone. A video you watched, liked, or favourited is not a file until you save it, and until a file exists on your phone, no backup app, SnapDisk included, has anything to copy. Three things put a file on the phone:

  • Save video in the app. Open the video in TikTok, tap Share, and tap Save video. TikTok shows Save video only when the creator allows downloads — if the button is missing, the creator has disabled downloads for that post, and TikTok provides no official download option for it. The saved copy keeps the TikTok watermark and the creator's handle, baked into the video. The file lands in your gallery — but the guides disagree about the folder underneath, and the honest thing is to say so rather than pick one and be wrong on your phone. itechguides says the downloaded copy "should appear in your Android phone's Gallery, Google Photos, or another media app, although the album and folder name vary by phone manufacturer, Android version, TikTok release, and app permissions". pixellize says on Android the clip appears "in your Gallery or Google Photos, usually under a Videos or Download folder". maketecheasier says "you will be able to find it in your Gallery app under the Camera folder on Android" and, in the same guide, "in the TikTok folder in the Gallery app". Check your gallery first — Videos, Recently added, or a TikTok album — then look in the Files app in Downloads, in DCIM, and in Pictures. The file is in one of them, and the gallery is the reliable check, not the folder path.
  • Save to device before posting — your own videos. If the video is yours, the cleanest copy is the one you keep before it is ever posted: on the Post screen, open More options and turn on Save to device. TikTok's own help recommends keeping an offline copy this way, and the saved file has no watermark — it is the TikTok-ready export of your own video, not the watermarked re-download. For a video you already posted, the original is usually still in Photos, Gallery, Files, or your editing app — that original is a better source than downloading the published post. If the original is gone, Save video on your own posted video still works, watermarked.
  • A downloader app or site. SnapTik and the other downloader apps take a public video's link and hand back the MP4 — SnapTik's own page says on Android the file lands in the Downloads folder and the gallery picks it up within a few seconds, and it can also save a photo slideshow's images one by one. This is a one-link-at-a-time route, and it is unofficial: SnapTik states it is not affiliated with TikTok or ByteDance. Use it for your own videos, or videos you have permission to store — and do not treat a "no watermark" download as a clean master: a downloader cannot restore detail TikTok's processing removed, and removing the watermark does not remove the copyright or grant permission to repost. SnapDisk never strips a watermark — it copies the file exactly as it sits on your phone.

Three more facts about where things are. First, a Favorite is not a file: tapping the bookmark icon saves a video to your Favorites inside TikTok — a pointer to the video on TikTok's servers, not a copy on your phone. If the creator deletes the video, or TikTok removes it, the Favorite points at nothing. A Favorite is a list for later watching, not a backup. Second, drafts live only in the app: an unsaved draft is not in the export and not a file — save it to the device before posting, or it dies with the app, the phone, or a logout. Third, photo slideshows save through the same step: TikTok's photo posts are slideshows, and the same Share → Save video step saves the slideshow as it plays; if the individual photos matter, a downloader's image option saves them one by one. And the export, covered in full in Method 2: TikTok's Download your data request returns links and metadata, not MP4s — so the export is the list of what you posted, and the files still have to be saved one by one.

What you need

An Android phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk); a USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter (most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone doesn't see the drive, try a different adapter); and enough space on the drive for the TikTok videos you want to keep. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit. And one check before you start: save the videos you want first — Share → Save video, or Save to device before posting — and check in your gallery that they are actually there. The save is the step people skip, and a backup run over a phone with no saved TikToks copies nothing and proves nothing.

Method 2: the Download your data export — an index, not the files

Method 1 copies the TikToks already saved on your phone as files. If you want everything — every video you ever posted, including the ones you never saved one by one — TikTok offers an export, and it is worth understanding exactly what it is, because it is not what most people expect. Here is the whole route:

  1. Request the export. In TikTok, go to Profile, tap the menu, then Settings and privacy → Account → Download your data. Pick a format — JSON if you want the machine-readable list, TXT if you want to read it yourself — and tap Request data. TikTok builds the file after you ask; it is a request, not an instant download.
  2. Wait, then download within 4 days. TikTok notifies you when the archive is ready, and the download link expires after 4 days — download it promptly, or request it again. The last 24 to 48 hours of some data categories may be missing from the archive, so treat it as a snapshot, not a live mirror.
  3. Understand what arrived. The archive is a ZIP of your account records: profile information, watch-video history, comment history, likes, and links to your videos. The video files themselves are not in the ZIP — the export carries metadata about your videos and URLs pointing at where they live on TikTok's servers, and not one MP4. This is the single most surprising fact about the export, and every independent guide agrees on it: the official export alone is an index, not a backup.
  4. Turn the index into files. The export is still useful: it is the complete list of what you posted, with URLs, and that list outlives a deleted account or a deleted video. To get the actual files, save each video with Share → Save video, one by one — slow, but it needs no computer — or feed the URL list into a desktop tool on a computer, which is the bulk route the guides point at. There is no bulk on-device route that hands you every video as an MP4, and this article does not promise one.
  5. Keep the record too. The export ZIP is not a photo or video, so SnapDisk skips it — copy the ZIP to the drive by hand in the Files app if you want the record on the drive alongside the videos. The ZIP is one file on the same phone that could be lost, and its link expires in 4 days, so the hand copy is the step that turns the export into something kept.

Two honest notes. First, the export is a record of your account, not a copy of your media: drafts, LIVE recordings, and recent activity are not guaranteed to be in it, and some data affecting other people's privacy may be unavailable. Second, the export does not rescue a video that is already gone — a video deleted from TikTok, or a video whose creator disabled downloads, is not fetchable from the export's link either. Save the videos that matter while they are still there; the export is the list, the save is the file.

Method 3: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you'd rather not install anything, Android's built-in Files app can copy the saved TikToks to the USB drive — after the save step, which no file manager can do for you: 1. Save the videos first, exactly as in Method 1 — Share → Save video in TikTok, or Save to device before posting — and check the gallery holds them. 2. Plug in the USB drive. A notification or a "USB drive" entry should appear in the Files app under Storage devices. 3. Open Files, browse to the folder that holds the saved TikToks — Internal storage > Download, a TikTok folder inside DCIM or Pictures, or wherever your gallery shows them — select the videos (or long-press the folder to select the whole thing), tap ⋮ → Copy to, then choose the USB drive as the destination. 4. Wait for the copy to finish — and don't unplug the drive until the Files app says it's done. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time TikToks pile up — and you have to remember which folder holds the saves, because the Files app will not tell you that Downloads is not where TikTok puts them on every phone. For a TikTok habit that adds videos every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

The 5 GB free cap, and how it shapes the routine

SnapDisk's free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — verified in the repo as FREE_BACKUP_BYTES_LIMIT, 5 GiB — and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. For TikTok, 5 GB goes further than it does for a camera library: saved TikToks are the compressed short videos TikTok serves, and a folder of saved TikToks is often one free run. The long videos are the part that eats the cap — a Downloads folder full of three-minute clips can pass 5 GB on its own, and that is the count to do before you start: open the Files app, look at the size of the Download folder (or the TikTok folder), divide by 5 GB, and that is how many free runs the copy needs. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (product id pro_unlock, verified in the repo's billing code) that auto-starts the backup when a disk is connected and removes the size limit, so a whole saved-TikTok library copies in one run — and the auto-start is what makes the repeat actually happen, because the copy starts when the drive connects instead of waiting for you to remember. A file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped with its reason and the job continues — verified in the repo's BackupService. A single TikTok never reaches that, but a long screen recording of one can — and that file is skipped and listed, the videos around it copy normally, and the fix is the same exFAT format recommended above.

What this does not back up

This is the part people get wrong, so here it is plainly. Copying the saved TikToks backs up the files — the videos and the slideshow photos. It does not back up:

  • Your TikTok account. Your profile, your followers, your likes, and your account itself live on TikTok's servers, not in a folder on your phone. Copying files does not copy the account, and if the account is deleted or banned, the drive holds the videos, not the account.
  • Favorites and bookmarks. A Favorite is a pointer inside TikTok, not a file. The Download your data export lists your liked and favourited videos as URLs — the list is a record, and the record is not the video. Save the video itself if it matters.
  • Drafts you never saved. A draft lives only in the app on that phone. It is not in the export, and no backup app can copy it — save it to the device before posting, or accept that it dies with the app.
  • Videos still only in TikTok. A video you never saved — never pressed Save video on, never saved to device before posting — is not a file on your phone. Method 1 copies what is there; Method 2 is the list of the rest, not the files themselves. If the creator deletes the video or disables downloads, the save goes with it.
  • Chats and messages. Direct messages are text in TikTok's database, not files in a folder. The Download your data export includes them as records — SnapDisk copies image and video files only, so the chat history stays on the phone.
  • Other people's videos, without permission. A video someone else posted is theirs to allow or not — Save video appears only when the creator allows downloads. This article covers your own videos already on your phone, or videos you have permission to store. Downloading someone else's video does not give you permission to repost it, and stripping the watermark does not change that — the saved copy keeps the watermark and the creator's handle, and SnapDisk copies it exactly as it is.
  • The watermark-free original. The official save is TikTok's processed, watermarked copy. The clean master is the camera or editor export you kept before uploading — keep it, because no download recovers it once it is gone.

If you want the whole account — videos, likes, and account data together — the Download your data export in Method 2 is the route, and it is the only one TikTok itself supports. This article's one-tap route is for the videos and slideshows as files on a drive you hold.

What to avoid

  • Running the backup before saving anything. TikTok videos live in the app, not on your phone. A backup run over a phone with no saved TikToks copies nothing — and finishes green, which looks like a completed backup of an empty set. Save first, check the gallery, then back up.
  • Trusting one guide's folder and skipping the check. The guides disagree — a Videos folder, a Download folder, a TikTok album, the Camera folder — and your phone decides. Open the gallery and the Files app and look before you start, or you will back up an empty folder and think the job is done.
  • Treating a Favorite as a backup. The bookmark icon saves a pointer inside TikTok, not a file. If the video is deleted, the Favorite points at nothing. Save the video itself.
  • Treating the data export as the videos. The Download your data ZIP holds links and metadata, not MP4s, and its link expires after 4 days. Download it, keep it as the record — and save the videos separately, or the export was only a list.
  • Copying the ZIP and calling it a backup. SnapDisk copies image and video files, not zip archives — the export ZIP would be skipped. Unzip first, then copy the folder — and copy the ZIP itself by hand if you want the record on the drive.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few videos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Unverified — no wake lock, no battery-saver handling in the repo. Keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.
  • Downloading other people's videos and reposting them. A downloader makes it easy to pull anyone's public video onto your phone, but your archive is your own media or media you have permission to store — and a missing Save video button is the creator's no, not a puzzle to work around.
  • Waiting for the video to disappear. A creator can delete a video or turn downloads off at any time, and then the save is gone with it. If a video matters, save it while it is still there.

Rule of thumb: the videos live in TikTok until you save them — save them to the phone first, then the drive. The folder is whichever one your phone chose — check the gallery, not one guide's path. The export is a list, not the files — save the videos separately. Your own videos, or ones you have permission to store, only. Do the save, check the copy on the drive before you delete anything, and request the data export as well if the list of everything you posted matters.
