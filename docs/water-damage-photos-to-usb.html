What water does to a phone

A wet phone is not one situation — it is two, and which one you are in decides everything, including whether this article applies to you at all:

  • The phone is on and working normally. It got splashed, dunked briefly, or caught in the rain, and it still turns on, the screen responds, nothing glitches, and it is not warm. This is the narrow window, and it is the only case Methods 1 to 3 are for: back the library up now, verify it on the drive, and then power the phone off and let it dry properly — the copy is a race, not a repair, and the phone still needs drying after the backup is done.
  • The phone is off, dead, glitching, warm, or the port is wet. This article does not apply. Power it off, leave it off, do not charge it, do not plug anything into it, and go to Method 4 — the lab. A phone that will not turn on has no software route, and a wet port has no drive going into it.
  • Corrosion starts within hours and spreads over days. Water on a powered board starts eating copper connections within hours, and salt water is the worst case — dramatically more conductive and more corrosive than fresh water. A phone that works now may not turn on tomorrow, which is why the backup cannot wait for the weekend.
  • Turning it on to "check" it can be the damage. Every second a wet phone stays powered on increases the chance of a short circuit frying a critical component — and powering it back on to see if it still works pushes current through pathways that may already be corroding. If it is off, leave it off.
  • Charging it can be the damage too. A phone that survived the dunk can go on the charger overnight and be stone dead by morning — charging pushes current through a corroding board, and the short circuits that follow can fry components permanently. That is why a wet phone is never charged, and why the backup in this article runs on the battery the phone has left.
  • Rice does nothing. Rice absorbs humidity from the air, slowly — it does not pull water out of the inside of a phone, and it adds dust to the ports. A hair dryer is worse: it pushes water deeper and cooks adhesives. Neither belongs anywhere near a wet phone.
  • The memory chip survives water better than the rest of the phone. Your photos sit on a memory chip that outlasts the board around it, which is why labs recover data from fully dead water phones every week — but only by reading the chip directly, with the phone's original security hardware, which is a lab job, not an app.
  • The backup has to finish while the phone still works. A copy still running when the phone dies is not a backup, and after the phone dies there is no undo — what survives is what is already on the drive, plus whatever already uploaded to the cloud.

None of this is fixed by backing up after the phone dies — after it dies, the phone either comes back from the lab with its photos, or it does not, and the backup is already too late. The fix is a copy that finishes while the phone still works: oldest-first, verified on the drive, and the drive kept dry and separate once the copy is done.

What you need

  • A phone that is on, working normally, and has a dry USB port — check this first, before anything else, because if any of the three is false, this article does not apply and Method 4 does. Dry the port with the charging port facing down, shake the water out gently, and dry the outside with a cloth — and do not plug the drive in until the port is dry.
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. For a whole library, a portable SSD holds more — but a portable SSD may need a powered hub, because the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • No charger — a wet phone must not be charged, so the copy runs on the battery the phone has left. Check the battery before you start: if it is nearly dead, copy the oldest photos first and accept that the copy may not finish — SnapDisk copies oldest-first, and finished files stay on the drive, so a partial copy is still a partial rescue. A powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk — and it still puts power into a wet port, so it is not the route for a wet phone.
  • Wi-Fi, if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3 — the drive copy itself needs none of it. SnapDisk has no internet permission at all.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do it now, not after the phone has dried, and do not delete from the phone until the check is done.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the phone will keep filling it — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job at a time. The phone's photos need only the drive; the charger needs to stay in the drawer until the phone is dry.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive, and it needs no Wi-Fi at all. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start. Two honest limits first: this method is only for a phone that is on, working normally, and has a dry port — if any of those is false, go to Method 4 — and the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no water-damage run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified.

  1. Dry the port first. Hold the phone with the charging port facing down, shake the water out gently, and dry the outside with a cloth. Do NOT plug the drive in until the port is dry — power through a wet port can burn out the chip that holds the photos. If the port stays wet, stop here and go to Method 4.
  2. Check the phone is actually working normally. Screen responds, touch works, nothing glitches, the phone is not warm, and nothing smells wrong. If anything is off, stop — power the phone off and go to Method 4. The lab's advice wins the moment anything about the phone is wrong.
  3. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos on the phone are the ones a dying phone puts most at risk.
  4. Check the battery. The copy runs on the battery the phone has left — a wet phone must not be charged — so if the battery is low, the copy may not finish, and that is why oldest-first matters: the earliest photos copy first, and finished files stay on the drive even if the battery dies mid-copy.
  5. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  6. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  7. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  8. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  9. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  10. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it. The backup has to complete before the phone dies, and a dying phone cannot wait for a backup. If the copy is interrupted, resume it — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  11. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  12. Unplug the drive and put it somewhere safe and dry — not in the phone, and not next to the wet phone. A backup that stays plugged into a dying phone is a backup in the blast radius. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.
  13. Power the phone off and let it dry. The backup is done, and the phone still needs drying — power it off, leave it off, keep it in a dry, ventilated spot with the ports down, and get it inspected if it matters. Do not charge it until it is dry and checked.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A phone that has never been backed up often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size, so the next emergency is covered the moment you plug the drive in. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every phone-to-USB guide above describes — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when you want the backup done now:

  1. Dry the port first — port down, shake the water out, dry the outside — and do not plug the drive in until the port is dry. If the port stays wet, stop and go to Method 4.
  2. Plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  3. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  4. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  5. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder a dying phone takes with it.
  6. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big and the phone may be dying.
  7. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  8. Unplug the drive and put it somewhere safe and dry — not in the phone — and only then think about deleting anything from the phone, after the check. Then power the phone off and let it dry.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is still dying, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time, with the battery you have left.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step most people skip after a phone gets wet — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. And the check has a catch of its own: with no Wi-Fi, the upload cannot run at all, so the cloud check waits for Wi-Fi — the drive copy does not:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone, and on Wi-Fi, not while the phone is dying. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and dry the phone now.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones a dying phone takes with it.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the Wi-Fi, the account, or the subscription the dying phone does not care about.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, when Wi-Fi returns — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on Wi-Fi, an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round — and why, after water damage, the drive copy is the backup that is finished when you need it, not the upload that is still running.

Method 4: if the phone is off or dead — the lab route

The phone is off, dead, glitching, warm, or the port is wet. There is no software route left — no app, no Files copy, no cable trick — and trying one can finish the damage. What is left is stopping the damage and letting a lab do the reading:

  1. Power it off — or leave it off. Not sleep. Off. If it is already off or dead, leave it that way. Do not turn it back on to "check" it, and do not keep testing the buttons.
  2. Do not charge it and do not plug anything into it. Not a charger, not a drive, not a computer cable. Charging pushes current through a corroding board, and the short circuits that follow can fry components permanently — a phone that survived the dunk can be stone dead by morning after a night on the charger.
  3. Take the case off and pop out the SIM tray. If the phone has a removable battery — rare these days, but some older Androids do — remove it. Hold the phone with the charging port facing down so water can drip out, and dry the outside with whatever is handy.
  4. Do not use rice, a hair dryer, or heat. Rice does not pull water out of the inside of a phone and adds dust to the ports; a hair dryer pushes water deeper and cooks adhesives. Leave the phone in a dry, ventilated spot, ports down.
  5. Note what kind of water it was and how long it was in. Salt water is the worst case — dramatically more conductive and more corrosive than fresh water — and the lab will ask, because it changes the urgency.
  6. Check what is already backed up — from another device, not this phone. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and see what actually uploaded, and check any other cloud service the same way. What is already in the cloud is already safe, and what is not there is what the lab is for.
  7. Contact a recovery lab today, not next week. Corrosion spreads, and the longer a wet phone waits, the worse it gets. A lab inspects the board, cleans it, and repairs the faults that keep the phone from booting — the storage chip usually survives water better than the board around it, which is why labs recover data from fully dead water phones every week. The lab mdrepairs.com quotes a $100 diagnostic, credited toward a successful recovery — and the recovery itself is priced per case, because it depends on what the water reached.
  8. Do not try a repair shop fix first if the data matters. A board repair that powers the phone up the wrong way can finish what the water started — tell the lab the photos are the point, before any repair work is authorised.
  9. If the lab brings the phone back, back it up then — with Method 1, the moment it boots. Do not test-boot a wet phone yourself to see if it works: the check that matters is the lab's, and your job after the lab is the copy, oldest-first, verified on the drive.

One thing the lab route does not do: it does not bring back a phone whose storage chip itself is destroyed, or a phone that was factory-reset before it died. That is the case for the backup that should have finished while the phone still worked — and the reason Method 1 exists for the next phone, before it gets wet.

What this does not do

  • Does not dry the phone. The backup copies photos — it does not remove water from the board. After the copy, power the phone off and let it dry, and get it inspected if it matters. Drying is a separate job, and the phone still needs it.
  • Does not fix water damage. The backup does not repair corrosion, a shorted board, or a phone that will not boot — it only makes the damage survivable for the photos. If the phone dies, it still needs a lab; the photos just do not go down with it.
  • Does not undo a death. Once the phone dies with photos still on it, the photos on the phone are out of reach until a lab reads the chip. The backup has to finish while the phone still works, not after it.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Back those up in Settings, Google, Backup, too — and export 2FA tokens and transit passes separately, because a phone that dies takes those with everything else.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com when Wi-Fi returns. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the phone dies, not after it.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no water-damage run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not charge the phone while the drive is in the port. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — and a wet phone must not be charged at all, so the copy runs on the battery the phone has left.
  • Does not power a portable SSD from the phone alone. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. A spinning hard drive may need its own power too.
  • Does not fix a phone that will not boot. If the phone will not turn on, no software route exists — check the cloud, then the lab.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only. On an iPhone the wet-phone route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive — and the same rules apply: dry port, no charging a wet phone, and the lab if it will not turn on.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Charging a wet phone. Charging pushes current through a board that may already be corroding, and the short circuits that follow can fry components permanently. Never charge a wet phone — the backup runs on the battery the phone has left.
  • Plugging anything into a wet USB port. Not a drive, not a charger, not a computer cable. Dry the port first — port down, shake the water out — and if the port stays wet, the drive stays out and Method 4 applies.
  • Powering the phone on to "check" it. If it is off, leave it off. Every power-on with water inside is another chance for a short circuit to fry a critical component.
  • Waiting to back up. Corrosion starts within hours and spreads over days — a phone that works now may not turn on tomorrow. The backup is now, not after the phone has dried, and not at the weekend.
  • Burying the phone in rice. Rice does not pull water out of the inside of a phone, and it adds dust to the ports. A hair dryer is worse — it pushes water deeper and cooks adhesives.
  • Starting the copy before checking the phone works. If the screen glitches, the touch fails, the phone is warm, or anything smells wrong, stop — power off, Method 4. The lab's advice wins the moment anything about the phone is wrong.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want — when Wi-Fi returns.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder a dying phone takes with it.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is still dying, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then decide.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then decide — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it, not after the copy fails.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole library in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole library is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.
  • Assuming the generic guides cover water damage. The alibaba.com and topselect.ca guides move photos to a USB drive — but neither says the phone may die, neither dries the port, neither forbids charging, and neither sends a dead phone to a lab. The transfer is Method 2; the water is Method 4.

Rule of thumb: a wet phone that still works is a race, not a repair — dry the port, copy the whole library oldest-first, check a few photos on the drive, unplug the drive, and power the phone off to dry; and if the phone is off, dead, or the port is wet, leave it off and call a lab, because no app can copy photos off a phone that will not turn on.
