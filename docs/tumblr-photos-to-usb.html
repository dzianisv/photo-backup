Save Tumblr photos to USB from Android

Your photos on Tumblr live on Tumblr's servers, not on your phone. Save each one in the app — it lands in the Tumblr folder — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

The confusion first: Tumblr is not a photo library — and Save is not a bookmark

Tumblr sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. A Tumblr dashboard is a feed of posts, and a post can hold text, a photo, several photos, a GIF, or a video — and the photo you see is one piece of a post, not a file in a library. The guides that say "download the post" and the guides that say "save the photo" are describing two different jobs, and this article is about the second one: the photo, as a file, on your phone, on a drive. A post is not a file. A photo inside a post is not a file either, not until you save it. Keep the two apart and the rest of the article is straightforward; mix them up and every step after it answers the wrong question.

The second confusion is Tumblr versus a photo library, and it is the one that decides whether the job works at all. Tumblr is a social feed. Photos live in posts, in dashboards, in blogs, in likes — and they live on Tumblr's servers, not on your phone. A photo library — Google Photos, iCloud Photos, the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and album, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. Tumblr is not that. It shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Tumblr account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Tumblr either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no social account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of Tumblr and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a save. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The third confusion hides inside Tumblr itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. A bookmark keeps a post inside your Tumblr account, in a list you can scroll later. It is the fastest option, and it is not a download: you are keeping the post, not the photo. You cannot send it in a chat as a file, you cannot copy it to a drive, and if the author deletes the post, the bookmark points at nothing — the post is gone, and the thing you kept was a pointer to it. Liking a post is even less: a like is a signal, not a save of any kind, and it writes nothing anywhere. Reblogging is a different job again: a reblog puts the post on your own blog, still on Tumblr's servers, still not a file on your phone. Copying a post's permalink is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the post remaining available. The only tap that writes a file is the long press on the photo itself — or the three-dot menu's Save — and if you have been bookmarking photos and wondering why none of them are on your phone, this is why: you have been keeping posts, and posts are not files.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on Tumblr's servers. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The save is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the save is a step you have to take yourself, one photo at a time, because Tumblr offers no save-them-all button and no auto-save setting. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the save-one-and-check rule, because for Tumblr the date problem is a save problem: the file is written when you save it, and the day you saved it is the day it sorts by.

The wall: Tumblr saves nothing by itself — the save is yours to do

Tumblr does not put dashboard photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the save describes a manual step — press and hold the photo, tap the download or save option — because the manual step is the only step there is. The only media-adjacent settings Tumblr offers are about data, not about saving: a data-saving mode and media autoplay, under General Settings. None of them saves anything. Data saving is about how much data the dashboard uses, not about where photos go, and turning it on makes the saved copy smaller, not automatic — it loads the lower-resolution image, and the photo you save is the photo you loaded. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache, which saves data when you scroll back over posts you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is Tumblr's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's cache is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you save it, and you save it one photo at a time.

The save itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because the guides agree on it in the same words across independent sources. Press and hold the photo. A menu appears. Tap Download photo — that is the Android wording EaseUS documents — or Save image or Download image, the wordings gbtimes documents, because the exact label varies between app versions. The photo lands in a folder called Tumblr, visible in your Gallery. That is the Android Central user's version too, first-hand: long-press the photo in the Tumblr app, tap save photo, and a folder called Tumblr appears in the gallery — the same folder Facebook and X create for their own saves. Rottenwifi gives the same gesture and points at Tumblr's own documentation for it: Tumblr's Android shortcuts and tips page documents the press-and-hold save. If the long press shows no menu, tap the photo first to open it full-screen, then press and hold it again — the save option belongs to the photo, and a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, not the file. The other route is the three-dot menu: tap the image, tap the three dots, tap Save, which is the version llccars documents for the app. Try the long press first, then the menu, then the browser route in the fixes below.

There is one more thing the save does not let you do, and it is worth knowing before you save a hundred photos, because it decides how you organise them afterwards. The save goes straight to the Tumblr folder: no guide that describes the save describes a location picker in the Tumblr app, and the first-hand account shows the folder created automatically, with no step where you choose where the file goes. One guide alone, HitPaw, adds a choose-a-folder step after the download tap — and no other guide, and no first-hand account, shows a picker in the app. Treat that step as unverified: the save lands in the Tumblr folder, every time, on the versions the guides describe. That is a limit, and it is also a convenience: every Tumblr photo you save is in the same folder, and the folder is the thing you point a backup at. Do not waste an evening looking for a save-location setting in the Tumblr app. There is not one, on Android, in any guide this run found.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the save sometimes silently does nothing. The Tumblr app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the save tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first save, open the Gallery and look for the Tumblr folder. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every save after it works the same way. If it is not there, open the phone's Settings, go to Apps, find Tumblr, open Permissions, and grant Photos and videos — then save the photo again. Do not save fifty photos and check at the end. Save one, check the folder, then save the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the Tumblr app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the Tumblr account, because the photos live in that account's dashboard and blogs. Enough free space on the phone to hold the saves: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, save in batches and copy each batch to the drive before saving the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the save steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a save guide.

One more check, on the Tumblr side: open the post and confirm the photo is actually there. If the photo is in a post you can open, it is a photo you can save. If the post is from a blog you do not follow that is private, or it was deleted, or it is flagged and hidden from your dashboard, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be saved, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. Do not save first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then press and hold it. A save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Save one photo from the dashboard, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Tumblr app on your Android phone and sign in. Scroll through your dashboard — or open the blog that posted the photo, which lists that blog's posts in one place — and press and hold the photo you want. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a save of a thumbnail is a save of a thumbnail. Tap Download photo in the menu that appears. Then open the Gallery and look for the Tumblr folder: the photo should be there, at full size, and the size should be plausible for a photo, not a few kilobytes.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the Tumblr folder is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo in the Tumblr folder is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of the Tumblr folder, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where Tumblr put it. The default run, described in the next section but one, reads the media index with no folder filter, and the Tumblr folder is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the post on Tumblr if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is in the Tumblr folder, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the saved Tumblr photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the saved batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one blog, the photos from one photoset. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand long-press saves is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole blog you own, use Method 3, which gets the blog's photos in one export instead of one save at a time. For someone else's blog there is no bulk route — Tumblr offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the save option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, update the Tumblr app: the menu wording varies between app versions, and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there — gbtimes notes the long press may not work on some devices or older app versions. Second, try the other gesture: open the photo full-screen and use the three-dot menu's Save instead of the long press, or the long press instead of the menu. Third, open the post in Chrome instead of the app: open the post at tumblr.com in the browser, tap the picture to enlarge it, press and hold it, and choose the download or save-image option the browser offers — the file lands in Downloads, and the gallery shows it from there. Fourth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, Tumblr, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and save again. One of those four fixes the save in almost every case, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, in the Tumblr folder, before you save the next one.

Method 2: Save a whole post as an image — the Share route

There is a second in-app route, and it is worth knowing because it answers a different question: not "save the photo" but "keep the post". Open the post in the Tumblr app, tap the Share icon, and choose Save as Image, when available. Tumblr describes this as a clean image of the post without the phone's status bar, navigation, or Tumblr interface — a rendered picture of the post as it looks on screen, saved as a single image file. It lands in your Gallery like any other saved image, and from there the backup is the same as Method 1: the default run copies it with the rest of the library, no computer.

The distinction matters, because the two routes produce different things, and choosing the wrong one is the commonest disappointment in this article. Method 1 saves the photo: the image file itself, at the size Tumblr served it, with nothing else in the frame. Method 2 saves a picture of the post: the photo plus the blog name, the caption, the notes, the reblog chain — everything visible on screen, baked into one image. The text in a Save as Image file is pixels, not text: you cannot copy it, search it, or edit it, and the photo inside it is the dashboard rendition, not a separate file. If you want the photo, use Method 1. If you want the post — the joke with its caption, the thread with its replies, the thing as it looked — use Method 2. And the "when available" matters too: the option does not appear on every post, in every version, and this article does not claim it does. If Share shows no Save as Image, the post is a Method 1 post, or a screenshot post — and a screenshot is a picture of your screen, status bar and all, which is what Save as Image exists to avoid.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the Tumblr folder, select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 3: A whole blog you own — the export, and the no-bulk wall

There is no save-them-all button in the Tumblr app, and that is not a guess: every guide that describes the save describes one photo at a time, and none of them describes a batch command, a select-all, or a download-a-blog button in the app. The workaround for a thread or a blog is to make the finding fast, not the saving: open the blog that posted the photos and scroll its posts in one place instead of scattered through the dashboard. Then save them one by one, in order, with the same gesture — press and hold, Download photo — and check the Tumblr folder after the first few, so a permission or menu problem shows up on photo three, not photo three hundred.

For a whole blog you own, the honest route is the export, and it is Tumblr's own route, not a third-party tool. It is available on the web, not in the apps — on a phone, use a browser rather than the Tumblr app, and if the export controls are not visible, try the browser's request-desktop-site option. Open Tumblr in a browser and sign in to the account that controls the blog. Open Settings. Select the blog from the sidebar. Scroll to Export and click Export, with the blog's name. Wait for Tumblr to prepare the backup — check the same settings page periodically, and Tumblr also sends a completion email. When Download backup appears, click it to download the ZIP file. Export each blog separately, and make sure the selected blog is the one you intend to back up.

The ZIP is a real archive, and it is worth knowing what is in it before you download it. According to Tumblr's export documentation, it can include a Posts folder with an HTML file for each post — including reblogs, drafts, private posts, flagged posts, and other hidden posts associated with the blog — a Media folder with uploaded images, videos, and other media, in formats such as JPG, GIF, PNG, and MP4 where applicable, a CSV file containing comments, and HTML representations of messaging conversations. This is a collection of files intended as a backup, not a visual recreation of the blog's live theme, and some embeds may remain links or references rather than locally saved media. Unzip it first if it arrives as a ZIP, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives — and then back up the files it contains, the Media folder first.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the export is the route for your job. The export is for a blog you control: it does not provide an export for blogs you do not control, and it does not include liked posts — Tumblr's blog export does not include liked posts, so a library of likes is not a library you can export. A finished backup stays available for three days, and each blog allows four exports a month, so download the ZIP promptly when it is ready rather than starting repeated exports while one is processing. It is also slow: the backup is prepared on Tumblr's side, and the email or the Download backup button arrives when it is ready, not when you ask. Request it, wait, download it, unzip it, and then back up the files it contains. For ten photos, save them in the app. For a whole blog you own, request the export. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Tumblr folder — or the export's Media folder — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's blog there is no bulk route. Tumblr offers no export for a blog you do not control, no download-a-blog button, and no save-them-all in the app. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take a blog name or a post link and fetch the media — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them: they are outside Tumblr, they need your trust to run, and the series does not endorse third-party scrapers. If the photos are on someone else's blog, the honest routes are the ones in this article: save the photos you actually want one by one, or ask the blogger for the files. A thousand one-by-one saves is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a saved Tumblr photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a saved photo in the Tumblr folder copies with the rest of the library. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the Tumblr folder in shared storage.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: save the photo, open the gallery, and look for it in the Tumblr folder. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in the Tumblr folder: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into Tumblr. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Tumblr for anything. A photo still on Tumblr's servers is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on Tumblr's servers is invisible to the file manager. The save is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Tumblr; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Tumblr photos without saving them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Tumblr folder came from Tumblr, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Tumblr route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the save that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the Tumblr photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the Tumblr folder and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Tumblr folder yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on this exact folder — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a saved photo shows the save date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo saved from Tumblr shows the day you saved it, not the day it was posted — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a saved file is the save time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a saved Tumblr photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you saved it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For Tumblr, the taken date almost certainly did not survive, and the reason is the quality section below: Tumblr serves you its own copy of the photo, and a served copy is the step most likely to rewrite or drop the metadata. This article states that as the expected case, not as a certainty, because no source this run tested the taken date after an in-app save — but the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app: the file is written by the save, at save time, and its modified date is the save date. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: save one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its posted date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you save a hundred of them.

There is one way in which Tumblr is better than the other clouds on the date, and it is worth stating fairly. No source this run says Tumblr rewrites the taken date when the photo is uploaded — Tumblr stores the upload and serves a copy of it, and the upload is not the step that loses the date. That is the opposite of OneDrive, where Microsoft's own support forum says the taken date can be replaced by the upload date, especially by camera upload or a bulk upload, before you ever tap Save. For Tumblr the risk is at the save, not at the upload, and the risk is a property of the copy Tumblr serves, not a choice you make — there is no Unmodified Originals option in the Tumblr app, and no format choice before you press and hold. The date you get is the save date, and the defence is to know it before you save the batch.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Tumblr names the saved file itself, and two saves of the same photo — or two photos Tumblr named the same way — can arrive in the Tumblr folder under the same name, so the second save either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the post on Tumblr if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to save a batch and copy it to the drive before saving the next, or to rename the files in the Tumblr folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to save.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Save one blog's photos, back up the Tumblr folder, clear it, save the next blog's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the blogs in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the saved copy is Tumblr's copy — and the GIF that is really a video

A photo saved from Tumblr is not the photographer's original file. It is the copy Tumblr serves for the dashboard, and the difference is real even when it is not measured in pixels on the spot. Tumblr says larger versions may be available up to 2048 pixels wide and 3072 pixels tall — those dimensions are a ceiling for the cases where a larger version exists, not a promise about every image — and the file you save by long-press is the dashboard rendition, not necessarily the largest version Tumblr holds. The saved copy is a real photo, and it is good enough for the gallery, the drive, and a print at ordinary sizes. It is not the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app save is the wrong tool, and this article says so instead of letting you discover it on the drive.

The route to the larger version is a browser step, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the only route that gets the bigger file — and it is a browser route, not an app route. Open the image on tumblr.com in a browser, click it to see whether Tumblr offers a larger version, and save the image that loads. Tumblr's own troubleshooting guidance is the source for the ceiling: larger versions may be available up to 2048 pixels wide and 3072 pixels tall. On a phone it is a fiddly tap-and-hold per photo for a gain that is often small, and the honest advice is to use the browser route only when the photo matters at full size, and to use the in-app save for everything else: the in-app save is a long press, and the browser route is a detour per photo. Use each one for the job it fits.

Then there is the GIF question, and it is the trap this article exists to name, because Tumblr is the home of the animated post and the animated post is where the save breaks down. Not every looping image on Tumblr is a GIF file. Increasingly, Tumblr users embed short video clips — MP4s — that loop like GIFs, because they are smaller and cheaper to serve, and a GIFV post is a video wearing a GIF's clothes. The long-press save behaves differently on the two, and the guides say so in different words: a true GIF saves like a photo, through the same long-press menu; an MP4 loop offers a video save instead — "Save Video As" on the desktop menu — or, in the app, no save at all. One downloader roundup puts it bluntly: Tumblr still does not give you a native save button for video, GIF, or photoset posts. The honest position, across the guides, is that the photo save is reliable for still photos and unreliable for animated posts: a true GIF may save, an MP4 loop may save as a video file, and either may offer nothing, depending on the post and the app version.

The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds. Save the animated post, open the Tumblr folder, and look at what arrived: if it moves, it saved as an animation or a video; if it is a single still frame, the save caught one frame of the loop and the animation is gone. Either way the backup covers it, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .gif is in the image list, and .mp4 is in the video list, so a saved GIF and a saved MP4 both copy with the default run. The trap is not the backup — it is the expectation. A still frame is not the animation, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the animation matters, save it from the browser, where the menu tells you whether you are saving an image or a video, and check what arrived before you save the next twenty. And a post with several images — a photoset — saves one image at a time: press and hold the one image you want, not the post around it, because the save belongs to the image under your finger, and the other images in the post stay on Tumblr until you save each of them in turn.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: save one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the saved copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before saving — turn the data-saving mode off and wait for the photo to load crisp before you press and hold it. A save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the saved file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the save is worth doing at all. A photo saved from Tumblr is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the size: the saved copy is Tumblr's copy, and no backup step will put the missing pixels back. Save the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. If it is not, take the browser route for that one photo, and save the rest in the app.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest save, a middle one, and the most recent save. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Tumblr folder on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the Tumblr folder holds the photos plus whatever else Tumblr saved there — but the photo count should match the number you saved, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you saved, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second save overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the Tumblr folder before the backup ever saw it. Save one batch at a time and copy each batch before saving the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you saved, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Tumblr batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a saved photo shows the save date, not the posted date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the save, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, save that photo again from the browser at full size, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in Tumblr's copy, not at upload — and no save route will recover it, because the file Tumblr holds is the original, and the file you saved is the copy.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Tumblr login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The public-link downloader sites need no sign-in: copy the post link, paste it in, and save the file — that is the whole flow, and the guides that cover them say so plainly. A tool that asks for your Tumblr password to download a public photo is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours. The in-app save needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not bookmark photos and expect them on the drive. A bookmark keeps the post inside your Tumblr account, not a file, and a bookmarked post the author deletes is a bookmark that points at nothing. Bookmark the post to read it later, and save the photo to keep it. They are two different taps for two different jobs, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing in the Tumblr folder — because the photos were never saved, only bookmarked.

Do not save a whole blog one photo at a time. A thousand long-press saves is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Save the handful you actually want in the app, and request the export for a blog you own. The export is slow and the app is fast, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the export to reach someone else's blog: it is for a blog you control, and it will not contain liked posts either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the feed as the only copy. Tumblr is a feed, not a backup: posts can be deleted, blogs can go private or be closed, and shares can be revoked, and any one of those turns a photo on Tumblr into a photo that is gone. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the feed is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
