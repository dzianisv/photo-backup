What the search results actually offer

Search "back up photos from Google Pixel to USB flash drive without computer" and every top result teaches the same manual job, in different packaging:

  • YouTube (How to Connect USB Drive to Android Phone to Move Photos & Videos) — plug the drive in via OTG, open the phone's built-in file manager, select photos in DCIM, Move or Copy to the drive. Manual select-copy-paste, start to finish — and it suggests Move, which deletes the originals as it goes.
  • AnExplorer (How to Transfer Files from Android to USB Drive (OTG, No Root)) — connect the drive with an OTG adapter, open AnExplorer, long-press to select files in DCIM/Camera, Copy, Paste to the drive. A thorough manual guide, and still manual: you pick the files, and there is no resume or verification.
  • CellularNews (How to Move Pictures to a Flash Drive From a Google Phone) — connect the drive with an OTG adapter, then copy or move the photos using the Files app. The Files app on a Pixel is Files by Google, and the copy is by hand.
  • Dr.Fone (4 Practical Ways to Transfer Photos from Android to USB Flash Drive) — four ways: Google Drive, PC drag-and-drop, OTG copy in the file manager, or AirDroid over Wi-Fi. Three of the four need a computer or a cloud account; the OTG way is the same manual file-manager copy.
  • Manual do Bruxo (Transfer Photos from Android to Flash Drive: The Easy Way Most People Miss) — Files by Google, step by step: plug the drive in, open Files, Browse, Images, select, Copy to, choose the USB drive. The clearest Pixel-specific guide of the five — and it still ends with you selecting photos one by one.
  • Cloud routes — Google Photos backs up over Wi-Fi to Google's servers, not to a drive you hold. Its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos — that is where quality is actually lost, not on a USB copy. And a cloud backup stops working the day you stop paying for storage.

None of them offers an automated photo-library backup: nothing that takes the entire camera roll, copies it oldest-first in the background, shows progress, pauses and resumes, and verifies every file. That is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

What you need

A Google Pixel phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk); a USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into a Pixel, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter; and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Two Pixel specifics: there is no SD-card slot on any Pixel, so a USB drive is the only plug-in storage you get; and there is no Samsung-style My Files — the stock file manager is Files by Google. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Pixel camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?"
  3. Tap Backup. Your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. Method 2: Files by Google (the stock route, no app needed)

Pixel phones ship with Files by Google, and it can copy your photos to the USB drive: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a "USB drive connected" notification should appear. 2. Open Files, go to Browse, then Images — or Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 3. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or tap Select all). 4. Tap the three dots and choose Copy to, then select the USB drive. 5. Wait for the copy to finish, and don't unplug the drive until the Files app says it's done. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: through a computer (if you have one)

If a PC or Mac is handy, the old route still works: plug the Pixel into the computer with a USB cable, set the phone to File Transfer mode, and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive — or use a tool like Dr.Fone to bulk-export the photos first. This works fine, and it is the only route above that needs a second device in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google Photos' cloud backup is the tool if you want everything, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.

What to avoid

  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works, but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. Check Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting, too — it compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
