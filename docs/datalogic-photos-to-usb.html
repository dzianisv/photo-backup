Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. Host support is confirmed in the sources I found only on the Memor 10 and the Memor 20 — so on the Memor 11, the Memor 30, and the Memor 35 the drive mounting is the test itself: if it appears in Settings > Storage, the device hosts drives and the rest of this method works; if it never appears, the device does not host drives and Method 4 is the route. On the Memor 10, use the special USB-C OTG cable, not the stock cable: the manual says the stock cable works "only in client mode". The Memor 10 runs Android 8.1, the Memor 20 runs Android 9, the Memor 11 runs Android 11, upgradable to Android 15, and the Memor 30 and the Memor 35 run Android 13, upgradable to Android 18, so SnapDisk installs on all of them; the original Memor from 2008 runs Windows Mobile and cannot run it at all.
  2. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Memor camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  3. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: a file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

A file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — the generic guides' route is an OTG connection with the built-in file manager, and it is the manual route for external storage: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. On the Memor 10, use the special USB-C OTG cable, not the stock cable: the manual says the stock cable works "only in client mode". 2. Open the file manager (Files by Google on Memor handhelds) and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: Datalogic's own tools (Google Photos, Google Drive, or a microSD card — not phone-to-USB)

If you want Datalogic's own route, know what each tool actually does — there is no Datalogic backup page pointing at a USB drive at all, and none of the stock tools writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app, and it is the stock photo backup on Memor handhelds: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead. 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — "on Android devices the default built-in service is Google Drive. With Google Drive you have 15Gb free" — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 15 GB of free storage. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. 4. A microSD card is the local option on the Memor 10, the Memor 11, and the Memor 20, and on the Memor 30 and the Memor 35 up to 2 TB. It is a real local copy — but the card stays in the device, and opening the card anywhere else needs a card reader. 5. A computer copy is the other way to move files off the device — the Memor 10 manual's own route is a USB connection that lets you read and write files on the device's internal and external storage from a host computer. It is a real backup — but it needs a PC in between, and the backup lands on the PC, not on a USB drive as plain files straight from the device. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • USB host support is unconfirmed on three models. The Memor 10 and the Memor 20 list USB host support — the Memor 10 manual lists "High Speed USB 2.0 Host and Client with USB Type C", and the Memor 20 datasheet says its cable lets the device "work as host and client" — but no source I found mentions host or OTG support on the Memor 11, the Memor 30, or the Memor 35 at all, so plug a drive in and check Settings > Storage on those models before relying on it. The drive mounting is the test.
  • The Memor 10 needs the special OTG cable. Its stock cable 94A050044 works "only in client mode", and the manual says a "Special USB-C OTG cable is needed for Memor 10 to work in host mode" — so a Memor 10 with only the stock cable cannot host a drive at all, and Method 4 is the route until that cable is bought. The Memor 20's host cable is included with the device.
  • The original Memor cannot run SnapDisk at all. The Memor from 2008 runs Windows Mobile, below SnapDisk's Android 7.0 minimum — the Memor 10 runs Android 8.1, the Memor 20 runs Android 9, the Memor 11 runs Android 11, upgradable to Android 15, and the Memor 30 and the Memor 35 run Android 13, upgradable to Android 18, so they all clear it, but the original Memor does not, and the computer copy in Method 4 is the route for it.
  • Anything below Android 7.0 cannot run SnapDisk at all. Every model listed here clears the minimum — the Memor 10 runs Android 8.1, the Memor 20 runs Android 9, the Memor 11 runs Android 11, upgradable to Android 15, and the Memor 30 and the Memor 35 run Android 13, upgradable to Android 18 — but older Memor models exist, so check Settings > About phone if you are unsure: below Android 7.0, SnapDisk cannot install, and the computer copy in Method 4 is the route.
  • "Datalogic" also means barcode scanners, not just handhelds. Datalogic is a barcode-scanner company first, and most search results for the brand are scanners and their manuals — none of the top results for this query even mentions Datalogic at all. This article is about the photos stored on the Memor handheld itself.
  • The adapter differs by model. All five models have a USB Type-C port, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into any of them and a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter on any of them — except the Memor 10, which needs Datalogic's special USB-C OTG cable for host mode at all. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the device to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into the Memor 10, the Memor 11, the Memor 20, the Memor 30, and the Memor 35, all of which have USB-C — so an adapter is only needed for a regular USB-A drive, and it must be a USB-C OTG adapter on those models. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the device to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • Using the Memor 10's stock cable for host mode. The stock cable 94A050044 works "only in client mode" — it connects the Memor 10 to a computer, which is the opposite direction from hosting a drive. The manual says a "Special USB-C OTG cable is needed for Memor 10 to work in host mode": without it, the drive never mounts, and no app can fix that.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your device up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. A computer copy with a USB cable moves files to a computer — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The Datalogic datasheets and the Memor 30/35 product page are real Datalogic pages — but the datasheets only list an Android version, a chipset, or a USB connector type, and the product page only describes the hardware. Neither of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking host support on one model for host support on the other. The Memor 10 and the Memor 20 list USB host support — but no source I found mentions host or OTG support on the Memor 11, the Memor 30, or the Memor 35. Test with a drive on those models, and use Method 2 or 4 on that model if the drive never mounts.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk and Photos to USB work with any drive.
  • Mistaking a camera-card bridge for a handheld backup. Using a phone as a bridge between a camera's memory card and an SSD is a real field backup — but it backs up the camera's card, not the handheld's own gallery. If your photos are on the Memor itself, the drive plugs straight into the handheld, and Method 1 or 2 is the route.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — the Memor 10, the Memor 11, and the Memor 20 take a card, and the Memor 30 and the Memor 35 take one up to 2 TB — but the card stays in the device, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Memor handheld the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the device.
