Why RAW changes the backup

A JPEG is a finished photo. A RAW file — usually .dng on Android, sometimes .raw — is the unprocessed sensor data plus the settings to develop it later. It holds far more brightness and colour information than the JPEG the phone shows you, which is exactly why you shoot it, and exactly why the backup has to be exact: recompress it once, convert it to a JPEG "for convenience", or let a cloud service re-save it at a smaller size, and the extra data is gone. There is no undo. A RAW backup is therefore not a photo backup with bigger files — it is a different job, with one rule: the file that lands on the drive must be bit-identical to the file on the phone.

The cloud trap is sharper for RAW than for JPEGs. Google Photos "Storage saver" uploads are compressed copies, not originals — fine for a JPEG you already have, fatal for a .dng whose whole value is the uncompressed data. "Original quality" uploads keep the file, but then the backup lives on somebody else's server, counts against your storage quota at tens of megabytes a shot, and still has to come back down before it is a file you own. A USB drive holds the original file, offline, with no quota and no re-save — provided the copy itself does not touch the bytes. SnapDisk's copy does not: it streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive, with no transcoding step anywhere in the copy path, and it checks the size of every file after copying, renaming the .part file to the final file only after the size verification passes. A .dng that arrives on the drive is the same file that left the phone.

Size is the second way RAW changes the job. A phone JPEG is a few megabytes; a .dng is typically tens of megabytes, and a day of shooting is easily a gigabyte or several. That changes the arithmetic of every free tier and every drive format in this article — the free 5 GB cap, the exFAT choice, the six-hour background budget — in ways that barely matter for JPEGs. The sections below take them in that order.

What you need

  • An Android phone that shoots RAW. Most phones that offer it do so in the stock camera app's Pro mode or in a third-party camera app — the .dng files usually land in DCIM/Camera next to the JPEGs, sometimes in the camera app's own folder. Check where yours land before you start: open the Files app, look in DCIM/Camera, and confirm the .dng files are there. If they live in another folder, you will point SnapDisk at that folder instead — that is the certain route, covered below.
  • A USB drive with enough free space. Add up the shoot first: a hundred .dng files at 25 MB each is 2.5 GB, a thousand is 25 GB. A 64 GB drive covers most single trips; a 256 GB drive covers a season. Buy the size after the count, not before it.
  • A drive formatted exFAT. The format reference for this article is DriveForge's exFAT-vs-FAT32-vs-NTFS guide: exFAT has no 4 GB per-file limit and is read and written by current Android phones, Windows, and Mac. FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more — a single RAW file almost never reaches that, but the library total is large, and exFAT is the practical format for a RAW library. NTFS is a Windows format that most Android phones cannot open on their own — do not use it for a phone backup drive.
  • An OTG adapter if your drive is USB-A. A dual USB-C drive plugs straight in; a standard USB-A stick needs a data-capable OTG adapter. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for the drive.
  • SnapDisk v0.1.2. Not on Google Play yet — install the APK from the GitHub release page. It needs Android 7.0 or later (minSdk 24 in the repo). No account, no cloud, no ads, and no internet permission — your photos physically cannot leave the phone.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

This is the route this article is for. Plug the drive into the phone and wait for Android to detect it. Open SnapDisk, and pick the folders first — this is the step that decides whether your RAW files come along. Tap the From picker, browse to the phone's storage, and choose the folder that holds the .dng files: DCIM/Camera if that is where they land, or the camera app's own folder if they land elsewhere. The From picker is the system folder picker (verified in the repo as OpenDocumentTree), and on that route SnapDisk lists files by extension — and .dng and .raw are both in that list, verified in the repo's PhotoQuery.kt. Then pick the To folder on the drive, in the same picker. Plug the drive in, tap Backup in the notification, and the copy runs in the background: oldest-first by the date the photo was taken, with a progress notification showing percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left, plus Pause and Stop.

Why the folder route matters: the default DCIM backup reads the phone's MediaStore Images table, and that query carries no MIME filter — it asks for everything in the Images table, oldest-first. That sounds like it should include .dng files, and on a phone whose MediaStore indexes them, it does. But whether a given phone's MediaStore indexes .dng files at all is device-dependent — some phones list them, some do not — and the repo cannot promise it for your phone. The folder route does not depend on the MediaStore at all: it walks the folder you picked and copies every file whose extension is in the list, .dng and .raw included. So the honest instruction is: if your RAW files matter, pick the folder. Do not rely on the default route alone, and after the run, check the drive: open the backup folder and confirm the .dng files are there, with sizes that match the phone. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy.

Two honest limits on this route. First, the extension list is exactly that — a list. It recognises .dng and .raw, plus the usual JPEG, PNG, HEIC, and video extensions, but it does not include .tif or .tiff, and it does not include .xmp sidecar files. If your camera app writes TIFF RAWs or keeps edits in .xmp sidecars, those files are not copied on the folder route — copy them by hand in the Files app, in Method 2 below. Second, screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified: SnapDisk copies as a foreground service — the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no RAW run has ever been tested, so this article says so instead of promising it. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it. On Android 15 and later a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours, so a very long copy can be paused by Android itself, and opening the app resets the timer (WL-34).

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you would rather not install anything, the Files app does the same copy by hand — and for RAW files it has one advantage: you see exactly which files you selected. Plug the drive in, open Files by Google (or your phone's file manager), browse to the folder that holds the .dng files, long-press one, tap the rest, tap Copy, navigate to the USB drive, and tap Paste. Check the drive afterwards: open a few .dng files in a RAW viewer or on your computer, and confirm the sizes match.

What you give up is everything SnapDisk automates. There is no oldest-first order — you copy in whatever order you selected. There is no progress notification with speed and time left, no Pause and Stop, and no resume: if the drive is unplugged mid-copy, you start that file again and work out yourself which files already arrived. There is no per-file size verification, and no skipped-file list — a file that does not fit, or a copy that silently stops, fails quietly. For ten .dng files that is fine. For a thousand, the manual route is an afternoon of tapping and checking, and the one-tap route is the honest recommendation. The manual route is also the fallback for the files SnapDisk's folder route does not recognise — .tif/.tiff RAWs and .xmp sidecars: select them by hand and copy them the same way.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

Before you trust the cloud with RAW files, check what it actually holds. Open photos.google.com on a computer and look for the .dng files: if backup was off, if the device folder that holds them was never switched on, or if the uploads are "Storage saver" quality, the cloud does not hold your RAW files at all — it holds compressed copies, or nothing. Storage saver recompresses, and for a RAW file that is the damage this article exists to prevent. Original-quality upload keeps the file, but it counts against your Google storage quota at tens of megabytes a shot, and a library that fits on a $20 USB stick can cost a monthly subscription in the cloud.

The cloud is not the enemy — it is a fine second copy once the originals are safe on a drive you hold. The order matters: copy to the USB drive first, verify the .dng files on the drive, and only then let the cloud hold whatever it holds. And never delete the phone originals on the strength of a cloud badge alone. Check the drive copy, then delete — that order is what keeps your negatives safe.

The 5 GB free cap, and why it bites harder for RAW

SnapDisk's free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — verified in the repo as FREE_BACKUP_BYTES_LIMIT, 5 GiB — and runs can be repeated. Files already copied are never copied twice, so a second run picks up where the first stopped. For JPEGs, 5 GB is a whole library; for RAW, it is a few hundred shots. A thousand .dng files at 25 MB each is 25 GB — five runs, free, with each run picking up where the last stopped. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (product id pro_unlock, verified in the repo's billing code) that auto-starts the backup when a disk is connected and removes the size limit, so a whole season copies in one run.

The cap is per run, not per library, and that distinction is the whole story for RAW: the free version can finish any library, run by run, without paying — it just takes several taps. If you shoot RAW every day, Pro is the honest buy: one tap, one run, no cap. If you shoot RAW on a trip a few times a year, the free runs are enough. Either way, the count to do before you start is the library size: add up the .dng files, divide by 5 GB, and that is how many free runs the library needs. Do the arithmetic before you plug the drive in, not halfway through the copy.

One more size note: a file of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped with its reason and the job continues — verified in the repo's BackupService. A single RAW file is almost never 4 GB or more, so this almost never fires for a .dng. What it does catch is the long 4K video that sits next to your RAW files in the same folder — that file is skipped and listed, the RAW files around it copy normally, and the fix is the same exFAT format recommended above.

What this does not do

  • It does not convert, develop, or edit your RAW files. SnapDisk copies the .dng exactly as it is. Developing it into a JPEG, applying a profile, or exporting an edit is your editor's job — Lightroom, Darktable, Snapseed, or whatever you shoot with. The backup's job is to keep the negative intact.
  • It does not copy .tif/.tiff RAWs or .xmp sidecars on the folder route. The recognised extension list is .dng and .raw for RAW files. If your camera app writes TIFF RAWs or keeps edits in .xmp sidecars, copy those by hand in the Files app (Method 2).
  • It does not back up anything but photos and videos. No app data, no messages, no system state — and no Lightroom catalogues, presets, or edit history. Those live in the app's own storage, not in the photo folders, and they need their own backup.
  • It does not delete anything. SnapDisk only copies — the only delete calls in its source touch .part files, never your photos — so freeing space is a two-step flow you do yourself: copy to the drive, open a few .dng files on the drive to check they arrived, then delete the originals from your phone. That flow is covered step by step in How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive.
  • It does not promise the copy keeps running with the screen off under battery saver. No wake lock, no battery-saver handling in the repo, no RAW run ever tested — stated plainly above, not promised. Keep the phone awake for the run.
  • It does not restore or view your photos. There is no in-app viewer and no restore feature — you open the copied .dng files in a RAW viewer or on your computer yourself. The SnapDisk vs VaultDrive comparison covers what the rivals add here, and what they charge for it.

What to avoid

  • Trusting the default DCIM route for RAW without checking. Whether your phone's MediaStore indexes .dng files is device-dependent. Pick the folder with the From picker — that route recognises .dng and .raw by extension — and check the drive afterwards.
  • Letting a cloud service recompress your negatives. Google Photos "Storage saver" uploads are compressed copies, not originals. Check photos.google.com before trusting the cloud, and copy to the drive first.
  • Formatting the drive NTFS. Most Android phones cannot open NTFS on their own. Use exFAT — DriveForge's guide is the format reference — and keep FAT32 only for a drive an older device must read.
  • Buying a drive before counting the library. A thousand .dng files is 25 GB. Count first, then buy the size, and divide by 5 GB to know how many free runs the library needs.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few .dng files on the drive, check the sizes match, then delete from the phone — never the other way round.
  • Assuming the .xmp sidecars came along. They did not, on the folder route — the extension list does not include them. Copy sidecars and TIFF RAWs by hand.
  • Assuming the copy keeps running with the screen off. Unverified — no wake lock, no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  • Starting a multi-gigabyte copy on a nearly dead battery. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Forgetting the videos next to the RAW files. The same folder holds long 4K clips, and a clip of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive is skipped — listed, not failed — while the RAW files copy normally. Use exFAT and the problem goes away.
  • Searching for the wrong thing. The guides the search returns are generic photo transfer guides — file manager, PC middleman, cloud — and none of them is RAW-specific. Search for the .dng, not the photo, and the gap is obvious.

Rule of thumb: a RAW file is the negative — the copy has to be byte-for-byte, and the check has to be on the drive. Pick the folder, copy, open a few .dng files on the drive, then delete — that order is what keeps your negatives safe.
