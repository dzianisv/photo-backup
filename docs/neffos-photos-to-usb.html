Method 1: SnapDisk — proven on four

SnapDisk is the one-tap version. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which eighteen covered Neffos phones clear — and the one-tap backup is proven on four of them: each one clears the floor and lists USB OTG Yes.

  1. Install SnapDisk v0.1.2 — it is not on Google Play yet, so install the APK from the GitHub release page. You do not need an account, and there are no ads.
  2. Plug the USB drive into the phone — straight in on a Type-C phone (the N1 and the X9), or through a micro-USB OTG adapter on a micro-USB phone (the X20 Pro, and the X20 if its port turns out to be micro-USB).
  3. Open SnapDisk and tap Backup. SnapDisk copies photos and videos oldest-first from the phone to the drive, in the background, with live progress, Pause and Stop.
  4. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size. On the eight that need the mounting test — the C5s, C5A, C7s, C9A, C9s, C9 Max, X1 Lite and Y5s — the drive may or may not mount, because no source settles OTG on them: plug the drive in and see if it shows up in the app. On the six with USB OTG No — the C7, C7A, C7 Lite, C9, C5 Plus and P1 — the one-tap route does not work: use Method 3 or 4 instead. On the two contested-floor models — the X1 Max and the A5 — check the version on the unit first: if the unit shows Android 7.0 or later, Method 1 works; if it shows 6.0 or 5.1, it does not. On the six below-floor models and the two feature phones, SnapDisk does not install at all.

Method 2: file manager + OTG adapter

Same plug, no app to install. On the four proven phones it works exactly as on any other Android phone with OTG support:

  1. Plug the USB drive into the phone — straight in on a Type-C phone, or through a micro-USB OTG adapter on a micro-USB phone.
  2. Open the phone's file manager and find the DCIM folder — that is where the camera photos live.
  3. Select the photos and videos, copy them to the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish.
  4. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

On the eight that need the mounting test — the C5s, C5A, C7s, C9A, C9s, C9 Max, X1 Lite and Y5s — the drive may or may not show up in the file manager, because no source settles OTG on them. On the six with USB OTG No — the C7, C7A, C7 Lite, C9, C5 Plus and P1 — the drive will not mount at all, so this method does not work: use Method 3 or 4 instead. On the two contested-floor models — the X1 Max and the A5 — check the version on the unit first. On the six below-floor models — the C5, C5 Max, C5L, X1, Y5 and Y5L — the drive may still mount in the file manager even though SnapDisk does not install: the file-manager copy needs OTG, not Android 7.0 — but OTG is unstated on the C5 Max, C5L, X1, Y5 and Y5L, and contested on the C5, so test it on the unit. On the two feature phones there is no file manager and no Android, so this method does not work at all.

Method 3: through a computer

The fallback that works on all twenty-eight covered phones, including the ones no phone-to-USB method reaches: the six below-floor models, the two contested-floor models, the six with USB OTG No, and the two feature phones — if the phone stores photos at all.

  1. Plug the phone into the computer with a USB cable — a plain charging cable works here, because the computer is the other end.
  2. On the phone, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer.
  3. On the computer, open the phone's DCIM folder and copy the photos to the computer, then to the USB drive.
  4. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

On the two feature phones there is no Android and no stated camera, so if yours takes photos at all, copy the phone's photo folder instead of DCIM. This is the only method that works on the two feature phones.

Method 4: microSD card reader route

If the phone has a card slot — and twenty do, for certain — the card is the backup, and the reader is the bridge to the USB drive:

  1. Put a microSD card in the phone and copy the DCIM folder to the card with the file manager.
  2. Take the card out, put it in a card reader, plug the reader into the computer, and copy the photos from the card to the USB drive.
  3. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

A microSD card fits twenty phones for certain — the C5, C5 Max, C5L, C7, C7A, C7 Lite, C7s, C9, C9 Max, C9s, N1, P1, X1, X1 Lite, X1 Max, X9, X20, X20 Pro, Y5L and Y5s — so the card-reader route is proven on those twenty. Ceilings are stated on thirteen — 32 GB on the C5, C5 Max, C5L and Y5L; 64 GB on the C7A; 128 GB on the C9 Max, C9s, X1, X1 Lite, X1 Max, X20 and X20 Pro — contested on four — the C7 Lite (128 vs 256 GB), the C7s (128 vs 64 vs 32 GB), the N1 (128 vs 256 GB) and the C9 (128 vs 256 GB) — and unstated on three — the X9, Y5s and P1. The slot itself is unstated on five smartphones — the C5s, C5A, C9A, Y5 and A5 — so check the tray before buying a card. The slot is also unstated on the two feature phones, so this route is not proven on them either — use Method 3 there. A card copy is a real local copy, but the card stays in the phone until you take it out, and opening it anywhere else needs the card reader — a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.

Method 5: Google Photos, or the cloud

The cloud is the last resort, and the one Neffos itself points to: the C9 user manual says to switch on Back up to Google Drive, and Google Photos is the photo version of the same idea. It works on all twenty-six smartphones — the two feature phones have no Google Photos, so Method 3 is the route there.

  1. Open Google Photos, turn on backup, and wait for the upload to finish — this needs Wi-Fi, and a big library takes a while.
  2. On a computer, open Google Photos, download the photos, and copy them to the USB drive.
  3. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

Two warnings. First, Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost — check the setting before trusting a cloud copy. Second, a cloud backup is not a USB backup: your photos sit on Google's servers, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying for storage. If you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy — that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.

What this does not do

  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later, and six run below it as shipped — the C5, C5 Max, C5L, X1, Y5 and Y5L — so SnapDisk does not install on those. Two more are contested-floor — the X1 Max and the A5 — so check the version on the unit: if the unit shows 6.0 or 5.1, SnapDisk does not install.
  • Does not install on a feature phone. The N105 and N150 have no Android at all, so SnapDisk cannot install on those either — only the computer copy in Method 3 works on them, if the phone stores photos at all.
  • Does not work if the phone does not host a drive. Six list USB OTG No — the C7, C7A, C7 Lite, C9, C5 Plus and P1 — so on those six the one-tap route does not work at all. Eight more need the mounting test — the C5s, C5A, C7s, C9A, C9s, C9 Max, X1 Lite and Y5s — because no source settles OTG on them.
  • Does not delete photos. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into two of the twenty-eight phones — the N1 and the X9 — so an adapter is only needed for a regular USB-A drive, and it must be a USB-C OTG adapter on those two, a micro-USB OTG adapter on the thirteen micro-USB models. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. A computer copy with a USB cable moves files to a computer — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a manual page for a phone-to-USB guide. The Neffos spec pages are real Neffos pages — but they only list an Android version, a chipset, or a USB connector type. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the phone.
  • Mistaking OTG support on one model for OTG support on the other. Four phones list USB OTG Yes — but no source settles OTG on the C5s, C5A, C7s, C9A, C9s, C9 Max, X1 Lite and Y5s, the X1 Max is contested, and six list USB OTG No. Test with a drive on those nine, and use Method 3 or 4 on any model where the drive never mounts.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — twenty phones take a card — but the card stays in the phone, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 20, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Neffos phone the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.
  • Trusting one spec listing. Android is two numbers on five phones — the X1 Max at 6.0 vs 7.0, the A5 at 5.1 vs 9.0 Go, the C5 at 5.0 vs 5.1, the C7s at 8.1 vs 9.0 Go and the N1 at 7.1 vs 7.1.1 — and only the X1 Max and the A5 flip the 7.0 floor on their contest, so check the version on the unit before buying a drive expecting the one-tap backup. Launch dates are contested on four — the C5, the N1, the C5 Plus and the C7s — and devicesfaq's pages for the 2019 models carry wrong specs, so do not trust devicesfaq for the A5, the X20 or the C9 Max.

Rule of thumb: twenty-eight phones, eighteen clear Android 7.0, four list USB OTG Yes, twenty take a microSD card — the one-tap backup is proven on four, and the card-reader route is proven on twenty.
