Method 1: SnapDisk on the five — and why there is no phone route on any of them

On the five phones that clear Android 7.0, SnapDisk installs — the Speed X runs 7.0, the Flash X1 runs 7.0, the Flash X2 runs 7.0, and the P5000 and Z5000 run 7.0 — and the one-tap backup needs the phone to host a drive, which none of the five does. Gadgets360 lists USB OTG No for all five, so the honest outcome of this method is a no-mount, not a backup:

  1. Install SnapDisk. Until the Play listing is live, install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page. It installs on Android 7.0 and later, so it installs on the five phones that clear 7.0 — the Speed X, Flash X1, Flash X2, P5000 and Z5000 — and the one-tap backup to a drive works on none of them, because none of them hosts a drive. On the Flash X1, UMA Technology claims OTG is supported — but the same UMA page's Flash X2 sheet contradicts the launch reporting on nearly every field, so the Gadgets360 No stands. It does not install on the other thirty-one: they run below Android 7.0, so Method 2, 3 or 4 is the route on those.
  2. Plug in the USB drive with the right adapter. The P5000 and Z5000 take USB Type-C, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into those two, or a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. The Speed X, Flash X1 and Flash X2 take micro-USB, so a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter on any of them.
  3. Look for the mount notification. The drive should not mount on any of the five — Gadgets360 lists OTG No on all five — so there is no phone route on any of them, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. If a drive does mount on your unit despite the listing, tap Backup in the notification and SnapDisk copies the whole camera roll oldest-first in the background, showing progress, speed and ETA, with Pause and Stop — but do not buy an adapter expecting that outcome. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size.
  4. Check the copy, then delete from the phone if you need the space. SnapDisk verifies every file's size after copying, but open a few photos on the drive yourself before deleting anything.

This is the only method in this article that takes the whole library in one tap, with progress, pause, resume and verification. Its limits: it works only on phones that run Android 7.0 and host a drive — and on all five the sources agree the phone does not host one. On the thirty-one below Android 7.0, only Methods 2, 3 and 4 exist, since none of them can run SnapDisk.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 5: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Zopo offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — no Zopo-specific backup advice turned up in any source beyond a computer copy, Google Drive, or Google Photos — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and devicesfaq's Zopo FAQ says Google Drive gives 15 GB free. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 2, 3 or 4 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What this does not do

  • It does not back up your settings or your contacts. SnapDisk copies photos, videos, and any files you pick — the files themselves, exactly as they are. App settings, call logs, texts, and your accounts stay with your phone and your Google account. For those, Android's built-in backup is the tool — it backs up personal settings to Google's servers with a Google account — not SnapDisk.
  • It does not delete photos from your phone after copying. Deleting is always your choice, after you check the copy on the drive. SnapDisk never deletes originals — it only copies.
  • It does not work if your phone does not host a drive. On the five phones that clear Android 7.0 the sources agree the phone does not support OTG, so no backup app of any kind can copy your photos straight to a USB drive on any of those five — and the same holds for the Flash X Plus, the Color M5 and the Hero 2 below 7.0. If the drive never mounts, that settles it for your unit — use Method 3 or 4 instead.
  • It does not back up photos from apps that hide them. Some apps keep photos in private folders SnapDisk cannot read. Those stay on the phone — check each app's own export or share option.
  • It does not compress your photos. It copies the original files byte-for-byte, so the copy is the same size as the original. On the Speed 8, with its 21 MP camera, the P5000 and Z5000, with their 13 MP cameras and 16 MP front cameras, that is real storage on the drive — bring a drive with room.

What to avoid

  • Don't move files instead of copying them. The file manager's Move deletes the original as it goes. Copy first, check the copy on the drive, and only then delete the originals from the phone if you need the space.
  • Don't unplug the drive until the copy is done. If the copy is still running — SnapDisk shows progress, speed and ETA — unplugging leaves half-copied files. Wait for it to finish, then eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it.
  • Don't buy an adapter before checking your phone's port. Twenty-eight of the thirty-six phones take micro-USB; only the Speed 8, P5000 and Z5000 take USB Type-C. A USB-C adapter does not fit a micro-USB port — and on five models the sources do not state the connector at all, the ZP600, ZP920, Flash G5 Plus, Color C5 and ZP999 — so check your unit's port before buying anything.
  • Don't assume the drive will mount. On the five phones that clear Android 7.0 the sources agree it does not mount — all five list OTG No — so there is no phone route on any of them, and on the sixteen below 7.0 where OTG is disputed or unstated a no-mount settles it for your unit. Use Method 3 or 4 instead.
  • Don't let one folder hold every photo. SnapDisk copies oldest-first, and the copy is the same size as the original, so a full camera roll on a phone with a 21 MP camera — the Speed 8 — fills a small drive fast. Bring a drive with room, or back up in batches and check the copy before deleting anything.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a copy. A backup finds your photos, copies them all, and tells you when it's done.
