Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. It mounts on plug-in on most Android phones with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. No Hard Case model lists OTG support in the sources I found, so the drive mounting is the test itself: if it appears in Settings > Storage, the phone hosts drives and the rest of this method works; if it never appears, the phone does not host drives and Method 4 is the route. The H280S and the H242S run KaiOS and the H240S runs Android 6.0 with no Google Play, so SnapDisk cannot run on those three at all.
  2. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Energizer camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  3. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: a file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

A file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — the YouTube guides' route is an OTG connection with the built-in file manager, and askfilo's route is the same, and either is the manual route for external storage: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. 2. Open the file manager (Files by Google on Energizer phones) and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

If you want Energizer's own route, know what each tool actually does — there is no Energizer backup page pointing at a USB drive at all, and none of the stock tools writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app, and it is the stock photo backup on Energizer Hard Case Android phones: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.

If a PC or Mac is handy, the old route still works — and it is the route Energizer's own backup advice names: plug the Energizer Hard Case phone into the computer with a USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • OTG is not confirmed on any Hard Case model. No Hard Case model lists USB OTG support in the sources I found — the spec tables list USB Type-C for the H550S, the H570S, the H590S, and the H620S and microUSB 2.0 for the H500S, with no OTG mention on any of them — so confirm the drive mounts in Settings > Storage before relying on it. The only Energizer OTG listing I found is the cancelled Ultimate U620S Pop, a different line that was never sold, so it says nothing about the Hard Case phones. SnapDisk still needs Android 7.0 or later.
  • Two Hard Case models cannot run SnapDisk at all. The H280S and the H242S run KaiOS, not Android — there is no Android app to install on them, and their photos move off the phone by computer copy or microSD card instead. The H240S runs Android 6.0 with no Google Play access, below SnapDisk's Android 7.0 minimum, so it is out too.
  • The H570S specs are disputed. Gadgets360's 2018 launch story lists Android 8.0, a Helio P18, 3 GB of RAM, 32 GB of storage, microSD up to 128 GB, and a 4800 mAh battery, while phonesdata, specspro, and others list Android 9.0, a MediaTek MT6739, 2 GB of RAM, 16 GB of storage, microSD up to 32 GB, and a 6500 mAh battery, announced February 2019 — so check Settings > About phone on that model before trusting either spec set. Either way it runs Android 7.0 or later, so SnapDisk installs if the phone mounts a drive.
  • The H500S Android version is disputed. pkgsm lists Android 8.0, while mobilespecs and spec411 list Android 7.0 — so check Settings > About phone first. Either way it runs Android 7.0 or later, so SnapDisk installs if the phone mounts a drive; its port is microUSB 2.0 in every source, so it needs a micro-USB OTG adapter, not a USB-C one.
  • The H621S connector is unverified. I found the H621S runs Android 11 with 64 GB of storage, but no USB or OTG detail in the page I found — so check your own port before buying an adapter. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later, which the H621S clears.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into the H550S, the H570S, the H590S, and the H620S, which all have USB-C — so an adapter is only needed for a regular USB-A drive, and it must be a USB-C OTG adapter, not a micro-USB one. The H500S and the Energy E500S have microUSB 2.0 instead, so they need a micro-USB OTG adapter. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. And the H621S connector is unverified in my sources — check the port first.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. Energizer's own backup advice is a computer copy with a USB cable and a file browser — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a FAQ page for a phone-to-USB guide. The H550S FAQ page and the Hard Case spec tables are real Energizer pages — but the FAQ only says to connect the phone to a computer and copy the files with a file browser, and the spec tables only list a USB connector type with no OTG mention. Neither of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the phone.
  • Mistaking the Ultimate U620S Pop's OTG listing for a Hard Case listing. GSMArena lists "USB Type-C 2.0, OTG" for the Ultimate U620S Pop — but that phone was cancelled, it is from the Ultimate line, not the Hard Case line, and it was never sold. It says nothing about whether a Hard Case phone hosts a drive. Test with a drive.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — the H550S takes a card up to 32 GB, the H500S takes one up to 64 GB, and the H240S takes one up to 32 GB — but the card stays in the phone, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On Energizer the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Installing SnapDisk on a Hard Case phone that cannot run it. The H280S and the H242S run KaiOS, not Android, and the H240S runs Android 6.0 with no Google Play access — SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later and cannot install on any of the three. Check Settings > About phone before installing; every other Hard Case model I found runs Android 7.0, 8.0, 9.0, 10, or 11.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
