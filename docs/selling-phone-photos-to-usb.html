What selling or trading in actually requires

Selling a phone is not one tap. It is a short list of steps, done in order, and the order is the whole job — because the last step erases the phone, and the phone is gone after that:

  • The backup comes first, and it has to finish. A factory reset erases everything on the phone, as Google's support page states plainly — start with the backup, never the wipe. The ezznology guide says it the same way: back up before anything else. A backup that is still running when the phone is wiped or handed over is not a backup.
  • A factory reset before hand-over is not optional. Trade-in, buyback, and carrier-return programs ask for the phone reset before hand-over, and Google Pixel and Samsung trade-in rules say the reset has to run from the Settings app, not from recovery mode — the androidphonesblog guide names both. A phone handed over still signed in is a phone the buyer cannot set up.
  • The accounts come off before the reset, not after it. After a factory reset, Android asks for the Google account that was on the phone before — Factory Reset Protection. If you do not remove the account first, the buyer is asked for your password at setup, and the phone can lock them out of a device they just paid for. Remove every Google account in Settings, Passwords and accounts, and sign out of the Samsung account too if the phone has one — the digitaltrends guide lists both, and the androidphonesblog guide says removing them disarms FRP so the phone boots to a clean setup wizard.
  • The SIM, the microSD card, and the eSIM leave with you. Take out the SIM card and any microSD card before handing the phone over — the ezznology guide warns that a factory reset does not wipe a microSD card, and the card can still hold your photos and documents. For an eSIM, check the erase-downloaded-SIMs option at reset time when the phone is being traded in or sold.
  • Any on-device copy is not a backup. A second folder on the same phone, a copy in a different app on the same phone, a photo in an app's private storage on the same phone — all of it is internal storage, and the reset wipes internal storage. A backup only counts when it lives on a different device: the USB drive, the cloud, or a computer.
  • The cloud copy only counts if it actually uploaded. Google Photos backup can be off, it can cover the camera folder only, and it can be set to storage saver, which uploads compressed copies instead of the originals. Seeing a photo in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Method 3 is the check.
  • The wipe cannot wait, and it cannot be undone. Once it runs, there is no pause, no resume, and no recovery of what it erased — and once the phone is handed over, there is no getting it back to check. That is why the order is fixed: copy, check, remove accounts, wipe, hand over — never wipe, then check.
  • The buyer checks the phone before leaving. The ezznology guide ends the same way this article does: power the phone on before the buyer leaves — it should show the setup welcome screen, with no request for your account. If it asks for your Google account, the accounts did not come off, and the sale is not done.

None of this is fixed by wiping first and sorting it out after — after is too late, and after the hand-over the phone is not yours to fix. The fix is a copy that finishes before the wipe starts: oldest-first, verified on the drive, and only then the accounts off, the wipe, and the hand-over.

What you need

  • An Android phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk).
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. A spinning hard drive may need its own power.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A phone about to be sold often holds years of photos, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • Your Google account password — written down or known, not guessed after the wipe. You need it to sign out cleanly, and to remove the phone from your trusted devices afterwards.
  • A charger, if you have one — a big library is a long copy, and the phone powers the USB drive while it copies. Charge first if you can, and keep the phone plugged in during the copy if the drive is not using the port — or use a USB-C hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Wi-Fi, if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3 — the drive copy itself needs none of it. SnapDisk has no internet permission at all.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do not wipe until the check is done, and do not hand the phone over until the wipe is done.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the phone will keep filling up until the sale — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job at a time. The phone's photos need only the drive; the Google account password needs only you, before the wipe.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start. One honest limit first: the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no selling-prep run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified.

  1. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos are the ones a sale puts most at risk.
  2. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  3. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  4. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  5. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  6. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  7. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it. The backup has to complete before the wipe, and a wipe cannot wait for a backup. If the copy is interrupted, go to Method 4's resume step first — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  8. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  9. Unplug the drive. A factory reset erases the phone's internal storage, not a USB drive — but unplug it before wiping anyway, because the wipe is the moment a mistake costs the most.
  10. Only now, remove the accounts and wipe the phone — Method 4, in that order. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and before a sale it is the wipe that does it anyway.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A phone about to be sold often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every phone-to-USB guide above describes — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when the phone is already too full or too broken to install:

  1. Plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  2. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  3. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  4. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder the wipe erases.
  5. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The techsith guide says so plainly: keep the phone unlocked while connected. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  7. Unplug the drive, and only then remove the accounts and wipe the phone — Method 4, in that order.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and a wipe is coming, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. The Facebook reel's version of the same warning is the reason its Smart Switch backup keeps the originals until the backup completes. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step the wipe-before-selling guides all describe, and the step most people skip — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. The check takes five minutes and it is the difference between a sale that keeps your photos and a sale that strands them:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use on the new device.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and sell now.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones the wipe erases.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the account you are about to sign out of — which is exactly the account a sale asks you to remove.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, before the sale — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round.

Method 4: after the backup — verify, remove accounts, then wipe

This is the step the order exists for, and it is the step where the one-tap version and the manual version split apart — because the wipe has not happened yet, and the copy can still be fixed:

  1. If the copy was interrupted, resume it before wiping. Charge the phone a little if it needs it, plug the USB drive back in, reopen SnapDisk, and tap Resume. The copy continues from where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — verified in the repo, where a paused copy resumes the same file, byte-exact, from its partial file.
  2. One more limit, also verified: on Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours, so a very long copy can be paused by Android itself. Opening the app resets the timer — which is what you just did by reopening it — so the resume works.
  3. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good. Count the folders on the drive against the folders on the phone — a folder you never copied is a folder the wipe erases.
  4. Unplug the drive. The wipe erases the phone, not the drive — but the drive leaves the port before the wipe runs.
  5. Remove the accounts. In Settings, Passwords and accounts, remove every Google account — secondary accounts first, then the primary — and sign out of the Samsung account too if the phone has one. Removing the accounts disarms Factory Reset Protection, so the buyer is not asked for your password at setup.
  6. Take the SIM card, the microSD card, and the eSIM out or off. A factory reset does not wipe a microSD card — take the card out before handing the phone over, and check the erase-downloaded-SIMs option at reset time when the phone is being traded in or sold.
  7. Wipe the phone from Settings. Settings, then System, then Reset options, then Erase all data — the exact path varies by phone maker. Run the wipe from the Settings app, not from recovery mode: Google Pixel and Samsung trade-in rules say the Settings reset is the one for a trade-in, and the androidphonesblog guide says the same.
  8. Power the phone on before the buyer leaves. It should show the setup welcome screen, with no request for your account — the ezznology guide ends the same way. If it asks for your Google account, the accounts did not come off, and the sale is not done.
  9. After the hand-over, remove the old phone from your trusted devices. Sign in from another device and remove it from the account's trusted-devices list — the ezznology guide's last step, and the one that closes the account side of the sale.

If you were copying by hand in the Files app, there is no resume to tap: re-select the photos and copy again, and open the file that was in flight when the copy stopped and check it before trusting it — an interrupted copy before a sale is exactly the interruption that leaves a file incomplete on the drive. The manual copy's answer to an interrupted copy is to start over; SnapDisk's answer is to resume.

What this does not do

  • Does not undo a wipe or a hand-over. Once the reset runs, the photos on the phone are gone — there is no un-reset, no recycle bin, and no recovery of what the wipe erased. Once the phone is handed over, there is no getting it back to check. The backup has to finish before the wipe, not after it.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Back those up in Settings, Google, Backup, before the sale too — and export 2FA tokens and transit passes separately, because a reset erases those with everything else.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the sale, not after it.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no selling-prep run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not remove your accounts for you. Factory Reset Protection asks for the account that was on the phone before the reset — removing the Google account and the Samsung account is your step, in Method 4, before the wipe. If you skip it, the buyer can be locked out of the phone they just paid for.
  • Does not take the SIM or microSD card out for you. A factory reset does not wipe a microSD card — take the card out before handing the phone over, or the buyer gets your photos with the phone.
  • Does not fix a phone that will not boot. If the phone will not turn on, no software route exists — check the cloud, then repair.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only. On an iPhone the before-sale route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive, or iCloud — and the same cloud trap applies: check that the photos actually uploaded before wiping.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Wiping before the backup finishes. The backup has to complete and be checked before the wipe — a reset erases the phone including any on-device copy, and there is no undo. Copy, check, remove accounts, wipe, hand over — never wipe, then check.
  • Handing the phone over with your accounts still on it. Factory Reset Protection asks the next owner for your Google account password. Remove every Google account and sign out of the Samsung account before the wipe, and power the phone on before the buyer leaves to check it shows the setup welcome screen.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder the wipe erases.
  • Leaving the microSD card in the phone. A factory reset does not wipe a microSD card. Take the card out before handing the phone over — and the SIM card too.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and a wipe is coming, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then wipe.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then wipe — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Leaving the drive plugged in during the wipe. A factory reset erases the phone, not the drive — but unplug it before wiping anyway, because the wipe is the moment a mistake costs the most.
  • Starting the manual copy on a big library. The manual copy needs the phone unlocked for the whole transfer, so the screen stays on, and it has no resume — on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, an interrupted copy means re-selecting everything. The background copy with resume is the before-a-sale route — that is Method 1, not Method 2.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a phone about to be sold often holds years of photos. Check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole library in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole library is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.

Rule of thumb: a sale cannot be undone, so the copy comes first and the hand-over comes last — copy the whole library oldest-first, check a few photos on the drive, remove the accounts, wipe the phone from Settings, and only then hand it over; and check the cloud on another device, because the Photos app showing a photo is not proof it uploaded.
