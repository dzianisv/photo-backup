Save MEGA photos to USB from Android

Your photos on MEGA live on MEGA's encrypted servers, not on your phone. Download each one in the app — then find it in Downloads — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 14 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see in MEGA is not on your phone yet. MEGA is encrypted file storage that lives on MEGA's servers, not a folder on your phone: you can see the photo, open it, preview it, share its link, mark it available offline, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you download it. In the MEGA app on Android, tap Drive on the bottom navigation menu, tap the three-dot icon to the right of the file or folder, and tap Download — that is MEGA's own help, word for word — and the file lands in your default download location, which you can see and change under Menu, Settings, Download location, Default download location. Which folder that is, exactly, MEGA does not name on its help page: the independent guides report the Downloads folder, so check Downloads first, then Pictures, then the Gallery's recent view, and the folder-check section below walks the whole search in order. From wherever the file landed, the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the downloaded MEGA photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: MEGA downloads nothing by itself — no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and the app's automatic features run the other way, sending your phone photos up to MEGA, not bringing account photos down to your phone; previewing a photo is not downloading it, and a previewed file lands in the app-private folder /Android/data/mega.privacy.android.app, where no file manager and no backup app can reach it; a downloaded photo sorts by the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken, because the file is written when you download it; MEGA meters downloads on free accounts and publishes no fixed number — commonly reported around 5 GB per six-hour window — so a big library cannot be pulled in one sitting; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The good news, and it is real: a MEGA download is the original file as you uploaded it — MEGA stores files, it does not re-encode photos — so what you back up is the full file, not a served copy. The confusion first: MEGA is encrypted file storage — and Download is not Offline

MEGA sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. MEGA is encrypted file storage — files and folders on MEGA's servers, protected by end-to-end encryption, where every file is encrypted on your device with a key derived from your password and MEGA's servers see only ciphertext — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like a photo library, it behaves like a photo library when you open a photo in it, and it is not your library. It is storage on MEGA's servers, and the photos in it are files that live there, not on your phone. A photo library on your phone — the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. MEGA shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your MEGA account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into MEGA either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no cloud account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of MEGA and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a download. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion MEGA's own design invites. MEGA is where people keep their photos because the free plan is generous — 20 GB of encrypted storage, free, with more available through achievements — and the account feels like an archive: your files, your folders, your shared links, all in the order you put them in, all yours, all encrypted so that nobody but you can read them. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy on someone else's server, under someone else's account rules, and it is a free account behind a transfer quota as well as behind a login: MEGA meters downloads, and a free account's downloads are limited by how much your address has downloaded over the past six hours. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits in an account that can be suspended after months of inactivity is a photo at risk twice over: MEGA's terms allow it to suspend a free account or remove its data after a long stretch of inactivity, and its help centre says the process takes at least 10 months from your last activity — so an account you forget about is an account that can quietly empty itself. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the cloud is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

The third confusion hides inside MEGA itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. Downloading a file copies it from MEGA's servers to a folder on your phone that other apps can open. Making a file available offline keeps a copy inside the MEGA app, so you can view it later without a connection — and that copy stays inside the app, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and gone from your reach the moment you clear the app's data or uninstall it. Previewing a file is thinner still: opening a photo to look at it downloads it for viewing into the app-private folder /Android/data/mega.privacy.android.app, and MEGA's own help says so plainly — previewing unsupported file types downloads them there, and to land a file in the default destination you must use the Download menu option instead. Sharing a file's link is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the file remaining available in the account. The only tap that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive is the three-dot menu's Download — and if you have been previewing photos and wondering why none of them are in the Downloads folder, this is why: you have been looking at photos, and looked-at photos are not downloads.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on MEGA's servers. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The download is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the download is a step you have to take yourself, because MEGA offers no download-them-all setting and no auto-download. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the download-one-and-check rule, because for MEGA the date problem is a download problem: the file is written when you download it, and the day you downloaded it is the day it sorts by. And the one thing this article will not do, which the other guides do without saying so, is invent a folder: MEGA names none as the default, so the folder section below is a search plus a setting to check, not a claim.

The wall: MEGA downloads nothing by itself — the download is yours to do

MEGA does not put your account photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the download describes a manual step — tap Drive, tap the three dots, tap Download — because the manual step is the only step there is. MEGA's settings hold nothing about downloading to the phone automatically: the app has a download-location setting, which chooses where downloads land, and no auto-download toggle, and no sync-to-device option. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache and in the app-private folder, which saves data when you scroll back over photos you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is MEGA's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's data is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you download it, and you download it one photo at a time — or one selection at a time, with the multi-select described below.

The download itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because MEGA's own help states it in the same words this article uses. Open the MEGA app. Tap Drive on the bottom navigation menu. Tap the three-dot icon to the right of a file or folder. Tap Download. That is MEGA's own help centre, first-hand, fetched live this run, updated November 3, 2026: "How do I download a file or folder from MEGA?" In some app versions the option may be labelled Save to device instead of Download — one independent guide notes the label varies — so look for the download arrow or the word Download if the menu shows a different label. The same help page lists where you can download from: your own Cloud drive, incoming shared folders, file and folder links, and files shared directly within chats on MEGA. A shared link is a download source like any other: tap the link, open it in the app, and download from there — no account needed for the link itself, though the transfer quota still applies to downloads without an account.

There is one more thing the download does not let you choose, and it is worth knowing before you download a hundred photos, because it decides where the files land — or rather, it decides that you choose it once, in settings, and the download obeys it. MEGA's app settings hold a download-location choice: Menu at the bottom right, Settings, Download location, Default download location. That is MEGA's own help again, from the "Where are my downloads?" page, and it is the setting to check before you download anything: open it, look at what it says, and you will know where every download is going. The independent guides report the Downloads folder as the usual destination — one reports a MEGA Downloads subfolder when downloading through Chrome — but the setting is the authority on your phone, not the guide, and the exact folder varies by device, Android version, and the current MEGA app build. That is a limit, and it is also the reason the next section exists: the folder is a setting to check plus a search, not a claim. Do not waste an evening looking for a download-location setting in a photo app. In MEGA there is one, and this paragraph just told you where it is.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the download sometimes silently does nothing. The MEGA app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the download tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first download, open the Gallery and look for the photo. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every download after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission in Settings, Apps, MEGA, Permissions, Photos and videos, and download the photo again. Do not download fifty photos and check at the end. Download one, check the folder, then download the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the MEGA app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the MEGA account, because the photos live in that account, and a shared link is the one case where no sign-in is needed at all. Enough free space on the phone to hold the downloads: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, download in batches and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next — and remember the quota while you are at it, because a batch that hits the transfer limit stops until the window resets. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the download steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a download guide.

One more check, on the MEGA side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can download. If the photo is in a folder you can open, it is a photo you can try to download. If it is from a link that expired, or the file was deleted, or it sits in a shared folder you were removed from, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be downloaded, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. And if the Download option is missing entirely, that is usually one of two things, not a bug: the photo is already on the device in some form, or the photo is shared with you in a way that does not include downloading — the next sections cover both. Do not download first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then tap the three-dot menu. A download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Download one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the MEGA app on your Android phone and sign in. Find the photo — in your Cloud drive, in a folder, in a shared folder, or through a link — and open it full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a download of a thumbnail is a download of a thumbnail. Go back to the file list, tap the three-dot icon to the right of the file, and tap Download. If you want more than one, press and hold a file to select it, then tap the others to build a selection, and use the download option in the toolbar to save them together. Then find the file — and finding it is a step of its own, because MEGA names no single default folder. Open the Gallery first: if the photo is there, the download worked, whatever folder it sits in. If it is not in the Gallery, open Files by Google and look in Downloads — Internal storage, Download — then in Pictures. One of those places holds it on almost every phone, and the folder-check section below walks the search in order, with the reason each place is worth checking. And if it is in neither place, check the setting itself — Menu, Settings, Download location, Default download location — because the setting is the authority on where the file went.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the folder the file landed in is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo the Gallery shows is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of Downloads or Pictures, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where MEGA put it. The default run, described in the section after next, reads the media index with no folder filter, and Downloads and Pictures are indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo in MEGA if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is a file on the phone, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the downloaded MEGA photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the downloaded batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo — or one selection — at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one folder, the photos from one trip. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand press-hold-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done — and on a free MEGA account the quota will stop you long before the tapping does. For a whole folder, use the multi-select route in Method 2, which gets the folder in one selection instead of one tap at a time. For a whole account, use Method 3, which works through the folders in quota-sized batches instead of one selection at a time. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what was shared — MEGA offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Download option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the photo is already on the device: search the Gallery and the Files app for the photo before anything else — it may already be a file, and the job may already be half done. Second, check that you are logged in to the right account: the photos live in the account you signed in with, and a second MEGA account holds a different drive. Third, update the MEGA app: the menu wording varies between app versions — Download in current versions, Save to device in some others — and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Fourth, try the folder route instead of the photo route: open the folder, select the photos inside it, and download the selection — MEGA's own help gives the folder the same three-dot Download as a single file. Fifth, open the photo on the web instead of the app: sign in on the MEGA website in Chrome, tap the three-dot menu to the right of the file, and tap Download, and the file arrives in the browser's Downloads folder. Sixth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, MEGA, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and download again. One of those six fixes the download in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, before you download the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the folder the download landed in — Downloads or Pictures — select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole folder — multi-select in the app, or the web download

MEGA is blunt about the one bulk step the app does offer, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the step every other cloud in this series offers in some form and MEGA offers in its own: the app downloads a file or a folder, and a folder download is one tap on the folder itself. MEGA's own help says it outright — in the Android app, tap Drive, tap the three-dot icon to the right of a file or folder, tap Download — and the same three steps cover a folder as a single file. For a folder of twenty photos it is one minute. For a folder of five hundred it is one tap plus a wait, and the wait is the quota, not the tap — use the batch-size advice below, and check the folder before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

For a batch on the web, the route is the browser, and it is worth following exactly, because the web route has two traps — the ZIP and the Downloads folder — that decide whether it works on a phone at all. Sign in on the MEGA website in Chrome, open the folder or the drive view, tap the three-dot menu to the right of a file and tap Download — or tap the three-dot menu to the right of a folder and tap Download as a ZIP file. The files arrive in the browser's Downloads folder, on the phone, in Internal storage/Download. When a folder is downloaded together, MEGA compresses it into a ZIP archive before downloading — that is the first trap, and it is a real one: SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives, so a ZIP sitting in Downloads is a file the backup will skip, and the photos inside it are still zipped. Open the ZIP in the Files app and tap Extract first, and back up the files it contains. The second trap is the folder: the web download lands in Downloads, not in Pictures and not in DCIM, and a reader who looks for the batch in the Gallery's Pictures folder will not find it until the media index picks the extracted files up — open one extracted photo once, which prompts the media scanner, or use the From picker route, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all.

The web route is also the route for the photos the app will not download, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the route for the edge cases. If a photo is in a folder someone shared with you, the web view shows the same folder, and the same three-dot Download works from there — but check each item, because sharing is per folder and per link, and a file shared with you for viewing is not automatically a file you can download: check what the share allows before assuming the selection is yours to take. If a photo is in a folder link someone sent you, the same rule holds: the link shows you the file, and the download is a separate permission the owner controls. And if the Download option is missing for a single photo in the app but present on the web, the web is the fix — the app and the web do not always agree on what is already on the device, and the web download writes a fresh file either way.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. The download is the photos you selected, not the folder as an object: the folder name, the folder order, and the folder membership do not travel with the files, so keep the folder context yourself — a dated folder on the drive, named for the MEGA folder, is the simplest way, and the checking section below says so again, because it is the step people skip. The ZIP is prepared by the browser, and the download arrives when it is ready, not when you ask — select, download, wait, unzip, and then back up the files it contains. Each time more photos are added to the folder, you need to download again, or the new photos will not be in the batch. And the quota is the limit that decides the batch size: a free account's downloads are metered over a rolling six-hour window, so a folder of 4 GB downloads in one sitting and a folder of 40 GB does not — work through the folders in quota-sized batches, copy each batch to the drive, and come back for the next. For ten photos, download them in the app. For a whole folder, use multi-select in the app, or the web download. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Downloads folder — or the unzipped batch folder — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole account — what MEGA does and does not export

For a whole account, the honest answer is that MEGA has no one-tap whole-account export in the app, and this article says so instead of inventing one. There is no settings page that zips your entire drive, no download-all button that compiles everything you ever uploaded, and no archive that arrives by email with all your folders in it. The export unit in the app is the file or the folder: one photo at a time, one selection at a time, or one folder at a time. If you have ten folders, that is ten downloads. If you have a hundred, that is a hundred — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

The route that does exist for a whole account is the folder-by-folder download in quota-sized batches, and it is worth doing in order, because the order is what keeps it from becoming a mess. Start with the folders that matter most — the photos with no other copy anywhere — and work down the list. For each folder, download it in the app or on the web, unzip the batch if it arrived as a ZIP, and back up the files it contains before downloading the next folder — unzip first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives. Keep each folder's files in their own dated folder on the drive, so the drive ends up with the folders in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower than a single export would be, and it is the route that exists. The app's multi-select is the faster route for a single folder, and the web download is the route for the folders the app will not take. Use each one for the job it fits.

For the whole account at once, the route that exists is the computer: MEGAsync, MEGA's desktop app, syncs a whole MEGA drive to a folder on a computer, and that is the honest boundary of this article — it is the computer route, and this article is the no-computer route, so it names the boundary instead of pretending the phone can do the same job. If you have a computer, even borrowed for an afternoon, MEGAsync plus a USB drive is the fastest way to empty a MEGA account: install it, sign in, let it sync, and copy the synced folder to the drive. If you do not have a computer, the phone route is the folder-by-folder download, in quota-sized batches, and the honest time to budget is days, not an evening — a free account moves roughly 5 GB per six-hour window at best, so a 20 GB free account takes a day at the outside and a larger account takes a week of coming back. Do not leave the job until the account lapses: MEGA's terms allow it to remove a free account's data after a long stretch of inactivity, and the time to take the photos out is while the account is in good standing, not after the limit bites.

Two facts about the account downloads are worth stating because they decide who they work for. First, they are for photos you can download: your own photos, and other people's photos where the sharing allows it. They do not provide an export for someone else's account, and they do not include photos you viewed but did not download — a library of views is not a library you can export. Second, the download is not locked to you: a file you download is a file, and a file can be copied, shared, and forwarded by anyone who holds it, so keep the drive somewhere safe and do not forward a batch in a group chat and assume only the people you meant will open it. If the photos need protection, the protection is the drive in your drawer, not the account on MEGA's server.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what was shared. MEGA offers no export for an account you do not control, no download-a-whole-drive button, and no save-them-all in the app beyond a single file or folder. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take a username or a folder link and fetch the files — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them, because MEGA itself warns against them in the same help page that gives the download steps: the tools are not secure, they do not fully implement MEGA's cryptographic protocols, they can cause issues with accessing your account even to the point of locking it up, they cannot detect your quota, and they have not been approved or authorised by MEGA. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: download the photos you actually want one by one, or the whole folder where the sharing allows it, or ask the person for the files. A thousand one-by-one downloads is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a downloaded MEGA photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a downloaded photo copies with the rest of the library, whatever folder MEGA put it in — Downloads, Pictures, or any other shared folder. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the folder the MEGA download landed in.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: download the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in Downloads and then in Pictures: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into MEGA. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask MEGA for anything. A photo still on MEGA's servers is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on MEGA's servers is invisible to the file manager. The download is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at MEGA; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up MEGA photos without downloading them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Downloads folder came from MEGA, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the MEGA route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the download that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the MEGA photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the folder the downloads landed in — Downloads, or Pictures — and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Downloads or Pictures folder for MEGA downloads yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on these exact folders — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a downloaded photo shows the download date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo downloaded from MEGA shows the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a downloaded file is the download time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a downloaded MEGA photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you downloaded it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For MEGA, the taken date has a better chance than for the other clouds, and the reason is the quality section below: a MEGA download is the original file as uploaded, byte for byte, so the taken date recorded inside the file survives the download — the file is the same file, and the date inside it is the same date. But the date inside the file and the date the media index records are two different things, and the index is what the default run sorts by: the scanner may read the taken date out of the file and record it, or it may record the download date, and no source this run tested which one a given phone does after an in-app download. This article states download-date order as the expected case, not as a certainty, because the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app — the file is written by the download, at download time, and its modified date is the download date — while noting the one way MEGA differs: the original file survives, so the taken date is still inside it. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: download one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you download a hundred of them.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. MEGA names the downloaded file itself, and two downloads of the same photo — or two photos MEGA named the same way — can arrive in the Downloads folder under the same name, so the second download either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo in MEGA if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to download a batch and copy it to the drive before downloading the next, or to rename the files in the Downloads folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to download.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Download one folder's photos, back up the Downloads folder, clear it, download the next folder's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the folders in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the downloaded copy is the original file — and the quota is the limit

A photo downloaded from MEGA is the photographer's original file. That is the one place where MEGA is better than every other cloud in this series, and it is worth stating plainly, because it changes the whole quality question. MEGA is file storage, not a photo service: it stores the file you uploaded, encrypted, byte for byte, and a download returns that same file. There is no served copy, no resized version, no size menu to get wrong — the app download offers no size choice because there is no size to choose. The downloaded copy is the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app download is the right tool, and this article says so instead of sending you to the web for a quality the app already gives you.

The limit, for MEGA, is not the quality but the quota. MEGA meters downloads, and it does not publish a fixed number for free accounts: the limit is based on how much data your address has downloaded over the past six hours, and it varies with your location and current network load. The number the independent guides report, again and again, is around 5 GB per six-hour window — which works out to roughly 20 GB a day at best — and the free plan itself holds 20 GB of storage. Uploading uses storage but not transfer quota, so the quota bites only on the way out, which is exactly the direction this article needs. The practical rule is to size the job against the quota first: a folder under 5 GB downloads in one sitting, a folder over it does not, and the download that hits the limit stops until the window resets — the app will tell you the transfer quota is exceeded, and the fix is to wait, not to tap harder. Do not treat the quota as a reason to hand your account to a downloader that claims to bypass it: MEGA itself warns that those tools cannot detect your quota and often experience bandwidth issues, on top of the security warning in the section above.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because MEGA is file storage that holds videos too — and videos are the one place the quota bites hardest. The app download covers videos the same way it covers photos: open the video in the MEGA app, use the three-dot menu and Download, and the file lands on the phone — MEGA's own help says "a photo or video you want to save" in the same breath. The backup covers it from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, so a downloaded photo and a downloaded video both copy with the default run. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: download the video, open the folder, and look at what arrived. If it plays, it downloaded as a video; if it arrived as a single still frame, the download caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, download it again and check what arrived before you download the next twenty. And remember the quota while you are at it: a video-heavy folder fills a six-hour window long before a photo folder does, and a download of a video you already have on the phone is a download you did not need.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: download one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the downloaded copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before downloading — wait for the photo to load crisp before you tap the three-dot menu, because a download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the downloaded file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload. And check the metadata before you delete the cloud copy: a download should be checked for metadata, edits, and motion-photo packaging before the cloud copy is deleted — and a motion photo may arrive as a still, and the motion is gone for good if the cloud copy goes first.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the download is worth doing at all. A photo downloaded from MEGA is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the quota: the downloaded copy is the original, and no backup step will put the missing photos back if the quota stopped the download halfway. Download the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. If the quota stopped the batch, wait for the window to reset and download the rest — and back up each batch before downloading the next, so a stopped batch is a finished batch, not a lost one.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest download, a middle one, and the most recent download. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Downloads and Pictures folders on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the folders hold the photos plus whatever else landed there — but the photo count should match the number you downloaded, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second download overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the folder before the backup ever saw it. Download one batch at a time and copy each batch before downloading the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the MEGA batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a downloaded photo shows the download date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the download, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, download that photo again, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in the download, not at upload — and because a MEGA download is the original file, a date that does not survive is worth one more check in the file itself before you give up on it.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your MEGA login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The download needs no third party at all: the app's three-dot menu does the single photo, the web download does the batch, and MEGAsync does the account on a computer. A tool that asks for your MEGA password to download a shared folder is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours — the same account that holds your files, your recovery key, and your encrypted storage, not just your photos. MEGA itself warns that third-party downloading software is not secure, does not fully implement its cryptographic protocols, can cause issues with accessing your account even to the point of locking it up, cannot detect your quota, and has not been approved or authorised by MEGA. The in-app download needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not preview photos and expect them on the drive. Previewing a file writes it to the app-private folder /Android/data/mega.privacy.android.app, where no file manager and no backup app can reach it — MEGA's own help says so, and tells you to use the Download menu option to land the file in the default destination instead. Previewing is looking, not downloading, and a photo you only previewed is a photo that was never on the phone. The same goes for making a file available offline: the copy stays inside the MEGA app, and the app's storage is not a file in shared storage. Download the photo with the three-dot menu, check the Gallery, and then back it up. They are three different taps for three different things, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing new in the Downloads folder — because the photos were never downloaded, only viewed.

Do not download a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand press-hold-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done — and on a free MEGA account the quota will stop you first. Download the handful you actually want in the app, use multi-select or the folder download for a folder, and work through your own folders one by one in quota-sized batches. The app download is fast and the web download is fiddly, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the download to reach someone else's account: it is for photos you own or that were shared with download rights, and it will not contain your views either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the cloud as the only copy. MEGA is encrypted storage on someone else's server, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can go over quota, and a free account that sits inactive for months can have its data removed under MEGA's terms. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the cloud is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
