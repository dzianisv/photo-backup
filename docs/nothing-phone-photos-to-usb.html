What you need

A Nothing phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk) that supports OTG — check your model's specs before buying anything; a USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into any Nothing phone, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter; and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Three Nothing specifics: first, OTG is not a given on every model. Nothing phones run Nothing OS — a near-stock Android skin — and the drive mounts on plug-in on models that support OTG: look for a "USB storage added" or "USB drive connected" notification. Spec tables list OTG for the Phone (1), Phone (2), Phone (2a), Phone (2a) Plus, and Phone (3a) — but the CMF Phone 1 is disputed: GSMArena lists its USB as Type-C with no OTG, one comparison site lists OTG as not supported, and another lists it as supported — so check your own phone before buying an adapter. If the phone ignores the drive, check Settings > Storage. Second, Nothing OS ships Google's apps, so the stock file manager is Files by Google, which handles the phone's internal storage and connected external storage — and Nothing camera photos live in DCIM/Camera — and only the CMF Phone 1 has a microSD slot; the main Phone models have none, so a USB drive is the plug-in storage for them. Third, Nothing phones ship with Google Play, so SnapDisk installs from the v0.1.2 APK on the GitHub release page today and from Play once the listing is live — there is no sideload-only situation here. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 2: Files by Google (the stock route, no app needed)

Nothing phones ship with Files by Google as the stock file manager, and it can copy your photos to the USB drive: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage. 2. Open Files and confirm the USB drive shows up under external storage. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until Files says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: Nothing's own tools (Google Photos and Google backup — not phone-to-USB)

If you want Nothing's own route, know what each tool actually does — Nothing has no own cloud backup for photos; the routes on a Nothing phone are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app, and the gallery app on Nothing OS 2.x: open Photos, tap your profile picture, Photos settings, Backup, and turn Backup on — it automatically backs up photos to your Google account, with 15 GB free with every Google account. On Nothing OS 3.0 and later the gallery app is Nothing Gallery instead — a clean media viewer for photos and videos, not a backup tool — and Google Photos is still the backup route. Either way it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead. Google backup is the route Nothing's guides point to first: Settings > Google > Backup, Back up by Google One on — it backs the phone up to your Google account so you can restore app settings and data to a new phone. It is a real backup — but the backup lives in Google's cloud and restores to a phone, not a folder of photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. And the SD-card route copies DCIM to a memory card with Files — a real local copy, but only the CMF Phone 1 has a microSD slot, and the card stays in the phone (or needs a card reader to open anywhere else), not a USB drive you can plug into any computer. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 17, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • No OTG, no USB backup. On a model without working OTG — the CMF Phone 1's OTG support is unconfirmed, with spec tables disagreeing — a USB drive may simply never appear, and no app can change that. The cloud routes (Google Photos, Google backup) are the backup for those phones.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter before checking OTG. Not every Nothing phone is confirmed to have OTG — the CMF Phone 1's OTG support is disputed between spec tables. Check your own model's specs first, or the drive will never appear no matter what you plug in.
  • Hunting for an OTG switch that does not exist. Nothing OS mounts the drive on plug-in with a "USB storage added" notification on models that support OTG — there is no OTG toggle to turn on. The toggle advice in general OTG guides is for OnePlus, OPPO, Realme, and Vivo phones, not Nothing. If your Nothing phone ignores a drive, check Settings > Storage — but a missing switch is not proof the phone is broken; some models simply have no working OTG.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google backup and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. Drag-and-drop through a computer works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop tool.
  • Mistaking an SD-card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a memory card is a real local copy — but only the CMF Phone 1 has a microSD slot, the card stays in the phone, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader. A USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 17, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On Nothing the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google backup — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
