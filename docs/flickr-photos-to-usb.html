Save Flickr photos to USB from Android

Your photos on Flickr live on Flickr's servers, not on your phone. Save each one in the app — it lands in the Flickr folder — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 13 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see on Flickr is not on your phone yet. Flickr is a photo library that lives on Flickr's servers, not a folder on your phone: you can see the photo, open it, fave it, and share its link, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you save it, one photo at a time. In the Flickr app on Android, open the photo, tap the share icon — or the three dots, depending on the version — and tap Save, and the photo lands in a folder called Flickr, visible in your Gallery. That is the whole first half of the job, and it is the half every other guide skips: the generic Android-to-USB guides start from photos already on the phone, and the Flickr guides that do exist stop at the phone — save to the phone, done — with no next step to a drive. From the Flickr folder the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the saved Flickr photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: Flickr saves nothing by itself — no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and a fave keeps the photo inside your account, not a file, so if the photographer deletes the photo your fave points at nothing; a saved photo sorts by the day you saved it, not the day it was taken, because Flickr serves you its own copy and the taken date does not survive it; the saved copy is Flickr's served copy, not the photographer's original file, and the original lives behind the size menu on the web; the app saves one photo at a time and offers no bulk download, so a whole account goes through the web Camera Roll batches or the data request instead; an owner can switch downloads off for a photo, and then there is no Save to tap; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The confusion first: Flickr is a photo library on someone else's server — and Save is not a fave

Flickr sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. Flickr is a photo library — albums, a photostream, a Camera Roll organised by date — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like a library, it behaves like a library, and it is not your library. It is a library on Flickr's servers, and the photos in it are files that live there, not on your phone. A photo library on your phone — the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. Flickr shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Flickr account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Flickr either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no social account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of Flickr and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a save. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion Flickr's own design invites. Flickr is where photographers keep their work, and the account feels like an archive — your photostream, your albums, your Camera Roll, all in date order, all yours. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy on someone else's server, under someone else's account rules, and a free account holds only 1,000 photos — Flickr has deleted over-limit free photos before, and gave extra time only after people could not get theirs out. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits in an account you do not control is a photo at risk twice over. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the feed is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

The third confusion hides inside Flickr itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. Tapping the heart to fave a photo marks it inside your Flickr account, in your Faves, where you can find it later. It is the fastest option, and it is not a download: you are keeping the photo, not the file. You cannot send it in a chat as a file, you cannot copy it to a drive, and if the photographer deletes the photo, the fave points at nothing — the photo is gone, and the thing you kept was a pointer to it. The proof that a fave saves nothing locally is the small industry built on top of it: applets that save new Flickr favourites to Dropbox or Google Drive exist precisely because the fave alone writes no file anywhere, and the download is a separate step the applet has to do for you. Sharing a photo's link is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the photo remaining available. Adding a photo to an album organises it inside Flickr, still on Flickr's servers, still not a file on your phone. The only tap that writes a file is the share menu's Save — or the three-dot menu's Save — and if you have been faving photos and wondering why none of them are on your phone, this is why: you have been keeping photos, and kept photos are not files.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on Flickr's servers. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The save is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the save is a step you have to take yourself, one photo at a time, because Flickr offers no save-them-all button in the app and no auto-save setting. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the save-one-and-check rule, because for Flickr the date problem is a save problem: the file is written when you save it, and the day you saved it is the day it sorts by.

The wall: Flickr saves nothing by itself — the save is yours to do

Flickr does not put your photostream photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the save describes a manual step — open the photo, tap the share icon, tap Save — because the manual step is the only step there is. Flickr's settings hold nothing about saving to the phone: the app has no save-location setting, no auto-download toggle, and no sync-to-device option. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache, which saves data when you scroll back over photos you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is Flickr's own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's cache is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you save it, and you save it one photo at a time.

The save itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because the guides agree on it in the same words across independent sources. Open the photo in the Flickr app. Tap the share icon — or the three dots in the upper corner, depending on the app version — and tap Save. That is MultCloud's version, and TechSith's version, and Seed of You's version, all three in the same order: share, then Save. The photo lands in a folder called Flickr, visible in your Gallery. That is TechSith's version too, first-hand and plain: "The image lands in a specific 'Flickr' folder in your device's gallery or file manager." If the share sheet shows no Save, tap the photo first to open it full-screen, then try the three dots instead — the save option belongs to the photo, and a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, not the file. Try the share icon first, then the three dots, then the browser route in the fixes below.

There is one more thing the save does not let you do, and it is worth knowing before you save a hundred photos, because it decides how you organise them afterwards. The save goes straight to the Flickr folder: no guide that describes the save describes a location picker in the Flickr app, and the first-hand account shows the folder created automatically, with no step where you choose where the file goes. Treat any choose-a-folder step as unverified: the save lands in the Flickr folder, every time, on the versions the guides describe. That is a limit, and it is also a convenience: every Flickr photo you save is in the same folder, and the folder is the thing you point a backup at. Do not waste an evening looking for a save-location setting in the Flickr app. There is not one, on Android, in any guide this run found.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the save sometimes silently does nothing. The Flickr app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the save tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. MultCloud gives the check in the Android wording: open Settings, go to Applications, choose Flickr, click Permissions, and enable Storage. On current Android versions the same permission is listed as Photos and videos. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first save, open the Gallery and look for the Flickr folder. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every save after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission and save the photo again. Do not save fifty photos and check at the end. Save one, check the folder, then save the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the Flickr app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the Flickr account, because the photos live in that account's photostream and albums. Enough free space on the phone to hold the saves: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, save in batches and copy each batch to the drive before saving the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the save steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a save guide.

One more check, on the Flickr side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can save. If the photo is in a photostream or album you can open, it is a photo you can try to save. If it is from an account that is private, or the photo was deleted, or it is flagged and hidden from your feed, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be saved, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. And if the Save option is missing entirely, that is usually the owner's choice, not a bug: the next section but one covers it. Do not save first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then tap Share. A save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Save one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Flickr app on your Android phone and sign in. Find the photo — in your photostream, in an album, in your Faves, or in search — and open it full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a save of a thumbnail is a save of a thumbnail. Tap the share icon. Tap Save in the menu that appears. Then open the Gallery and look for the Flickr folder: the photo should be there, at full size, and the size should be plausible for a photo, not a few kilobytes. If the share sheet shows no Save, tap the three dots in the upper corner instead — the menu wording varies by version, and the three-dot menu's Save is the other route the guides document.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the Flickr folder is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo in the Flickr folder is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of the Flickr folder, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where Flickr put it. The default run, described in the next section but one, reads the media index with no folder filter, and the Flickr folder is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo on Flickr if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is in the Flickr folder, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the saved Flickr photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the saved batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one album, the photos from one photographer. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand share-and-save taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a batch of your own photos, use Method 2, which gets a whole Camera Roll date or an album in one ZIP instead of one save at a time. For a whole account, use Method 3. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route — Flickr offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Save option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the owner disabled downloads: if the photo is someone else's and marked All Rights Reserved, the download control disappears for everyone except the owner, and no tap in the app will bring it back — respect that choice, and ask the photographer for the file if you need it. Second, update the Flickr app: the menu wording varies between app versions, and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Third, try the other gesture: open the photo full-screen and use the three-dot menu's Save instead of the share icon, or the share icon instead of the three dots. Fourth, open the photo in Chrome instead of the app: open the photo page at flickr.com in the browser, tap the picture to enlarge it, press and hold it, and choose the download or save-image option the browser offers — the file lands in Downloads, and the gallery shows it from there. Fifth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, Flickr, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and save again. One of those five fixes the save in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, in the Flickr folder, before you save the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the Flickr folder, select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole album or a Camera Roll batch — the web ZIP route

There is no save-them-all button in the Flickr app, and that is not a guess: every guide that describes the save describes one photo at a time, and none of them describes a batch command, a select-all, or a download-a-photostream button in the app. Seed of You states it outright: the phone app can save individual items, but does not offer bulk downloads. The workaround in the app is to make the finding fast, not the saving: open the album or the photographer's photostream that holds the photos and work through them in one place instead of scattered through the feed. Then save them one by one, in order, with the same gesture — open, Share, Save — and check the Flickr folder after the first few, so a permission or menu problem shows up on photo three, not photo three hundred.

For a batch of your own photos, the honest route is the Camera Roll download on the web, and it is Flickr's own route, not a third-party tool. It is available in a browser, not in the app — on a phone, open flickr.com in Chrome rather than the Flickr app, and if the download controls are not visible, try the browser's request-desktop-site option. Sign in to the account that holds the photos. Open You in the top menu and select Camera Roll. Click Select All next to a date to grab every photo from that day, or click individual thumbnails to build a custom batch — the bottom of the screen tracks how many items you selected. Click Download in the pop-up menu at the bottom. Flickr will create a ZIP file: this is not instant, and you will receive a notification and an email when it is ready, in FlickrMail. When it is ready, open the FlickrMail message and click the download link. Unzip it first if it arrives as a ZIP, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives — and then back up the files it contains.

Albums work the same way, and they are the cleaner route when your photos are already organised. Navigate to You, then Albums. Hover over the album thumbnail and a download icon appears on the cover image, or open the album and find the download icon near the album title. Clicking it prompts Flickr to zip the entire album, and the finished ZIP arrives the same way, through FlickrMail. If you download someone else's album, the system only includes the photos that have download permission enabled: an album of 50 photos where 10 are All Rights Reserved will arrive as a ZIP of 40. Check the licence before assuming you have the full set.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. Flickr places a cap on how many photos you can zip at once from the Camera Roll — usually around 500 images per batch. If you select more, the download button may grey out or fail, so break a large job into chunks of a few hundred and repeat. A finished ZIP stays available only until you download it, so download it promptly when the FlickrMail message arrives rather than starting repeated exports while one is processing. It is also slow: the ZIP is prepared on Flickr's side, and the email arrives when it is ready, not when you ask. Request it, wait, download it, unzip it, and then back up the files it contains. For ten photos, save them in the app. For a whole album or a whole date, use the Camera Roll batch. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Flickr folder — or the unzipped batch folder — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole account — the data request, and the no-bulk wall

For a whole account, the honest route is the data request, and it is Flickr's own route, not a third-party tool. It is available on the web, not in the app — on a phone, use a browser rather than the Flickr app. Open Flickr in a browser and sign in. Go to your account settings and find the Your Flickr Data section. Click Request My Flickr Data. This triggers a server-side process that compiles everything you have ever uploaded — every photo, every video, and the metadata with them. This is not fast: Flickr says full-data preparation can range from a few hours to a few weeks, and for a large account it can take days. Flickr sends an email when the files are ready. The links are valid for a limited time, so download every offered archive part promptly when it arrives, before the expiry Flickr shows you.

The archive is a real backup, and it is worth knowing what is in it before you download it. The full-data request preserves the original media and its metadata — the original files, not the dashboard renditions — which is the difference between this route and the in-app save. Keep the original files in a dated folder and work on a copy, and keep the metadata files with the photos rather than discarding them: metadata Flickr added may be stored separately rather than embedded in the image file. Unzip every part first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives — and then back up the files the archive contains, checking a few on the drive before you delete anything.

Two facts about the data request are worth stating because they decide who it works for. First, it is for your own account: it does not provide an export for someone else's account, and it does not include photos you faved but did not upload — a library of faves is not a library you can export. Second, free members can request their full data: the full account-data request is separate from the ordinary single-item, Camera Roll, and album download limits, so the free tier's 1,000-photo cap is not a barrier to getting your own photos out. If you are leaving Flickr, or closing the account, request the data first and wait for the email before you do anything irreversible — the account is the only place the originals live until the archive is on your drive.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route. Flickr offers no export for an account you do not control, no download-a-photostream button, and no save-them-all in the app. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take a username or a photo link and fetch the media — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them: they are outside Flickr, they need your trust to run, and several of them ask for your Flickr login, which is something they do not need for a public photo and which puts your account at risk. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: save the photos you actually want one by one, where the owner left downloads enabled, or ask the photographer for the files. A thousand one-by-one saves is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a saved Flickr photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a saved photo in the Flickr folder copies with the rest of the library. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the Flickr folder in shared storage.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: save the photo, open the gallery, and look for it in the Flickr folder. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in the Flickr folder: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into Flickr. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Flickr for anything. A photo still on Flickr's servers is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on Flickr's servers is invisible to the file manager. The save is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Flickr; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Flickr photos without saving them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Flickr folder came from Flickr, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Flickr route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the save that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the Flickr photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the Flickr folder and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Flickr folder yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on this exact folder — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a saved photo shows the save date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo saved from Flickr shows the day you saved it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a saved file is the save time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a saved Flickr photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you saved it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For Flickr, the taken date almost certainly did not survive, and the reason is the quality section below: Flickr serves you its own copy of the photo, and a served copy is the step most likely to rewrite or drop the metadata. This article states that as the expected case, not as a certainty, because no source this run tested the taken date after an in-app save — but the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app: the file is written by the save, at save time, and its modified date is the save date. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: save one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you save a hundred of them.

There is one way in which Flickr is better than the other clouds on the date, and it is worth stating fairly. The data request in Method 3 preserves the original media and its metadata — the original files, not the dashboard renditions — so an archive built from the data request keeps the taken dates the in-app save loses. That is the opposite of OneDrive, where Microsoft's own support forum says the taken date can be replaced by the upload date, especially by camera upload or a bulk upload, before you ever tap Save. For Flickr the risk is at the save, not at the upload, and the defence is to know it before you save the batch: use the data request when the dates matter, and the in-app save when the photo matters.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Flickr names the saved file itself, and two saves of the same photo — or two photos Flickr named the same way — can arrive in the Flickr folder under the same name, so the second save either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo on Flickr if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to save a batch and copy it to the drive before saving the next, or to rename the files in the Flickr folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to save.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Save one album's photos, back up the Flickr folder, clear it, save the next album's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the albums in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the saved copy is Flickr's copy — and the Original on the web

A photo saved from the Flickr app is not the photographer's original file. It is the copy Flickr serves for the phone, saved as a JPG into your Gallery, and the difference is real even when it is not measured in pixels on the spot. Flickr stores multiple versions of every upload, and the app save gives you the served version, not a size you chose: there is no size menu in the app save, and no Original button on the share sheet. The saved copy is a real photo, and it is good enough for the gallery, the drive, and a print at ordinary sizes. It is not the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app save is the wrong tool, and this article says so instead of letting you discover it on the drive.

The route to the original is a browser step, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the only route that gets the bigger file — and it is a browser route, not an app route. Open the photo page on flickr.com in a browser, click the download arrow in the bottom-right corner of the image, and choose a size from the menu it offers. The sizes run from Square, 75 by 75, up to Original, often 4,000 or more pixels wide, and Original is the only choice for archival purposes: it preserves the exact pixel data from the camera, and a smaller version cannot be scaled back up later without losing detail. On a phone it is a fiddly tap-and-hold per photo for a gain that is often small, and the honest advice is to use the browser route only when the photo matters at full size, and to use the in-app save for everything else: the in-app save is a share and a tap, and the browser route is a detour per photo. Use each one for the job it fits. For your own photos the download arrow is always visible; for someone else's it appears only where the owner left downloads enabled.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because Flickr is a photo library that holds videos too. The app save covers videos the same way it covers photos: open the video in the Flickr app, use Share and Save, and the file lands on the phone. The backup covers it from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, so a saved photo and a saved video both copy with the default run. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: save the video, open the Flickr folder, and look at what arrived. If it plays, it saved as a video; if it arrived as a single still frame, the save caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, save it from the browser, where the menu tells you whether you are saving an image or a video, and check what arrived before you save the next twenty.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: save one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the saved copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before saving — wait for the photo to load crisp before you tap Share, because a save of a half-loaded thumbnail is a save of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the saved file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the save is worth doing at all. A photo saved from Flickr is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the size: the saved copy is Flickr's copy, and no backup step will put the missing pixels back. Save the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. If it is not, take the browser route for that one photo, and save the rest in the app.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest save, a middle one, and the most recent save. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Flickr folder on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the Flickr folder holds the photos plus whatever else Flickr saved there — but the photo count should match the number you saved, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you saved, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second save overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the Flickr folder before the backup ever saw it. Save one batch at a time and copy each batch before saving the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you saved, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Flickr batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a saved photo shows the save date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the save, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, save that photo again from the browser at Original size, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in Flickr's copy, not at upload — and the data request in Method 3 is the route that keeps it, because it preserves the original files with their metadata.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Flickr login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The public-link downloader sites need no sign-in: copy the photo link, paste it in, and save the file — that is the whole flow. A tool that asks for your Flickr password to download a public photo is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours. Several unofficial tools claim to download Flickr streams faster, and they pose exactly this risk: you must enter your login credentials into unknown software, which violates Flickr's terms of service in some cases and puts your account at risk. The in-app save needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not fave photos and expect them on the drive. A fave keeps the photo inside your Flickr account, not a file, and a faved photo the photographer deletes is a fave that points at nothing. Fave the photo to find it later, and save the photo to keep it. They are two different taps for two different jobs, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing in the Flickr folder — because the photos were never saved, only faved.

Do not save a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand share-and-save taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Save the handful you actually want in the app, use the Camera Roll batches for an album or a date, and request the data export for a whole account you own. The export is slow and the app is fast, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the export to reach someone else's account: it is for an account you control, and it will not contain your faves either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the feed as the only copy. Flickr is a library on someone else's server, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can go private or be closed, and a free account holds only 1,000 photos. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the feed is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
