Save Amazon Photos to USB from Android

Your photos on Amazon Photos live on Amazon's servers, not on your phone. Download each one in the app — then find it in Downloads or Pictures — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 14 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see in Amazon Photos is not on your phone yet. Amazon Photos is a photo library that lives on Amazon's servers, not a folder on your phone: you can see the photo, open it, share it, add it to a Family Vault, and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you download it. In the Amazon Photos app on Android, press and hold the photo until a check mark appears, tap the three-dot menu, and tap Download — that is Amazon's own help, word for word — and the file lands in shared storage, visible in your Gallery and in Files. Which folder, exactly, Amazon does not say: no Amazon help page names one, and the independent guides disagree — Downloads, Pictures, a dedicated Amazon Photos folder — so this article does not invent one. Check Downloads first, then Pictures, then the Gallery's recent view, and the folder-check section below walks the whole search in order. The confusion first: Amazon Photos is a photo library on Amazon's servers — and Download is not Auto-Save

Amazon Photos sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. Amazon Photos is a photo library — albums, folders on the web, Memories, a Family Vault, a search that finds faces and places — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like a library, it behaves like a library, and it is not your library. It is a library on Amazon's servers, and the photos in it are files that live there, not on your phone. A photo library on your phone — the Gallery itself — is a place where photos live as files, organised by date and folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. Amazon Photos shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Amazon Photos account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Amazon Photos either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no cloud account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of Amazon Photos and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a download. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion Amazon Photos' own design invites. Amazon Photos is where Prime members keep their photos, and the account feels like an archive — your library, your albums, your Family Vault, all in the order you put them in, all yours, with unlimited full-resolution photo storage for Prime members. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy on someone else's server, under someone else's account rules, and it is a paid service behind a Prime membership — which means the photos sit behind a subscription as well as behind an account. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits in an account that drops to 5 GB of storage when Prime lapses is a photo at risk twice over: Amazon's own storage guidance says that over quota you can still view, download, and share what is already there, but you cannot upload anything new until you free space or add storage, and Auto-Save and backup pause until you are back under the limit. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the cloud is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

The third confusion hides inside Amazon Photos itself, between the ways you can "save" something — and they are not the same thing at all. Turning on Auto-Save in the app — Profile, Photo Settings, Auto-Save, toggle on Photos, Videos, or both — uploads new photos from your phone up to Amazon Photos. It is the fastest option, and it is not a download: you are sending photos up, not bringing them down, and a photo Auto-Save uploaded is a photo that left the phone, not a photo that arrived on it. Sharing a photo's link is a link, not a file, and a link depends on the photo remaining available in the account. Adding a photo to a Family Vault puts it in the shared vault the whole group can view — up to six members on a Prime account, each with their own account — but the vault is still inside Amazon Photos, not a file in your Gallery: it is Amazon's storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and gone from your reach the moment the owner removes it or the sharing ends. The only tap that writes a file is the three-dot menu's Download — and if you have been turning on Auto-Save and wondering why none of the account photos are on your phone, this is why: you have been uploading photos, and uploaded photos are not downloads.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on Amazon's servers. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The download is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the download is a step you have to take yourself, because Amazon Photos offers no download-them-all setting and no auto-download. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the download-one-and-check rule, because for Amazon Photos the date problem is a download problem: the file is written when you download it, and the day you downloaded it is the day it sorts by. And the one thing this article will not do, which the other guides do without saying so, is invent a folder: Amazon names none, so the folder section below is a search, not a claim.

The wall: Amazon Photos downloads nothing by itself — the download is yours to do

Amazon Photos does not put your account photos in your Gallery on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-download, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every guide that describes the download describes a manual step — press and hold the photo, tap the three dots, tap Download — because the manual step is the only step there is. Amazon Photos' settings hold nothing about downloading to the phone: the app has no download-location setting, no auto-download toggle, and no sync-to-device option. Viewed photos sit in the app's cache, which saves data when you scroll back over photos you already saw, but a cache is not a Gallery folder: it is Amazon Photos' own storage, invisible to the file manager, invisible to SnapDisk, and wiped when the app's cache is cleared. A photo becomes a file on your phone only when you download it, and you download it one photo at a time — or one selection at a time, with the multi-select described below.

The download itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because Amazon's own help states it in the same words this article uses. Open the Amazon Photos app. Press and hold a photo or video until a check mark appears. If you want more, select each additional item, or press and hold an item and slide your finger to select multiple items. Tap the three-dot menu. Tap Download. That is Amazon's own help, first-hand, fetched live this run: "Download photos and videos to your device for offline use." The two conditions in that page are the whole gate, and they are worth reading twice: it is not possible to download entire albums — open the album, and select all the pictures you want to download — and if a photo is already on the device, the Download option may not display. The second condition is the one that confuses people: a missing Download button is not always a failure. It can mean the photo is already a file on your phone, and the check is the Gallery, not the menu — search the Gallery for the photo before assuming the download failed.

There is one more thing the download does not let you choose, and it is worth knowing before you download a hundred photos, because it decides where the files land — or rather, it decides that you do not get to decide. Amazon's app settings hold no download-location choice at all: no internal-or-SD-card choice, no folder picker, no save-to setting. Treat any choose-a-folder step as unverified: the download lands in shared storage, every time, on the versions the guides describe, and the exact folder varies by device, Android version, permissions, and the current Amazon Photos app build. That is a limit, and it is also the reason the next section exists: the folder is a search, not a setting, and the search is short once you know the three places to look. Do not waste an evening looking for a download-location setting in the Amazon Photos app. There is not one, on Android, in any guide this run found.

One more detail, on permission, because it is the reason the download sometimes silently does nothing. The Amazon Photos app needs storage permission to write the file, and on current Android versions that permission is the photos permission the system asks for when the app first tries to save. If you denied it once, the download tap may show nothing at all — and the photo is not in the Gallery, because there was nowhere to write it. The check is the Gallery, not the tap: after the first download, open the Gallery and look for the photo. If the photo is there, the permission is fine and every download after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission in Settings, Apps, Amazon Photos, Permissions, Photos and videos, and download the photo again. Do not download fifty photos and check at the end. Download one, check the folder, then download the rest.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the Amazon Photos app installed and signed in — the sign-in that matters is the Amazon account, because the photos live in that account, and the help page is explicit that a photo already on the device hides the Download option, so a signed-in account with photos you can see is the starting point. Enough free space on the phone to hold the downloads: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, download in batches and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the download steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a download guide.

One more check, on the Amazon Photos side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can download. If the photo is in an album you can open, it is a photo you can try to download. If it is from an account that is private, or the photo was deleted, or it sits in a Family Vault you were removed from, it may not load at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be downloaded, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake. And if the Download option is missing entirely, that is usually one of two things, not a bug: the photo is already on the device, or the photo is shared with you in a way that does not include downloading — the next sections cover both. Do not download first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then tap the three-dot menu. A download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail.

Method 1: Download one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Amazon Photos app on your Android phone and sign in. Find the photo — in your library, in an album, in search, or in the Family Vault — and open it full-screen. Wait for it to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a thumbnail, and a download of a thumbnail is a download of a thumbnail. Press and hold the photo until a check mark appears. If you want more than one, tap each additional photo, or press and hold and slide your finger across the ones you want. Tap the three-dot menu. Tap Download. Then find the file — and finding it is a step of its own, because Amazon names no folder. Open the Gallery first: if the photo is there, the download worked, whatever folder it sits in. If it is not in the Gallery, open Files by Google and look in Downloads — Internal storage, Download — then in Pictures. One of those three places holds it on almost every phone, and the folder-check section below walks the search in order, with the reason each place is worth checking.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the folder the file landed in is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo the Gallery shows is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of Downloads or Pictures, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where Amazon Photos put it. The default run, described in the section after next, reads the media index with no folder filter, and Downloads and Pictures are indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo in Amazon Photos if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is a file on the phone, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the downloaded Amazon Photos photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the downloaded batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo — or one selection — at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one album, the photos from one trip. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand press-hold-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole album, use the select-all route in Method 2, which gets the album in one selection instead of one tap at a time. For a whole account, use Method 3, which gets the library in one request instead of one selection at a time. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what the owner shared — Amazon Photos offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Download option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the photo is already on the device: Amazon's help says the Download option may not display if a photo is already on the device, so search the Gallery and the Files app for the photo before anything else — it may already be a file, and the job may already be half done. Second, check that you are logged in to the right account: the photos live in the account you signed in with, and a second Amazon account holds a different library. Third, update the Amazon Photos app: the menu wording varies between app versions, and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Fourth, try the album route instead of the photo route: open the album, select all the pictures inside it, and download the selection — Amazon's own help gives exactly that as the album route. Fifth, open the photo on the web instead of the app: sign in at amazon.com/photos in Chrome, select the photos, choose Download, and the files arrive in the browser's Downloads folder. Sixth, check the permission: Settings, Apps, Amazon Photos, Permissions, Photos and videos — grant it, and download again. One of those six fixes the download in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Gallery, before you download the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the folder the download landed in — Downloads or Pictures — select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole album — select-all in the app, or the web download

Amazon Photos is blunt about the one bulk step it does not offer, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the step every other cloud in this series offers in some form: there is no download-an-entire-album button in the app. Amazon's own help says it outright — "It's not possible to download entire albums. Open the album, and select all the pictures you want to download." That is the app route for a whole album, and it is one selection, not one tap per photo: open the album, press and hold the first photo, slide your finger across the rest or tap Select all if the app offers it, tap the three-dot menu, and tap Download. The selection is the batch, and the batch is the album. For an album of twenty photos it is one minute. For an album of five hundred it is a long press-and-slide, and the web route below is the better tool — use each route for the job it fits, and check the folder before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

For a batch on the web, the route is the browser, and it is worth following exactly, because the web route has two traps — the ZIP and the Downloads folder — that decide whether it works on a phone at all. Sign in at amazon.com/photos in Chrome, open the album or the library view, select the photos you want — the web selection works in batches, and several guides report selecting up to a thousand at a time — and choose Download. The files arrive in the browser's Downloads folder, on the phone, in Internal storage/Download. When several files are downloaded together, the browser or the application may package them as one ZIP archive — that is the first trap, and it is a real one: SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives, so a ZIP sitting in Downloads is a file the backup will skip, and the photos inside it are still zipped. Open the ZIP in the Files app and tap Extract first, and back up the files it contains. The second trap is the folder: the web download lands in Downloads, not in Pictures and not in DCIM, and a reader who looks for the batch in the Gallery's Pictures folder will not find it until the media index picks the extracted files up — open one extracted photo once, which prompts the media scanner, or use the From picker route, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all.

The web route is also the route for the photos the app will not download, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the route for the edge cases. If a photo is in a Family Vault you belong to, the web view shows the same vault, and the same select-and-download works from there — but check each item, because sharing is per item and per vault, and a photo shared with you for viewing is not automatically a photo you can download: the seedofyou guide's warning is the right one, to check ownership and access, including shared and Family Vault items, before assuming the selection is yours to take. If a photo is in an album someone shared with you by link, the same rule holds: the link shows you the photo, and the download is a separate permission the owner controls. And if the Download option is missing for a single photo in the app but present on the web, the web is the fix — the app and the web do not always agree on what is already on the device, and the web download writes a fresh file either way.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. The download is the photos you selected, not the album as an object: the album name, the album order, and the album membership do not travel with the files, so keep the album context yourself — a dated folder on the drive, named for the album, is the simplest way, and the checking section below says so again, because it is the step people skip. The ZIP is prepared by the browser, and the download arrives when it is ready, not when you ask — select, download, wait, unzip, and then back up the files it contains. Each time more photos are added to the album, you need to download again, or the new photos will not be in the batch. For ten photos, download them in the app. For a whole album, use select-all in the app, or the web download. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Downloads folder — or the unzipped batch folder — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole account — what Amazon Photos does and does not export

For a whole account, the honest answer is that Amazon Photos has no one-tap whole-account export in the app, and this article says so instead of inventing one. There is no settings page that zips your entire account, no download-all button that compiles everything you ever uploaded, and no archive that arrives by email with all your albums in it. The export unit in the app is the selection: one photo at a time, or one album's selection at a time. If you have ten albums, that is ten selections. If you have a hundred, that is a hundred — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

The route that does exist for a whole account is the web plus the data request, and it is worth doing in order, because the order is what keeps it from becoming a mess. Start with the library that matters most — the photos with no other copy anywhere — and work down the list. For each album, select its photos on the web at amazon.com/photos, download them, unzip the batch if it arrived as a ZIP, and back up the files it contains before downloading the next album — unzip first, because SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives. Keep each album's files in their own dated folder on the drive, so the drive ends up with the albums in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower than a single export would be, and it is the route that exists. The app's select-all is the faster route for a single album, and the web download is the route for the originals with the most control. Use each one for the job it fits.

For the whole account at once, Amazon's privacy centre offers a Request My Data export — privacy.amazon.com, request your data, and Amazon prepares it, which takes days rather than minutes, according to the migration guides that describe it. That is a reported route, not one this run tested end to end, and it is worth stating as such: the export arrives as a prepared package, you download it to the phone or to a computer, you unzip it, and you back up the files it contains. Do not treat it as a one-tap backup: it is a data request, it takes time to prepare, and the photos inside it still need the unzip and the backup steps before they are on a drive. And do not request it the week your Prime lapses: Amazon's own storage guidance says that once you are over the 5 GB that remains, you can still view, download, and share what is already there, but you cannot upload anything new — the export is a way out, not a way to keep uploading, and the time to take it is while the account is in good standing, not after the limit bites.

Two facts about the account downloads are worth stating because they decide who they work for. First, they are for photos you can download: your own photos, and other people's photos where the sharing allows it. They do not provide an export for someone else's account, and they do not include photos you viewed but did not download — a library of views is not a library you can export. Second, the download is not locked to you: a file you download is a file, and a file can be copied, shared, and forwarded by anyone who holds it, so keep the drive somewhere safe and do not forward a batch in a group chat and assume only the people you meant will open it. If the photos need protection, the protection is the drive in your drawer, not the account on Amazon's server.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what the owner shared. Amazon Photos offers no export for an account you do not control, no download-a-whole-library button, and no save-them-all in the app beyond a single selection. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and apps that take a username or an album link and fetch the media — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them: they are outside Amazon Photos, they need your trust to run, and several of them ask for your Amazon login, which is something they do not need for a shared album and which puts your account at risk. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: download the photos you actually want one by one, or the whole album where the sharing allows it, or ask the person for the files. A thousand one-by-one downloads is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a downloaded Amazon Photos photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a downloaded photo copies with the rest of the library, whatever folder Amazon Photos put it in — Downloads, Pictures, or any other shared folder. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the folder the Amazon Photos download landed in.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: download the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in Downloads and then in Pictures: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into Amazon Photos. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Amazon Photos for anything. A photo still on Amazon's servers is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on Amazon's servers is invisible to the file manager. The download is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Amazon Photos; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Amazon Photos photos without downloading them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Downloads folder came from Amazon Photos, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Amazon Photos route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the download that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the Amazon Photos photos, not the whole library. Point the From picker at the folder the downloads landed in — Downloads, or Pictures — and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Downloads or Pictures folder for Amazon Photos downloads yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on these exact folders — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works.

The date problem: a downloaded photo shows the download date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo downloaded from Amazon Photos shows the day you downloaded it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a downloaded file is the download time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a downloaded Amazon Photos photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you downloaded it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For Amazon Photos, the taken date may or may not have survived, and the reason is the quality section below: Amazon stores your photos at full resolution, and a download is a fresh file written at download time, so the modified date is the download date whatever the file holds inside. This article states download-date order as the expected case, not as a certainty, because no source this run tested the taken date after an in-app download — but the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app: the file is written by the download, at download time, and its modified date is the download date. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: download one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you download a hundred of them.

There is one way in which Amazon Photos is better than the other clouds on the date, and it is worth stating fairly. The service stores photos at full resolution for Prime members — unlimited, uncompressed, RAW included — and a full-resolution file is the one most likely to carry its taken date with it. That is the opposite of OneDrive, where Microsoft's own support forum says the taken date can be replaced by the upload date, especially by camera upload or a bulk upload, before you ever tap Save. For Amazon Photos the risk is at the download, not at the upload, and the defence is to know it before you download the batch: use the web download when the dates matter, and the in-app download when the photo matters.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Amazon Photos names the downloaded file itself, and two downloads of the same photo — or two photos Amazon Photos named the same way — can arrive in the Downloads folder under the same name, so the second download either overwrites the first or arrives with a number added, depending on the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo in Amazon Photos if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to download a batch and copy it to the drive before downloading the next, or to rename the files in the Downloads folder first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to download.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Download one album's photos, back up the Downloads folder, clear it, download the next album's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the albums in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the downloaded copy is Amazon's copy — and Original on the web

A photo downloaded from the Amazon Photos app is not necessarily the photographer's original file. It is the copy Amazon serves for the phone, saved into your Gallery, and the difference is real even when it is not measured in pixels on the spot. Amazon stores multiple versions of every upload, and the app download gives you the served version, not a size you chose: there is no size menu in the app download, and no Original button on the three-dot menu. The downloaded copy is a real photo, and it is good enough for the gallery, the drive, and a print at ordinary sizes. It is not necessarily the original, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app download may be the wrong tool, and this article says so instead of letting you discover it on the drive.

The route to the original is the web download, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the only route that gives you a choice — and the choice is the web's, not the app's. On the web at amazon.com/photos, the download options let you pick Unmodified Originals, Highest Resolution, or Most Compatible before you download, so a download at Unmodified Originals is the full file as it was uploaded, and a download at Most Compatible is the file re-encoded for the device. On a phone it is a fiddly detour per batch for a gain that is often small, and the honest advice is to use the web download at Unmodified Originals when the photo matters at full size, and to use the in-app download for everything else: the in-app download is a press, a menu, and a tap, and the web download is a sign-in, a selection, and an unzip. Use each one for the job it fits. For your own photos the download at Original is the full file; for someone else's it is the size the sharing allows.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because Amazon Photos is a photo library that holds videos too — and videos are the one place the storage limit bites. The app download covers videos the same way it covers photos: open the video in the Amazon Photos app, use the three-dot menu and Download, and the file lands on the phone — Amazon's own help says "a photo or video you want to save". The backup covers it from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, so a downloaded photo and a downloaded video both copy with the default run. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: download the video, open the folder, and look at what arrived. If it plays, it downloaded as a video; if it arrived as a single still frame, the download caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, download it from the web, where the format options are explicit, and check what arrived before you download the next twenty. And remember the storage rule while you are at it: videos always count against the limit, Prime included — 5 GB for videos and other files — so a video-heavy library fills up long before the photo storage does, and a download of a video you already have on the phone is a download you did not need.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: download one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the downloaded copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the high-quality version before downloading — wait for the photo to load crisp before you tap the three-dot menu, because a download of a half-loaded thumbnail is a download of a half-loaded thumbnail, and the quality of the downloaded file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload. And check the metadata before you delete the cloud copy: one independent guide's warning is the right one, that a download should be checked for metadata, edits, Live Photo data, and motion-photo packaging before the cloud copy is deleted — and Amazon Photos lists Google Motion Photos and Samsung Motion Photos as unsupported, so a motion photo may arrive as a still, and the motion is gone for good if the cloud copy goes first.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the download is worth doing at all. A photo downloaded from Amazon Photos is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the size: the downloaded copy is Amazon's copy, and no backup step will put the missing pixels back. Download the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. If it is not, take the web-download route at Unmodified Originals for that one photo, and download the rest in the app.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest download, a middle one, and the most recent download. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Downloads and Pictures folders on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the folders hold the photos plus whatever else landed there — but the photo count should match the number you downloaded, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the duplicate-name problem from the date section: a second download overwrote the first under the same file name, and the photo is gone from the folder before the backup ever saw it. Download one batch at a time and copy each batch before downloading the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Amazon Photos batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a downloaded photo shows the download date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the download, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, download that photo again from the web at Unmodified Originals, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in Amazon's copy, not at upload — and the web download at Original is the route that keeps it, because it gives you the file as it was uploaded.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Amazon login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The download needs no third party at all: the app's three-dot menu does the single photo, the web download does the batch, and the data request does the account. A tool that asks for your Amazon password to download a shared album is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours — the same account that holds your orders, your payment methods, and your Prime membership, not just your photos. Several unofficial tools claim to download Amazon Photos libraries faster, and they pose exactly this risk: you must enter your login credentials into unknown software, which puts your whole Amazon account at risk. The in-app download needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not turn on Auto-Save and expect the account photos on the drive. Auto-Save uploads new photos from your phone up to Amazon Photos; it does not bring account photos down to your phone. Turn it on to back up the camera roll, and download the photos to keep them. They are two different settings for two different directions, and confusing them is the commonest reason a reader finishes this article's steps and finds nothing new in the Downloads folder — because the photos were never downloaded, only uploaded.

Do not download a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand press-hold-and-download taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Download the handful you actually want in the app, use select-all or the web download for an album, and work through your own albums one by one from the web. The web download is slow and the app download is fast, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the download to reach someone else's account: it is for photos you own or that were shared with download rights, and it will not contain your views either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the cloud as the only copy. Amazon Photos is a library on someone else's server, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can go over quota, and a Prime membership that lapses drops the account to 5 GB and pauses Auto-Save and backup until you are back under the limit. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the cloud is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
