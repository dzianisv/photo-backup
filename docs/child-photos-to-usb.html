Why handing your phone to a child is the deadline

Handing your phone to a child is not a reminder to back up — it is a deadline, and the deadline is before the phone leaves your hand, not when it comes back:

  • A child taps everything. The Photos app, the delete button, the settings, the store — a small child does not know what a delete confirmation means, and an older child exploring will tap it anyway. The phone comes back, but a deleted photo does not.
  • A deletion has no undo you can count on. The drive copy is the undo. Google Photos trash can help, but only for photos that were backed up to Google Photos in the first place — deleted items sit in the trash for 30 days, 60 if they were backed up — and a photo that never uploaded has no trash copy to restore. A photo deleted from a file manager has no trash at all.
  • The phone comes back — and that is what makes this moment different. This is NOT lending and NOT selling or repair: no adult borrower who might snoop, no shop that might wipe it, no new owner. The risk is taps and deletes and purchases, not a stranger reading your messages — and the fix is a backup plus the phone's own lock-down, not a wipe.
  • Purchases are the other risk, and they are not a backup matter. A child can buy things inside a game — coins, gems, a subscription — and the backup does not stop it. The purchase setting in the Play Store does, and it is a store setting on the phone, covered in the hand-over section below.
  • Settings get changed, and that is fine. Wi-Fi off, brightness down, an alarm set for 3 a.m. — annoying, and fixable the moment the phone comes back. Photos are the only irreplaceable part, which is why the backup is about the photos and nothing else.
  • The hand-over is the point of no return for the photos. Before it, the phone is in your hand, the copy can still run, and the drive can still be checked. After it, the phone is in small hands, and you cannot check the drive or restart a copy until it comes back.
  • A child will not wait for a copy. A copy still running when you hand the phone over gets interrupted the moment the child unplugs the drive, closes the app, or drops the phone — the backup has to be done before the hand-over, not during it.
  • The drive is not a toy. Keep it at home, never hand it to the child — a drive handed over gets lost, dropped, or chewed, and a copy you no longer hold is not a backup.

None of this is fixed by backing up after the phone comes back with a photo missing — after a deletion with no copy anywhere else, the photo is gone, and the backup is already too late. The fix is a copy that finishes before the hand-over, oldest-first, verified on the drive, and kept at home while the child has the phone.

What you need

  • An Android phone running Android 7.0 or later that still works normally, with a dry, undamaged USB port — SnapDisk needs minSdk 24 in the repo. Never plug anything into a damaged port: a bent, loose, or wet port can short the board, and power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos. A dead phone is a repair-lab job only — no software route. On an iPhone, use Method 2: the Files-app copy has no version floor, and SnapDisk is Android only.
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. For a whole library, a portable SSD holds more — but a portable SSD may need a powered hub, because the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • A charged phone, or a powered hub — one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. Charge first, then plug the drive in — or use a powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do it at home before the child gets the phone, not while the child is already reaching for it, and do not hand the phone over until the check is done.
  • The game or app the child will use, already picked — hand the phone over with it open, not with the home screen and the Photos app waiting. The lock-down in the hand-over section works best when the app is already running.
  • Purchase authentication turned on in the Play Store before the hand-over — the store setting that stops a child buying things inside a game. It takes a minute, and it is done in the Play Store, not in SnapDisk.
  • No Wi-Fi needed — the drive copy runs entirely offline, and SnapDisk has no internet permission at all. Wi-Fi matters only if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the phone will keep filling it — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job, one quiet moment at home. The phone's photos need only the drive; the charger needs to be done before the drive goes in — and the child needs to wait until the drive copy is checked.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive, and it needs no Wi-Fi at all. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start. Two honest limits first: the copy has to finish and be checked before you hand the phone over — a copy still running when a child takes the phone is not a backup — and SnapDisk backs up photos and videos only, so the purchase setting and the lock-down in the next section are separate jobs on the phone itself. And the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-child run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified.

  1. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos on the phone are the ones a stray tap deletes first.
  2. Charge the phone first. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — so charge to full, then unplug the charger and plug the drive in. A powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  3. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  4. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  5. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  6. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  7. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  8. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it, before the child gets the phone. The child will not wait for a backup, and a deletion after the hand-over has no undo. If the copy is interrupted, resume it — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  9. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  10. Unplug the drive and put it away at home — not in the bag with the phone, and never in the child's hand. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A phone that has never been backed up often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size, so the next time a child asks for the phone, the copy starts the moment you plug the drive in. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every general guide above describes for photos — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when you want the backup done now, before the child gets the phone:

  1. Charge the phone first — one port holds the drive or the charger, not both — then plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  2. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  3. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  4. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder a stray tap can delete.
  5. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big and the child is already asking for the phone.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  7. Unplug the drive and put it away at home — not in the child's hand, and not in the bag with the phone — and only then think about deleting anything from the phone, after the check.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to go to a child, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time, with the child already waiting.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step most people skip before handing the phone to a child — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. And the check has a catch of its own: the hand-over is minutes away, so the cloud copy has to be finished and checked before the child takes the phone, not after it comes back with a photo missing:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone, and before the hand-over, not after. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and keep the drive at home.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones a stray tap deletes for good.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the Wi-Fi, the account, or the subscription — and unlike a cloud trash copy, it is there the moment you need it, with no 30-day clock on it.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, before the hand-over — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on Wi-Fi, an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round — and why, before a child uses the phone, the drive copy is the backup that is finished when you need it, not the upload that is still running. And the cloud is not the whole pre-hand-over job either: the purchase setting and the lock-down are separate — that is the next section.

Before you hand the phone over: the backup, the drive, and the lock-down

The copy is done while the phone is still in your hand — the hand-over is where the backup has to survive, not where it gets made. And one thing to be plain about: the photo backup is only part of the pre-hand-over job. SnapDisk backs up photos and videos only — it does not lock the phone, it does not stop a deletion on the phone, and it does not stop a purchase — so the rest of this section is your job, in the phone's own settings, before the child takes it:

  • Turn on purchase authentication — the one setting that stops a child buying things. Open the Play Store, tap your profile picture, go to Settings, then Authentication, and set Require authentication for purchases — every purchase then needs your fingerprint or password. A child can still tap Buy in a game, but the purchase cannot complete without you. That is a store setting on the phone, not a SnapDisk feature, and SnapDisk does not stop purchases.
  • Pin the screen — Android's own way to keep a child in one app. Open the game or app first, then open Recents, tap the app's icon, and tap Pin. The child stays in that app — the home button and the back button do nothing — until you hold Back and Recents together and unlock the phone. This is the phone itself doing the lock-down, never SnapDisk: SnapDisk copies, it never locks.
  • Use a kid or guest profile where your phone offers one. Some phones offer a kid space, a guest user, or a child profile — a separate space with no access to your photos, your accounts, or your store. Set it up before the hand-over, not during it, and check what it can and cannot open — the exact name and menu vary by phone, and a profile that still opens Photos is not a lock-down.
  • Use an app lock where your phone offers one. Some manufacturers let you lock Photos and the Play Store behind a PIN or a fingerprint — if your phone has it, lock both before the hand-over. If it does not, screen pinning above is the lock-down.
  • Check the drive copy first, then hand the phone over with the game already open. The copy has to be finished and checked before the child takes the phone — open a few photos on the drive, older ones and a long video — and only then open the game, pin the screen, and hand it over.
  • Keep the drive at home. The drive is the copy that saves the photos — it stays in a drawer, not in the child's hand and not in the bag with the phone. A drive handed over is a copy you no longer hold.
  • When the phone comes back, check the photos are still there. Open the Photos app and look — and if one is missing, copy it back from the drive. Open the drive on the phone or a computer, find the photo, and copy it back — the drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and that is what the backup was for.

The hand-over does not make the backup — it tests it. A drive at home, a phone with its purchase setting on and its screen pinned, and a copy that finished before the child took the phone is the whole plan, and the hand-over is where you stop worrying about it, not where you start it.

What this does not do

  • Does not keep a child out of the photos. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes and it never locks — the photos stay on the phone, and a child with the phone can still open them and delete them. The backup protects the photos — a deleted one comes back from the drive — it does not keep the child out of them. Screen pinning, a kid or guest profile, and an app lock are the phone's own features, never SnapDisk's.
  • Does not stop a deletion on the phone. The backup is a copy on a drive at home — it does not watch the phone, and it cannot undo a tap that already happened. The copy on the drive is what brings the photo back, after the phone comes back to you.
  • Does not stop in-app purchases. A child can still tap Buy inside a game — the backup does not stop it, because a backup is not a lock. The Play Store's Require authentication for purchases setting stops it, and that is a store setting on the phone.
  • Does not undo a deletion with no copy anywhere else. Once a photo is deleted with no copy on a drive or in the cloud, it is gone. The backup has to finish before the hand-over, not after the phone comes back with a photo missing.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Nothing else on the phone is at risk from a child except in-app purchases, which are a store setting, not a backup matter — so for this moment, photos and videos are the whole job.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com before the hand-over. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the child takes the phone, not after it comes back.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-child run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not charge the phone while the drive is in the port. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, then plug the drive in.
  • Does not power a portable SSD from the phone alone. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. A spinning hard drive may need its own power too.
  • Does not work on a damaged port or a dead phone. Never plug anything into a damaged USB port — power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos — and a dead phone is a repair-lab job only, not a free app.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only. On an iPhone the photo route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive — and the same rules apply: copy at home, verify on the drive, keep the drive at home, and use the phone's own Guided Access to keep a child in one app.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Handing the phone over before the copy finishes. A copy still running when a child takes the phone is not a backup — and a deletion after the hand-over has no undo. Copy, check, then hand over.
  • Handing the phone over before verifying the copy on the drive. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then hand over — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Handing the drive to the child with the phone. A drive handed over is a copy you no longer hold — it gets lost, dropped, or chewed. The drive stays at home.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want — before the hand-over, not after the phone comes back.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder a stray tap can delete.
  • Forgetting the purchase setting. SnapDisk backs up photos and videos only — it does not stop a purchase. Turn on Require authentication for purchases in the Play Store before the hand-over, or the child can buy things inside a game.
  • Handing the phone over on the home screen. A child on the home screen can open anything — Photos, the store, settings. Open the game first, pin the screen, and then hand it over.
  • Assuming the backup locks the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never locks — the backup protects the photos, it does not keep the child out of them. The lock-down is screen pinning, a kid or guest profile, or an app lock — the phone's own features, never SnapDisk's.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to go to a child, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then decide.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then decide — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it, not after the copy fails.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole library in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole library is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.
  • Assuming the general guides cover the before-child moment. The Android Authority, Android Central, Android Police, TheUnlockr, and Times of India guides back up photos to a computer or the cloud — but none of them copies the phone's own library to USB in one tap, none of them keeps the drive at home while a child has the phone, and none of them says which phone settings to change before the hand-over. The computer and the cloud are the general guides' job; the phone's library, before a child uses it, is this article's.

Rule of thumb: a hand-over is a deadline with small hands after it — copy the whole library oldest-first while the phone is still in your hand, check a few photos on the drive, put the drive away at home, turn on the purchase setting and pin the screen with the phone's own features, and only then hand it over.
