What you need

An AGM phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk) — the H6 and the H6 Pro list USB On-The-Go as Yes, and the H6 Lite lists USB Type-C with USB On-The-Go — so no adapter is needed for a USB-C flash drive on the current models, while a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter — and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Three AGM specifics: first, OTG is not a question on the current H6 family — spec tables list USB On-The-Go as Yes for the H6 and the H6 Pro, and USB Type-C with USB On-The-Go for the H6 Lite, and AGM's own product page lists OTG among the H6's features. Second, AGM has no phone-to-USB backup tool of its own — the H6 manual's storage mention is Drive, Google's cloud, and the stock photo backup is Google Photos; AGM camera photos live in DCIM/Camera, as on any Android phone. The H6 has a 3-card slot — two Nano SIM slots and a dedicated microSD slot, up to 512GB on AGM's own product page — so a card is an option, and unlike the hybrid slots on other rugged phones it does not cost you a second SIM, but the card stays in the phone and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader, while a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is. Third, SnapDisk installs from the v0.1.2 APK on the GitHub release page today and from Play once the listing is live. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. It mounts on plug-in on most AGM phones — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. The H6, H6 Pro, and H6 Lite all have OTG, so the drive should appear on any of them.
  2. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — AGM camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  3. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: a file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

A file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — the Google Photos community answer is to download the photos from Google Photos to the phone and then move them to USB with Files by Google, and Files by Google can access USB storage on Android 7.0 and later, and either is the manual route for external storage: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. 2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

If you want AGM's own route, know what each tool actually does — the H6 manual's storage mention is Drive, Google's cloud, and the stock photo backup is Google Photos, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google backup is the first way the stock guides describe — Settings > Google > All services > Backup, "Backup by Google One" on — it backs apps, settings, photos, videos, and contacts up to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead. 3. A computer copy is the generic economical method — the H6 manual says the USB-C port is "for charging and data transfer", so connect the phone to a computer with a USB cable and copy the files using the file browser. It is a real backup — but it needs a PC in between, and the backup lands on the PC, not on a USB drive as plain files straight from the phone. 4. A microSD card is the local option AGM's own product page names — the H6 has a 3-card slot with two Nano SIM slots and a dedicated microSD slot, up to 512GB. It is a real local copy — but the card stays in the phone, and opening the card anywhere else needs a card reader. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • The microSD card is dedicated, not shared. The H6's 3-card slot gives the microSD card its own slot alongside two Nano SIMs, so a card never costs you a second SIM — but a card is still not a USB drive: it stays in the phone and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader. SnapDisk still needs Android 7.0 or later.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into any current AGM — the H6, H6 Pro, and H6 Lite all have USB-C with OTG — so an adapter is only needed for a regular USB-A drive.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google backup and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. The generic backup guides' most economical method backs your phone up — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a screenshot or hard-reset guide for a backup guide. The devicesfaq pages for the AGM H6 are real AGM pages — but they explain how to take a scrolling screenshot or how to hard-reset the phone. Neither one describes backing photos up to a USB drive. There is no AGM backup FAQ on devicesfaq at all.
  • Mistaking the manual's Drive mention for a USB backup. The H6 manual lists Drive — "Store your content on the cloud, access it from anywhere, and share it with others" — that is Google's cloud storage, not a copy to a drive you hold. Cloud storage moves no files to a USB drive at all.
  • Mistaking a microSD copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — the H6 takes a card up to 512GB in its own dedicated slot, so no SIM is sacrificed — but the card stays in the phone, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 17, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On AGM the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google backup — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
