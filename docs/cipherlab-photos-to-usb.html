Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Check your model can run SnapDisk. The RS36 runs Android 12 or 14, the RS38 runs Android 13 (upgradable to Android 18), the RK26 runs Android 12 with an Android 14 upgrade path, and the RK25 runs Android 7 or 9 — every model listed clears the Android 7.0 minimum, so SnapDisk installs on all of them. The exception is older CipherLab handhelds below Android 7.0 — a supplier listing shows the RS50 on Android 6 — which cannot run SnapDisk at all, so Method 4 is the route on those units. Check Settings > About phone if you are unsure which Android version your unit runs.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into the USB-C port on the RS36 or the RS38, and a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter on those two; the RK25 needs the OTG adapter that fits its own port. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. One CipherLab wrinkle: no source found confirms OTG or host support on the RK26 at all, so on that model the drive mounting is the test — if the drive never appears in Settings > Storage, the device does not host drives and Method 4 is the route.
  3. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — CipherLab camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  4. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  5. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  6. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: a file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

A file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — the generic guides' route is an OTG connection with the built-in file manager, and it is the manual route for external storage: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a USB-C drive plugs straight into the USB-C port on the RS36 or the RS38, a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter on those two, and the RK25 needs the OTG adapter that fits its own port. A notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. On the RK26, if the drive never appears, the device does not host drives and Method 4 is the route. 2. Open the file manager (Files by Google on CipherLab handhelds) and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option. One note: the file-manager route works on older CipherLab handhelds below Android 7.0 too — it needs no app install, so the Android 7.0 minimum does not apply to it — but those units still need to host the drive, so the drive mounting is the test there as well.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • OTG is confirmed on three of the four models, but unconfirmed on the RK26. The RS36, the RS38, and the RK25 all list OTG in their spec interfaces — but no source found confirms OTG or host support on the RK26 at all: its user guide names a USB-C port (USB 3.1 Super Speed Gen 1) and describes charging and communication by USB-C cable to a PC or laptop, and says nothing about hosting drives. If the drive never appears in Settings > Storage on the RK26, the device does not host drives and Method 4 is the route.

A USB-C flash drive plugs straight into the RS36 or the RS38 — so no adapter is needed on either of them for a USB-C drive — while a regular USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter. The RK25 needs the OTG adapter that fits its own port. A plain USB-C charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the device to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • Assuming the RK26 hosts drives because the RS36 and the RS38 do. The RS36, the RS38, and the RK25 all list OTG in their spec interfaces — but no source found confirms OTG or host support on the RK26 at all. Test with a drive on your model, and use Method 2 or 4 on it if the drive never mounts.
  • Installing SnapDisk on a CipherLab unit below Android 7.0. Older CipherLab handhelds run Android 6 or earlier — a supplier listing shows the RS50 on Android 6 — check Settings > About phone first: on an Android 6 unit the computer copy in Method 4 is the route, not this article's one-tap method.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your device up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. A computer copy with a USB cable moves files to a computer — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The CipherLab spec sheets and the RS38 retailer listing are real CipherLab pages — but the spec sheets only list an Android version, a chipset, or a USB connector type, and the retailer listing only describes the hardware. Neither of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking a manual for a phone-to-USB guide. The RS36, RS38, and RK26 user manuals are real CipherLab documents — but the RK26 user guide's only file advice is a USB connection to a host computer, and none of the manuals describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking OTG support on one model for OTG support on the other. The RS36, the RS38, and the RK25 all list OTG in their spec interfaces — but no source found confirms it on the RK26, so test with a drive on your model, and use Method 2 or 4 on it if the drive never mounts.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • Mistaking a camera-card bridge for a handheld backup. Using a phone as a bridge between a camera's memory card and an SSD is a real field backup — but it backs up the camera's card, not the handheld's own gallery. If your photos are on the RS36 itself, the drive plugs straight into the handheld, and Method 1 or 2 is the route.
  • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup. Copying DCIM to a microSD card is a real local copy — the RS36, the RS38, and the RK25 all take a card — but the card stays in the device, and opening it anywhere else needs a card reader; a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 18, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a CipherLab handheld the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the device.
