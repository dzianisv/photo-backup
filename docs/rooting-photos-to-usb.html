Why unlocking the bootloader is the deadline

Unlocking the bootloader is not a reminder to back up — it is a deadline, and the deadline is before the unlock command runs, not when the phone reboots:

  • The wipe is by design, not an accident. Unlocking erases all user data so that someone who gains physical access cannot unlock a stolen phone and read what is on it — LineageOS installation guides print the warning, "Unlocking the bootloader will erase all data on your device!", and Android's own documentation describes the bootloader transition as a data-wiping event. It is not a risk of unlocking. It is what unlocking does.
  • The wipe is total. Photos, videos, downloads, messages, app data, authenticator seeds — everything on the user data partition goes. A backup that lives only on the phone goes with it: a copy stored in internal storage is deleted by the very wipe it was meant to survive.
  • After the wipe there is no recovery. No app, no recovery mode, and no undo brings back photos that were never copied anywhere else. The backup has to exist before the unlock command runs — not after the phone reboots, because by then the photos are already gone.
  • The unlock is the point of no return. Before it, the phone still boots, the photos are still there, and the copy can still run. After it, the phone is empty and setting itself up again — and the photos are either on the drive or gone for good.
  • The flash can fail too. A wrong ROM, a bad cable, or a dead battery mid-flash can leave the phone unbootable — and a phone that does not boot cannot back anything up. The copy has to be done while the phone still works, not after the flash goes wrong.
  • The phone needs its port for the computer. Flashing runs over USB from a computer — adb and fastboot — so the drive cannot stay in the port while the flash runs. The drive comes out before flashing starts, and stays aside until the new ROM boots.
  • The new system may not restore the old backups. LineageOS warns that some manufacturer backups may not be accessible once the new system is installed. A copy of plain photo files on a drive opens anywhere, with no restore app and no account needed — which is why the drive copy is the backup that survives a system change, not the manufacturer's backup.
  • If the phone cannot be unlocked, the backup is still worth having. A carrier variant with no unlock route still holds photos worth keeping — and the copy costs one evening, while the wipe costs everything. Check unlockability first, and back up either way.

None of this is fixed by backing up after the phone reboots empty — after a wipe with no copy anywhere else, the photos are gone, and the backup is already too late. The fix is a copy that finishes before the unlock, oldest-first, verified on the drive, and kept aside while the phone is flashed.

What you need

  • An Android phone running Android 7.0 or later that still works normally, with a dry, undamaged USB port — SnapDisk needs minSdk 24 in the repo. Never plug anything into a damaged port: a bent, loose, or wet port can short the board, and power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos. A dead phone is a repair-lab job only — no software route. On an iPhone, use Method 2: the Files-app copy has no version floor, and SnapDisk is Android only — and there is no bootloader to unlock on an iPhone in the first place.
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. For a whole library, a portable SSD holds more — but a portable SSD may need a powered hub, because the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • A charged phone, or a powered hub — one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both. Charge first, then plug the drive in — or use a powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do it at home before the unlock, not at the flashing desk, and do not run the unlock until the check is done.
  • Whether the phone can be unlocked at all — check before you plan the flash, not after the backup. Open Developer options and look for the OEM unlocking toggle: if it is missing or greyed out, the phone may be carrier-locked or unsupported, and the manufacturer's instructions are the next step, not the unlock command.
  • The ROM, the matching recovery, and the stock firmware ready before the wipe — the guides all say to download everything first — because after the wipe the phone cannot download anything for itself, and the stock firmware is the way back if the flash fails.
  • No Wi-Fi needed — the drive copy runs entirely offline, and SnapDisk has no internet permission at all. Wi-Fi matters only if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the phone will keep filling it — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job, one evening at home. The phone's photos need only the drive; the charger needs to be done before the drive goes in — and the unlock needs to wait until the drive copy is checked.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive, and it needs no Wi-Fi at all. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you start. Two honest limits first: the copy has to finish and be checked before the unlock command runs — a copy still running when the wipe starts is not a backup — and SnapDisk backs up photos and videos only, so the authenticator exports, chat backups, and system image in the next section are separate jobs. And the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-rooting run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified.

  1. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos on the phone are the ones the wipe takes first.
  2. Check the phone can be unlocked, before you plan the flash. Open Developer options and look for the OEM unlocking toggle. If it is missing or greyed out, stop: check the manufacturer's instructions for your exact model and carrier variant. The backup is still worth doing — but the unlock plan changes.
  3. Charge the phone first. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — so charge to full, then unplug the charger and plug the drive in. A powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  4. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  5. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  6. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  7. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  8. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  9. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it, before the unlock. The unlock cannot wait for a backup, and the wipe cannot be undone. If the copy is interrupted, resume it — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  10. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  11. Unplug the drive and put it aside — not in the bag with the phone, and not plugged in during the flash. The USB port is needed for the computer and fastboot, so the drive cannot stay in while the flash runs. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A phone that has never been backed up often holds a library bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size, so the next flash is covered the moment you plug the drive in. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every flashing guide above describes for photos — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when you want the backup done now, before the unlock:

  1. Charge the phone first — one port holds the drive or the charger, not both — then plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  2. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  3. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  4. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder the wipe takes with it.
  5. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big and the evening is short.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  7. Unplug the drive and put it aside — not plugged in during the flash, and not in the bag with the phone — and only then think about deleting anything from the phone, after the check.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to be wiped, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time, with the evening you have left.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step most people skip before an unlock — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. And the check has a catch of its own: the unlock wipes the phone, so the cloud copy has to be finished and checked before the unlock, not after the phone reboots empty:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone, and before the unlock, not after. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and keep the drive aside.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones the wipe takes with it.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the Wi-Fi, the account, or the subscription — and unlike a manufacturer backup, it still opens after the new system is installed.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, before the unlock — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on Wi-Fi, an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round — and why, before an unlock, the drive copy is the backup that is finished when you need it, not the upload that is still running. And the cloud is not the whole pre-unlock job either: authenticator seeds, chat backups, and the system image are separate — that is the next section.

Before you unlock: the wipe, the drive, and the unlock

The copy is done while the phone still boots — the unlock is where the backup has to survive, not where it gets made. And one thing to be plain about: the photo backup is only part of the pre-unlock job. SnapDisk backs up photos and videos only — it does not back up app data, authenticator seeds, messages, or system state — so the rest of this section is your job, in the apps themselves, before the unlock command runs:

  • Back up what SnapDisk does not. Export your authenticator accounts and save the recovery codes — an authenticator database does not survive a wipe without its export procedure, and a lost seed locks you out of the accounts it guards. Run the in-app backup for your chats — WhatsApp and Signal keep their own backups, and the cloud check in Method 3 does not cover them. Back up messages, contacts, and call history in the apps that hold them, and check each one on another device before the unlock.
  • Take a TWRP nandroid if you want the whole system back. A nandroid — system, data, and boot — is the full system image the guides describe, and it is the only backup that restores the operating-system state as it is now. It only helps if it is taken and moved off the phone before the wipe: a backup stored only in internal storage is deleted by the unlock. Whether TWRP can run on your phone before the unlock depends on the exact model — check the device-specific guide, and do not count on a nandroid you have not actually taken and verified.
  • Download everything before the wipe. The ROM, the matching recovery, the stock firmware or factory image for the way back, and any GApps package — with hashes checked where they are published. After the wipe the phone cannot download anything for itself, and the stock firmware is the recovery asset if the flash fails to boot.
  • Unplug the USB drive before flashing. The USB port is needed for the computer and fastboot — the drive cannot stay in while the flash runs. Keep the drive aside, somewhere safe, until the new ROM boots.
  • Charge the phone and use a known-good data cable. A dead battery or a charge-only cable mid-flash can brick the phone — the guides say to charge to a safe level, connect directly to the computer, and keep a spare cable at hand.
  • Run the unlock from the guides, not from memory. The exact command and key combination vary by manufacturer — fastboot flashing unlock on modern devices, fastboot oem unlock on older ones — and the manufacturer's instructions take priority. This article is the backup; the unlock itself is the guides' job, and the phone will warn you on screen before it wipes — read it before you confirm.
  • Know the costs before you confirm on the phone. Unlocking may void the warranty, it can break Play Integrity so banking apps refuse to run, and it can affect updates, DRM, and carrier features. The phone will warn you — and the warning is the last chance to stop.
  • When the new ROM boots, copy the photos back from the drive. Or leave them on the drive until the phone is set up and stable. The drive is the copy that saved the photos — it stays until the phone is whole again.

The unlock does not make the backup — it tests it. A drive aside, a phone with its non-photo backups done, and a copy that finished before the unlock ran is the whole plan, and the unlock is where you stop worrying about it, not where you start it.

What this does not do

  • Does not back up app data, authenticator seeds, messages, or system state. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Export authenticators, back up chats in the apps themselves, and take a TWRP nandroid for a full system image — all before the unlock.
  • Does not undo a wipe. Once the phone is unlocked and wiped with no copy anywhere else, the photos are gone. The backup has to finish before the unlock command runs, not after the phone reboots empty.
  • Does not unlock the bootloader, root the phone, or flash a ROM. SnapDisk is a backup app — the unlock and the flash are the guides' job, over USB from a computer, and no app on the phone does them for you.
  • Does not keep the drive plugged in during the flash. The USB port is needed for the computer and fastboot — the drive comes out before flashing starts, and stays aside until the new ROM boots.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com before the unlock. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the unlock, not after the phone reboots empty.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no before-rooting run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not charge the phone while the drive is in the port. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, then plug the drive in.
  • Does not power a portable SSD from the phone alone. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. A spinning hard drive may need its own power too.
  • Does not work on a damaged port or a dead phone. Never plug anything into a damaged USB port — power through a damaged port can burn out the chip that holds the photos — and a dead phone is a repair-lab job only, not a free app.
  • Does not make an unlockable phone out of a locked one. If the carrier variant cannot be unlocked, no app changes that — check the OEM unlocking toggle and the manufacturer's instructions before planning the flash, and do not pay for one-click unlock tools or remote services.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only — and there is no bootloader to unlock on an iPhone in the first place. On an iPhone the photo route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive — and the same rules apply: copy at home, verify on the drive, and keep the drive aside.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Running the unlock before the copy finishes. A copy still running when the wipe starts is not a backup — and after a wipe, there is no recovery. Copy, check, then unlock.
  • Running the unlock before verifying the copy on the drive. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then unlock — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Leaving the drive plugged in during the flash. The USB port is needed for the computer and fastboot. Unplug the drive before flashing starts, and keep it aside until the new ROM boots.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want — before the unlock, not after the phone reboots empty.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder the wipe takes with it.
  • Forgetting the authenticator exports and chat backups. SnapDisk backs up photos and videos only — an authenticator with no export is gone with the wipe, and a chat with no in-app backup does not come back. Export authenticators, save recovery codes, and run the chat backups before the unlock.
  • Counting on a TWRP nandroid you have not taken. A nandroid only helps if it exists, is complete, and is stored off the phone — and whether TWRP can run before the unlock depends on the exact model. Check the device-specific guide, and verify the backup before the wipe.
  • Flashing without the stock firmware at hand. If the ROM fails to boot, the stock firmware is the way back — download it before the wipe, not after, when the phone cannot download anything for itself.
  • Unlocking a phone that cannot be unlocked. A missing or greyed-out OEM unlocking toggle, a carrier variant with no unlock route — check first, and do not pay for one-click unlock tools or remote services. The guides say to treat the phone as unsupported.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the phone is about to be wiped, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then decide.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then decide — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it, not after the copy fails.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole library. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a phone that has never been backed up holds years of photos, so check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole library in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole library is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.
  • Assuming the general guides cover the before-unlock moment. The UMA Technology, PrivacyPortal, Sod Calculator, Awesome Android Root, and RottenWifi guides back up photos to a computer or the cloud — but none of them copies the phone's own library to USB in one tap, none of them keeps the drive aside while the phone is flashed, and none of them says which non-photo backups to finish first. The computer and the cloud are the general guides' job; the phone's library, before the unlock, is this article's.

Rule of thumb: an unlock is a wipe with a command before it — copy the whole library oldest-first while the phone still boots, check a few photos on the drive, unplug the drive and keep it aside, and only then run the unlock.
