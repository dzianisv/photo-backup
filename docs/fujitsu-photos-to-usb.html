Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Check your model can run SnapDisk — and host a drive. All five covered Fujitsu models run Android 7.1 or later, so SnapDisk installs on all of them. The drive is confirmed on four of them — the spec tables list USB Host and USB OTG 1.3 for the We2, the We2 Plus and the N, and smartprix lists USB on-the-go for the Alpha — but on the Arrows Tab F-02K no source found states OTG, so plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. A USB-C dual drive plugs straight into the USB-C port with no adapter; a USB-A drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter. It mounts on plug-in on most Android devices with USB host support — look for a "USB storage added" notification. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive.
  3. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — phone camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else — on a Fujitsu phone, the photos are the 50 MP shots from the Alpha's or the We2's rear camera, the 50 MP shots from the We2 Plus, or the 50 MP shots from the N, plus whatever image files sit in the folders you pick — tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  4. Tap Backup. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification when the drive connects: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" Tap it and your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the device normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  5. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  6. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: the built-in file manager (the stock route, no app needed)

The built-in file manager can copy your photos to the USB drive — on near-stock Android that is Files by Google, and no source found names the stock file manager on a Fujitsu device, so the stock route is the Android one: 1. Plug in the USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying "USB storage added"; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive. 2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Fujitsu offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — its own app, Easy Photo Transfer for arrows, moves photos and videos between phones over Wi-Fi Direct, not to a drive, and was last updated in July 2018 — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 5 GB of free storage by default since May 2026, with the full 15 GB available only after verifying a phone number on the account. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Google backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.

What to avoid

  • Assuming OTG because the port is USB-C. All five Fujitsu models have a USB-C port, and no source found labels the F-02K's port OTG — a USB-C port is not a promise the device hosts drives. Plug in a drive and look for the mount notification before buying an adapter.
  • Assuming your F-02K has the Play Store. No source found states whether the Arrows Tab F-02K ships Google Play. Check for the Play Store on your tablet and sideload the APK if it is absent.
  • Assuming a Stylistic tablet runs Android. The current Stylistic Q and V tablets run Windows 10 or 11 Pro, not Android. SnapDisk is an Android app and cannot install on them at all.
  • Assuming the Alpha is on sale where you live. The Arrows Alpha launched in Japan in summer 2025 with no launch outside Japan in the sources found. A unit outside Japan is a grey import, and the sources found say nothing about local warranty or service for it.
  • Assuming the Alpha's storage size. Most listings give the Alpha 512 GB of storage and one gives 256 GB. Check Settings > Storage on your unit before sizing a backup around the number.
  • Assuming Easy Photo Transfer backs up to USB. Fujitsu's own app, Easy Photo Transfer for arrows, moves photos and videos between phones over Wi-Fi Direct — it never touches a USB drive, and it was last updated in July 2018.
  • Assuming a microSD card is a USB backup. All five models take microSD, but a card is not a drive you can plug into any computer. If what you want is a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive, the card is a second copy, not the backup.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. A file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your device up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup. And Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost — and since May 2026 Google accounts get only 5 GB free by default, with the full 15 GB only after verifying a phone number.
  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The spec tables and the product pages are real documents — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device. The generic Android guides in the search results are the closest thing to a phone-to-USB guide, and they say nothing about Fujitsu.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Fujitsu device the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the device.
