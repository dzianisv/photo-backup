Save Bluesky photos to USB from Android

Your photos on Bluesky live on a server, not on your phone. Save each one in the app — it lands in the Bluesky album — then copy it to a USB drive, no computer.

November 26, 2026 · 15 min read

TL;DR: A photo you see on Bluesky is not on your phone yet. Bluesky is a social network built on the AT Protocol — a post lives on the poster's server, and the photos in it are served from Bluesky's image CDN, not stored on your phone — and the file appears on the phone only when you save it. In the Bluesky app, open the post, tap the photo to open it full-screen, tap the three-dot "Image options" button, and tap "Save image": that is Bluesky's own save, in Bluesky's own open-source app code, and the app answers with "Image saved". The file lands in an album named Bluesky in the Gallery — Bluesky's own code names the album, and if the album cannot be created it falls back to putting the file straight in DCIM — so the folder is a named album plus a short search (Gallery album first, then DCIM in a file manager), never a claimed loose folder and never the Downloads folder, which is where the web download lands instead. If the photo is already saved, there is nothing to save again — the file is already in the Bluesky album, and the backup covers it directly. From wherever the file landed, the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the saved Bluesky photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: Bluesky saves nothing by itself — no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and liking or reposting a photo writes no file at all; the save is one photo at a time, and a post holds at most four photos, so a whole account is an evening of saving post by post, not one tap; a saved photo sorts by the day you saved it, not the day it was taken, because the file is written when you save it; and the save is the full-size copy Bluesky serves, saved as a JPEG, so what you back up is the full file, not a thumbnail. The good news, and it is real: the save is built into the app — no third-party downloader, no login handed to a stranger's website, no screenshot-and-crop — and the saved file is a real file in shared storage, visible in the Gallery and in a file manager. The confusion first: Bluesky is a window onto a server — and a post is not a file

Bluesky sits in the middle of three confusions, and the whole job depends on clearing all three before you tap anything. The first confusion is the name of the thing you are looking at. Bluesky — the social network built on the AT Protocol — is a network of servers: a post lives on the poster's server, and the photos in it are files that live there, served to your phone from Bluesky's image CDN when you open the post. It is the network that comes with the app — the app is Bluesky's own, open-source, and the save this article describes is in its code, not in a third-party tool — and that is exactly why it is confusing: it looks like the Gallery, it behaves like the Gallery when you open a photo in it, and it is not your Gallery. It is a window onto posts that live on servers, and the photos in them are files that live there. A file manager on your phone — the Files app — is a place where files live as files, organised by folder, and you can open them, copy them, and back them up. Bluesky shows you photos; it does not hold them as your files. The file manager can see DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Bluesky account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Bluesky either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no social account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of Bluesky and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a save. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

The second confusion is the one that decides whether the job works at all, and it is the confusion every social app's design invites. Bluesky is where people keep their photos because it is theirs — the photos you posted, the photos people shared with you — and the photos feel like they are on the phone already: they are in the feed, in the posts, in the order you saw them in, all yours, all reachable from any device signed in to the same account. It feels like a backup already, and it is not one. It is a single copy on a server, under that server's rules — and worse, under no sync at all: there is no folder on your phone that mirrors your Bluesky posts, no camera-roll copy that appears when you post, and no setting that keeps a local copy of what you see. A photo with one copy is a photo at risk, and a photo whose only copy sits on a server you do not control is a photo at risk twice over. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph, and it is stated here rather than at the end, because the rest of the article is the answer to it: the network is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo needs a second copy somewhere you hold. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space. And never delete from Bluesky to free space and expect the phone copy to survive: the phone copy is a file you saved, and the post is a post on a server, and deleting the post deletes the post, not the file — but the file is only yours if you saved it first.

The third confusion hides inside the app itself, between the ways you can "have" a photo — and they are not the same thing at all. Saving a photo copies it from Bluesky's CDN to a folder on your phone that other apps can open — into the Bluesky album, a real file in shared storage — and that is the tap this article is about. Seeing a photo in the feed is thinner: the photo loads from the CDN when you scroll past it, and you can open it and look at it, but a photo you are looking at may be a thumbnail-sized preview whose full-size copy still sits on the server — and a look is not a file, and a look is not a backup. Liking a photo is thinner still: it records your like on the server, and a liked photo is a post you liked, not a folder of files. And reposting is a fourth thing, different again: it puts the post on your profile for other people to see, which is the same direction as looking, outwards, and it is not a save either. The only tap that writes the full-size file to a folder you can copy to a USB drive, unchanged, is Save image — and if you have been scrolling through Bluesky photos and wondering why none of them are in the Files app, this is why: you have been looking at photos, and looked-at photos are not saves.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still on Bluesky. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The save is the step that moves the photo from a place the drive cannot reach into a place it can, and the save is a step you have to take yourself, because Bluesky offers no save-them-all setting and no auto-save of photos you view. The one thing that does not always survive is the date, and the date section below says so plainly, with the save-one-and-check rule, because for Bluesky the date problem is a save problem: the file is written when you save it, and the day you saved it is the day it sorts by. And the one thing this article will not do, which the other guides do without saying so, is invent a folder: Bluesky names its folder in its own code — an album called Bluesky, with DCIM as the fallback — and the folder section below is a check, not a claim. The save lands in the album the app names, and that is the one honest sentence in this whole job.

The wall: Bluesky saves nothing by itself — the save is yours to do

Bluesky does not put your Bluesky photos on your phone on its own. That is the first fact, and it is the one that decides the whole route. There is no auto-save, no setting that turns one on, and no folder that fills up by itself as you scroll. Every description of the save describes a manual step — open the photo, open the menu, tap Save image — because the manual step is the only step there is. Bluesky's own app code, read first-hand this run, states the flow in the code itself, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. In the photo viewer, the three-dot button opens an "Image options" menu with two items — "Share image" and "Save image" — and the save item calls the app's own save, which downloads the full-size copy and writes it to the phone's media library, then answers with a toast that reads "Image saved". The long-press route is the same save from a different tap: long-press a photo in the feed and the menu offers "Save image", which saves the full-size copy. There is no other save in the app, and no setting anywhere that saves photos for you: the app saves the photo you tap, when you tap it, and nothing else.

The save itself is a real, built-in step, and it is worth stating exactly, because Bluesky's own code states it in the same words this article uses. Open the post. Tap the photo so it opens full-screen. Tap the three-dot button — the "Image options" button — and tap "Save image". That is Bluesky's own save action: a save of the photo you opened, offered in the photo viewer's own menu. If the photo is already saved — saved before, file still in the album — there is nothing to save again, and the job is already half done, because the file is already in the Bluesky album and the backup covers it directly. For a photo that is not saved yet, the tap downloads the full-size copy from Bluesky's image CDN and saves it to the phone. The same action works from the feed: long-press the photo, tap "Save image", and the same full-size copy lands in the same album. And for the photos you only looked at, the code gives the same step in its own words: the save asks the system for photo-library permission first, and if you denied it, the app tells you so — "Images cannot be saved unless permission is granted to access your photo library" — so grant it in system settings and save again.

There is one more thing the save does not let you choose, and it is worth knowing before you save a hundred photos, because it decides where the files land. Bluesky names its destination in its own code: the save puts the photo in an album called Bluesky. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The save function, in Bluesky's own app, downloads the image, moves it to a permanent path as a .jpg, and then — on Android — looks up an album named Bluesky, puts the image straight into it if the album exists, creates the album with the image if it does not, and if even that fails, puts the image in DCIM itself: the code's own comment on that last step reads "just put it in DCIM". The folder is the one named thing in this whole job, and it is named by Bluesky's own code, not by Android's default: a saved photo lands in the Bluesky album in the Gallery, and the folder-check section below walks the whole search in order. Do not waste an evening looking for the save in the Downloads folder. A save in the app does not go to Downloads. The web download does that, and the web is a different route, covered below. And do not look for the photo sitting loose in a folder called Bluesky on the drive, either: the album is a Gallery album, and the file manager check below says where to look for it.

One more detail, on the permission trap, because it is the reason the save sometimes looks like it did nothing. If you denied the photo-library permission — tapped "Don't allow" the first time the app asked, or denied it in system settings — the save fails silently behind a toast: the app tells you the permission is missing, and no file is written. A save that reports nothing is a save that did not happen, and a backup of nothing is a backup of nothing. The check is the file, not the toast: after the first save, open the Gallery and look for the Bluesky album, and check that the photo is in it — if it is there at full size, the save worked and every save after it works the same way. If it is not there, grant the permission in system settings and save again. Do not save fifty photos and check at the end. Save one, check the album, then save the rest.

What you need. Five things, none of them exotic. An Android phone — Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk — signed in to the Bluesky account that can see the photos, because the photos live in the posts you can open, and a post you cannot open cannot be saved. The network itself, reachable from the phone — on Wi-Fi, or on mobile data — because Bluesky is a network you reach over the network, not a folder on your home network; without a reachable Bluesky there is nothing to save, and a photo that does not load cannot be saved, which is a confusing way to discover the account is wrong. Enough free space on the phone to hold the saves: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, save in batches and copy each batch to the drive before saving the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the save steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a save guide.

One more check, on the Bluesky side: open the photo and confirm it is actually there, and that it is a photo you can save. If the photo is in a post you can open, it is a photo you can try to save. If it is from a post that was deleted, or the photo never loads, it may not save at all — and a photo that does not load cannot be saved. And if the Save image option is missing entirely, that is usually one of two things, not a bug: the photo is already saved and there is nothing to save, or you are looking at a video, not a photo — this guide covers photos, and the viewer's Save image is the photo save. Do not save first and diagnose later: open the photo full-screen first, wait for it to load crisp, and then open the menu. A save of a half-loaded photo is a save of a half-loaded photo.

Method 1: Save one photo in the app, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Bluesky app on your Android phone and sign in if it asks — the sign-in that matters is the Bluesky account, because the photos live in the posts that account can open. Open the post the photo lives in — posts are the units Bluesky organises by, and the photo lives inside one — and tap the photo to open it full-screen. Wait for the photo to load crisp first: a photo that is still loading is a preview, and a save of a preview is a save of a preview. Tap the three-dot button — "Image options" — and tap "Save image". The app answers "Image saved". Then find the file — and finding it is a short step, because Bluesky names the album. Open the Gallery and look for the Bluesky album — and inside it, the photo, under its own file name. If the album is not there, open a file manager and look in DCIM: the app's own code falls back to DCIM when the album cannot be created — and the folder-check section below walks the search in order, with the reason each place is worth checking.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the folder the file landed in is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo the Gallery shows in the Bluesky album is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of the Bluesky album, rename it, or put it anywhere else. Leave it where Bluesky put it. The default run, described in the section after next, reads the media index with no folder filter, and the Bluesky album is scanned into that index like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the photo in the post if you ever need to match them up again.

Once the photo is a file on the phone, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the saved Bluesky photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the saved batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the photos from one post, the photos from one thread. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand tap-and-save taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a whole post, use the post save in Method 2, which gets the post's photos in one pass instead of one tap at a time. For a whole account, use Method 3, which works through the posts one by one instead of one photo at a time. For someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what you can open and save — Bluesky offers none, and this article says so instead of sending you to a downloader. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

If the Save image option does not appear, work through the fixes in order. First, check whether the photo is already saved: open the Gallery and look for the Bluesky album before anything else — it may already be a file, and the job may already be half done, because a photo that is already saved needs no save at all. Second, check that you opened the photo full-screen: tap the photo first, then the three-dot button — the Save image item lives in the photo viewer's menu, not in the post's menu. Third, try the long-press route instead: long-press the photo in the feed and tap "Save image" — the same save serves both. Fourth, check the photo-library permission: open system settings, find Bluesky, and confirm the Photos permission is allowed — a denied permission is the commonest reason the save does nothing. Fifth, update the Bluesky app: the menu wording varies between app versions, and an old build is the likeliest reason a button you expect is not there. Sixth, open the photo on the web instead of the app: sign in to bsky.app in the browser, open the photo, and use Download image from the viewer menu, and the file arrives in the browser's Downloads folder. One of those six fixes the save in almost every case where a fix exists, and the check is always the same: the photo in the Bluesky album, before you save the next one.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to the folder the save landed in — the Bluesky album's folder, or DCIM, or Downloads if you used the web — select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Method 2: A whole post — save its photos one by one, or pull them on the web

Bluesky is blunt about the one bulk step the app does offer, and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the step every other social app in this series offers in some form and Bluesky offers in its own: the app saves one photo at a time, and a post holds at most four photos — the AT Protocol's own image lexicon caps a post's image list at four — so a whole post is at most four saves. Open the post, open each photo full-screen, and save it: tap the photo, tap the three-dot button, tap "Save image", and repeat for the next photo in the post. Each photo saves as its own file into the Bluesky album, at its own file name — and the wait is the save, not the tap — use the batch-size advice below, and check the folder before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

For a batch on the web, the route is the browser, and it is worth following exactly, because the web route has one trap — the Downloads folder — that decides whether it works on a phone at all. Sign in to bsky.app, open the post, open the photo, and use "Download image" from the viewer menu — Bluesky's own web code offers the same save in the browser — or right-click the photo and choose Save image as. One photo arrives as its own file, under its own file name, in the browser's Downloads folder. That is the trap, and it is a real one: the web download lands in Downloads, not in the Bluesky album, and a reader who looks for the batch in the Gallery will not find it until the file is opened — open the photo once, which puts it in the media index, and back it up, or use the From picker route, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. And the web route has its own limit, stated on the same page: the web viewer saves one photo at a time, just like the app — so a whole post does not go through the web in one go either, and the app route is the one for the photos you actually want right now.

The web route is also the route for the photos the app will not save, and it is worth one paragraph, because it is the route for the edge cases. If a photo is in a post someone shared with you, the web view shows the same post, and the same Download image works from there — but check each item, because a photo you can open is a photo you can save, and a photo you cannot open is not yours to take. If the photo is on a phone without the Bluesky app — or the app will not install — the web is the only route: sign in to bsky.app from any browser, open the post, and save from there. And if the Save image option is missing for a single photo in the app but present on the web, the web is the fix — the app and the web do not always agree on what is already on the device, and the web save writes a fresh file either way.

The limits are worth stating plainly, because they decide whether the batch route is the route for your job. The save is the photos you tapped, not the post as an object: the post text, the post order, and the post membership do not travel with the files, so keep the post context yourself — a dated folder on the drive, named for the post, is the simplest way, and the checking section below says so again, because it is the step people skip. Each time more photos are added to a post, you need to save again, or the new photos will not be in the batch. And there is a storage wall to size the batch against, and it is the phone's own: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so a post you want holds only as many photos as the phone has room for — save a batch, back it up, then save the next. For ten photos, save them in the app. For a whole post, save its photos one by one, or use the web and open the files it contains. Use each route for the job it fits, and check the Bluesky album — or the Downloads folder for a web batch — before you start the backup, because the backup can only copy files that are already on the phone.

Method 3: Your whole Bluesky — what Bluesky does and does not export

For a whole account, the honest answer is that Bluesky has no one-tap whole-library photo export in the app, and this article says so instead of inventing one. There is no settings page that zips your entire photo history, no save-all button that compiles everything you ever posted, and no archive that arrives by email with all your photos in it. The save unit in the app is the photo: one photo at a time. If you have ten posts, that is ten saves — or forty photos, if every post holds four. If you have a hundred, that is a hundred — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

The route that does exist for a whole account is the post-by-post save, and it is worth doing in order, because the order is what keeps it from becoming a mess. Start with the posts that matter most — the photos with no other copy anywhere — and work down the list. For each post, save its photos in the app, or save them on the web and open the files, and back up the files it contains before saving the next post. Keep each post's files in their own dated folder on the drive, so the drive ends up with the posts in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower than a single export would be, and it is the route that exists. The app's Save image is the faster route for a single post, and the web Download image is the route for the posts the app will not take. Use each one for the job it fits.

For the whole account at once, the route that exists is not on the phone at all, and the boundary is worth stating because it is specific to Bluesky in a way it is not for the other social apps in this series. Bluesky is built on the AT Protocol, and the protocol serves every public post — and every photo in it — from public addresses: the image CDN serves a full-size copy at a public URL, and the protocol's own sync call fetches the exact file a poster uploaded. That is the honest boundary of this article — it is the protocol route, and this article is the no-computer phone route, so it names the boundary instead of pretending the phone can do the same job. If you have a computer handy, the protocol route is the faster way to pull a whole account by a long way, and the phone route is the one for the photos you actually want right now. Do not leave the job until the account goes away: the time to take the photos out is while the account is running, not after it stops answering.

Two facts about the Bluesky saves are worth stating because they decide who they work for. First, they are for photos you can open: your own photos, and other people's photos where you can open the post. They do not provide an export for someone else's account, and they do not include photos you viewed but did not save — a feed of views is not a feed you can export. Second, the save is not locked to you: a file you save is a file, and a file can be copied, shared, and forwarded by anyone who holds it, so keep the drive somewhere safe and do not forward a batch in a group chat and assume only the people you meant will open it. If the photos need protection, the protection is the drive in your drawer, not the network on the service.

And the wall, stated once, plainly: for someone else's photos there is no bulk route beyond what you can open and save. Bluesky offers no export for an account you do not control, no download-a-whole-account button, and no save-them-all in the app beyond a single photo. The third-party downloaders exist — tools and sites that take a post link and fetch the files — and this article does not use them, does not link them, and does not recommend them, because the app's own Save image does the job they claim to do, and a tool that asks for your Bluesky login to download a post is asking for something it does not need. If the photos are on someone else's account, the honest routes are the ones in this article: save the photos you actually want one by one, or the whole post where you can open it, or ask the person for the files. A thousand one-by-one saves is an evening of tapping that will not get done — and that is the truth, not a reason to hand your account to a scraper.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a saved Bluesky photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a saved photo copies with the rest of the library, whatever folder Bluesky put it in — the Bluesky album, DCIM, Pictures, Download, or any other shared folder. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:23-36, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:60 takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or the folder the Bluesky save landed in.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: save the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery, it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in DCIM: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet — and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route, covered below, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the folder, and the folder is the answer to the question this section asks — the Gallery is a view of the index, and the folder is the files themselves.

What the default run does not do is reach into Bluesky. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Bluesky for anything. A photo still on Bluesky is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still on Bluesky is invisible to the file manager. The save is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Bluesky; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Bluesky photos without saving them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in the Bluesky album came from Bluesky, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/BackupEngine.kt:126. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Bluesky route, the WhatsApp route, the Telegram route, the Messenger route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the save that puts it there.

The From picker route deserves its own paragraph, because it is the route for the reader who wants only the saved Bluesky photos, not the whole library — and because for Bluesky it is the one place the folder matters, which is itself worth saying. Point the From picker at the folder the saves landed in — the Bluesky album's folder, or DCIM if that is where the fallback put them — and the app walks that folder directly: listTreeFiles, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:90-95, walks the document tree recursively and returns the files oldest-first, and isMediaName, at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:125-128, keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension from the code's lists. The picker is ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree with no folder pre-selected, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/MainActivity.kt:62 and :71, so you choose the folder yourself, and the walk does not depend on the media index at all. The honest limit is that no SnapDisk test has picked the Bluesky album folder for Bluesky saves yet, so that route is code-supported and untested on this exact job — use it, but verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, and treat the default run as the road that always works. And the one thing to get right: for Bluesky, the picker must point at the folder the save landed in — the Bluesky album, or DCIM — not at Downloads, which is where only a web batch lands.

The date problem: a saved photo shows the save date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo saved from Bluesky shows the day you saved it, not the day it was taken — and that is expected, not a failure. The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:47-48, and the sort order is a CASE expression, at app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:56-58: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a saved file is the save time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a saved Bluesky photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you saved it — unless the taken date survived inside the file.

For Bluesky, the taken date has a worse chance than for the other photo services, and the reason is the quality section below: a Bluesky save is the full-size copy Bluesky serves, saved as a JPEG — and a file written fresh by the save carries the save time as its modified date, and the taken date inside it is the one the upload carried, if it carried one at all. But the date inside the file and the date the media index records are two different things, and the index is what the default run sorts by: the scanner may read the taken date out of the file and record it, or it may record the save date, and no source this run tested which one a given phone does after a Bluesky save. This article states save-date order as the expected case, not as a certainty, because the mechanism is the same one the series has documented for every other app — the file is written by the save, at save time, and its modified date is the save date — while noting the one way Bluesky differs: the save is the full-size file as served, so the taken date is still inside it if the upload kept it. The practical rule is simple, and it is the rule this article gives instead of a certainty: save one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you save a hundred of them.

And there is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Bluesky names the saved file after the file on the CDN, at its own file name, and two saves of the same photo arrive in the same folder under the same name, so the second save replaces the first — there is no numbered copy piling up beside it. Either way, something is lost if you are not careful: a re-save replaces the file you already had, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the photo in the post if you ever need to match them up again. The defence is to save a batch and copy it to the drive before saving the next, or to leave the names alone and let the replace do its job, since the file it replaces is the same file. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was replaced before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to save.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Save one post's photos, back up the Bluesky album, clear it, save the next post's photos, back up again: the drive ends up with the posts in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are save dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

The quality question: the save is the full-size original

A photo saved from Bluesky is the full-size copy Bluesky serves. That is the one place where Bluesky is better than every photo service in this series that hands you a thumbnail and calls it a save, and it is worth stating plainly, because it changes the whole quality question. Bluesky's own code downloads the full-size copy for the save: the save function requests the download preset of the image, and the long-press route passes the full-size address outright — so the file the app writes is the full-size file, saved as a JPEG, and the app download offers no size choice because there is no size to choose. The saved copy is the full-size original as Bluesky serves it, and if the original matters — a photo you took, a photo you want at full resolution — the in-app Save image is the right tool, and this article says so instead of sending you to the web for a quality the app already gives you.

The one place a size choice could exist is the web, and the answer is the same there: the web viewer's Download image is the same save — the same code path, the same full-size copy — so the quality question has the same answer on both routes, and there is no resized copy to get by mistake. The same warning holds for a photo you only looked at, and it is the one place the two ways of seeing a photo differ: the feed shows a smaller preview of the photo, and the full-screen view loads the full-size copy, and the save downloads the full-size copy — so wait for the photo to load crisp before you open the menu, because a save of a half-loaded photo is a save of a half-loaded photo. In the app there is no size choice to get wrong on the save route: the save is the full-size copy, every time.

The limit, for Bluesky, is not the quality and not a quota — it is the tapping. Bluesky publishes no transfer meter on saving your own photos: there is no six-hour window, no published number, no message that the transfer quota is exceeded, because there is no quota to exceed — the photos live in posts you can open, and the only meter in sight is the phone's free space. A library bigger than the phone's free space was never fully saved in the first place — so if the photos you want add up to more than the phone holds, save in batches and copy each batch to the drive before saving the next. If the photos are someone else's — a post you can open but do not own — their visibility applies instead, and a post that stops being visible can still slow you down, so check with them if the saves stall. But for your own posts, the wall is the tapping, not a meter. Do not treat the absence of a quota as a reason to hand your account to a downloader that claims to be faster: the app's own Save image does the job, and a tool that asks for your Bluesky login to do it is asking for something it does not need.

Then there is the video question, and it is worth one paragraph because Bluesky holds videos too — and videos are the one place this guide stops. The viewer's Save image is the photo save: it saves the photo you opened, and this guide covers photos, not videos. A video in a Bluesky post has no Save image in the viewer, and no step in this article saves one. The backup covers videos from there, because the code's lists hold both kinds: .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl are in the image list, in app/src/main/java/dev/dzianisv/snapdisk/backup/PhotoQuery.kt:131-134, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi are in the video list, at :135-137, so a saved photo and a saved video both copy with the default run — once the video is a file on the phone. The check is the file itself, and it takes ten seconds: save the photo, open the folder, and look at what arrived. If it opens full-size, it saved as a photo; if it arrived as a single still frame, the save caught one frame and the motion is gone, and no backup step will put the missing frames back. If the video matters, do not use this guide for it — this guide is the photo route, and it says so instead of pretending the photo save covers videos too.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all for the quality check, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article: save one photo, open it in the Gallery, and look at it full-screen. If it looks crisp at full-screen, the saved copy is good enough for the backup. If it looks soft, load the full-size version before saving — wait for the photo to load crisp before you open the menu, because a save of a half-loaded photo is a save of a half-loaded photo, and the quality of the saved file is the quality of the loaded file, not the quality of the original upload. And check the metadata before you delete the post: a save should be checked for metadata before the post copy is deleted — and a photo whose date does not survive is worth one more check in the file itself before you give up on it.

One more thing about quality, because it decides whether the save is worth doing at all. A photo saved from Bluesky is a JPEG, and JPEG is in the code's image list — .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl — so the default run copies it like any other photo, and the gallery shows it like any other photo. The format is not the trap. The trap is the look: the saved copy is the full-size file, and no backup step will put the missing photos back if you never saved them. Save the photo, check it full-screen, and if it is good enough, back it up. Back up each batch before saving the next, so a stopped batch is a finished batch, not a lost one.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest save, a middle one, and the most recent save. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against the Bluesky album on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and the folder holds the photos plus whatever else landed there — but the photo count should match the number you saved, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you saved, the likely cause is the replace rule from the date section: a second save of the same photo replaced the first under the same file name, so the folder held one copy where you expected two — save one batch at a time and copy each batch before saving the next, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you saved, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Bluesky batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a saved photo shows the save date, not the taken date, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the save, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, save that photo again, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost in the save, not at upload — and because a Bluesky save is the full-size file, a date that does not survive is worth one more check in the file itself before you give up on it.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not hand your Bluesky login to a downloader app or a downloader site that asks for it. The save needs no third party at all: the app's menu does the single photo, the long-press does the feed photo, the web viewer does the batch, and the protocol serves the files for the computer route. A tool that asks for your Bluesky password to save a post is asking for something it does not need, and the account it gets is yours — the same account that holds your posts, your photos, and your follows, not just the photos you meant to save. The in-app save needs no third party at all. Use it.

Do not confuse looking with saving. A photo you opened in the feed is a photo you looked at, and a photo you liked is a photo you liked, not a file. Save the photo with the save action, check the Bluesky album, and then back it up. And do not confuse reposting with saving: reposting puts the post on your profile for other people to see, and a reposted photo is a post you shared, not a folder of files. Know which one you did, and look in the folder that tap writes to.

Do not delete the post to free space. The phone copy is a file you saved, and the post is a post on a server: deleting the post deletes the post, and the file stays — but only if you saved it first, and only if the backup already finished and you checked it. Delete from the phone only after the drive copy is verified, and never delete the post first and assume the file follows: the file is yours because you saved it, not because the post is gone. And do not delete the phone copy before the drive copy is checked, either: the save is the only copy on the phone, and the drive copy is the only copy off it, and deleting either before the other is verified leaves you with one copy again.

Do not save a whole account one photo at a time. A thousand tap-and-save taps is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. Save the handful you actually want in the app, use the post save for a post, and work through your own posts one at a time. The app save is fast and the web save is fiddly, and each one is the right route for the job it fits. And do not expect the save to reach someone else's account: it is for photos you can open, and it will not contain your views either.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not trust the network as the only copy. Bluesky is a copy on a server, not a backup: photos can be deleted, accounts can be closed, and a network you stop paying attention to is a network you have stopped paying attention to — and a post deleted is a post gone, with the photos in it. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the network. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the network is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Save it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
