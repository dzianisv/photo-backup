Sony's Xperia phones run near-stock Android with almost no Sony apps on board — Google Photos is the gallery, Files by Google is the file manager — and Sony's own backup tool needs a PC. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight in. Here is the manual way, and the one-tap version.

What you need

A Sony Xperia 1, 5, or 10 phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk); a USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into a modern Xperia phone, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter; and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Three Xperia specifics: first, there is no OTG switch to enable. Xperia phones run near-stock Android, so unlike OnePlus or Xiaomi there is no "OTG connection" toggle hiding in Settings — plug the drive in and the phone mounts it on its own. If the phone shows no reaction at all, check the USB notification: if it says the phone is charging a connected device, tap it and switch to file transfer. Second, Sony ships almost no apps of its own on recent Xperia phones — Google Photos is the gallery and Files by Google is the file manager — and expandable storage varies by model: the Xperia 1 VI and 10 VI take a microSDXC card in the shared SIM slot (up to 1.5 TB on the 1 VI), so check your own model's specs. If your phone has no card in the slot, a USB drive is the plug-in storage you get. One speed note: the Xperia 1 VI's USB-C port runs at USB 3.2 Gen 1 speed (5 Gbit/s), while the Xperia 10 VI's is USB 2.0 — a big library copies noticeably faster on the flagship. Third, Xperia phones ship with Google Play, so SnapDisk installs from the v0.1.2 APK on the GitHub release page today and from Play once the listing is live — there is no Huawei-style sideload-only situation here. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Xperia camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. No switch to enable first — Xperia mounts the drive on its own. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?"
  3. Tap Backup. Your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: Files by Google (the stock route, no app needed)

Recent Xperia phones ship with Files by Google as the stock file manager, and it can copy your photos to the USB drive: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear; tap it to open the drive. 2. Open Files by Google, scroll to Storage devices, and tap your USB drive to confirm it mounted. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or tap the three dots and Select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until Files by Google says it's done. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: Xperia Companion (Sony's own tool — needs a computer)

If you want Sony's own route, Xperia Companion is the PC software Sony recommends for backing up an Xperia: install it on a Windows PC or Mac, plug the phone in with a USB cable, open Xperia Companion, and click Backup. It backs up call logs, messages, calendar, settings, and media — including photos — to the computer. This works fine as a device backup. Its limits, for photos specifically: it needs a computer in between, so it is not a phone-to-USB copy at all; and the backup is stored as a package on the PC, not as a folder of plain photo files you can open anywhere — you restore it through Xperia Companion. Older Xperia phones also shipped an Xperia Backup & Restore app that could write a backup to a USB drive connected through a USB host adapter, but the same limit applies: it writes a backup, not your photos as files, and you restore it through that app. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

Method 4: through a computer (if you have one)

If a PC or Mac is handy, the old route still works: plug the Xperia phone into the computer with a USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer (by default an Xperia phone charges only) — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive, or use Xperia Companion to back the phone up to the PC first. This works fine, and it is the only route above that needs a second device in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Xperia Companion is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same backup, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.

What to avoid

  • Hunting for an OTG switch that isn't there. Xperia has no OTG toggle — that is a OnePlus/Xiaomi thing. If your Xperia phone ignores a drive, the switch is not the problem: check the cable or adapter, check the USB notification, and check the drive is formatted exFAT or FAT32 — stock Android cannot mount NTFS drives.
  • Mistaking a backup package for a photo copy. Xperia Companion writes a backup to the computer, not your photos as files. If you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy — that is Method 1 or 2, not Xperia Companion.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC, or Xperia Companion, works — but both need the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On Xperia the cloud route is Google Photos — and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
