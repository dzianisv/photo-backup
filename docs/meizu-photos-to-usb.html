Method 1: SnapDisk on the eight (the one-tap route)

On the eight phones that clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG, the automated route exists. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — the M6s runs 7.0, the E3 runs 7.1, the 15 and 15 Plus run 7.1.2, the 16X runs 8.1, the 16s, 16Xs and Note 9 run 9.0 — and the one-tap backup needs the phone to host a drive, which all eight list. The other seven phones that clear 7.0 — the Pro 7 Plus, M6 Note, 15 Lite, 16th and 17 with OTG disputed, and the Pro 7 and M6 with OTG No — have the drive mounting as the test, or no phone route at all:

  1. Install SnapDisk. Until the Play listing is live, install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page. It installs on Android 7.0 and later, so it installs on the fifteen phones that clear 7.0 — the Pro 7, Pro 7 Plus, M6, M6 Note, M6s, E3, 15, 15 Plus, 15 Lite, 16th, 16X, 16s, 16Xs, Note 9 and 17 — and the one-tap backup to a drive works on the eight that list USB OTG, the M6s, E3, 15, 15 Plus, 16X, 16s, 16Xs and Note 9. On the other seven, SnapDisk installs but the one-tap route is unproven or absent until a drive mounts. Kimovil lists the M6 Note without Google Mobile services, so on a Chinese-variant M6 Note the APK sideload stays the install route even once the Play listing is live. It does not install on the other six: they run Android 6.0 or 5.1, below the minimum, so Method 3 or 4 is the route on those.
  2. Plug in the USB drive with the right adapter. The E3, 15, 15 Plus, 16X, 16s, 16Xs and Note 9 take USB Type-C, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into those seven, or a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. On the M6s the adapter is micro-USB: it takes micro-USB and needs a micro-USB OTG adapter. On the Pro 7, Pro 7 Plus, 15 Lite, 16th and 17 a USB-C drive plugs straight in too. On the M6 and M6 Note the adapter is micro-USB as well.
  3. Look for the mount notification, then tap Backup in the notification. If the drive mounts, SnapDisk offers the backup when the drive connects, then copies the whole camera roll oldest-first in the background, showing progress, speed and ETA, with Pause and Stop. If nothing mounts on the Pro 7 Plus, M6 Note, 15 Lite, 16th or 17, the phone does not host the drive — the sources dispute OTG on all five, so a no-mount settles it for your unit, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. On the Pro 7 and M6 the drive should not mount at all — both list OTG No — so there is no phone route on those two, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size.
  4. Check the copy, then delete from the phone if you need the space. SnapDisk verifies every file's size after copying, but open a few photos on the drive yourself before deleting anything.

This is the only method in this article that takes the whole library in one tap, with progress, pause, resume and verification. Its limits: it works only on phones that run Android 7.0 and host a drive — and on five of the fifteen that clear 7.0 the sources do not agree that the phone hosts one, and on two more the sources agree it does not. On the six below Android 7.0, only Methods 3 and 4 exist, since none of them can run SnapDisk.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

This is the stock route, and the only phone route besides SnapDisk. It works if — and only if — your phone hosts the drive. On the eight with OTG Yes it should mount; on the five with OTG disputed it is the test; and it does not exist on the Pro 7 and M6, which both list OTG No — and on the six below Android 7.0 the OTG line is No on the MX6 and unstated on the other five, so the drive mounting is the test there too, with no SnapDisk involved:

  1. Plug in the USB drive with an OTG adapter. On the Pro 7, Pro 7 Plus, E3, 15, 15 Plus, 15 Lite, 16th, 16X, 16s, 16Xs, Note 9 and 17 that is a USB-C OTG adapter, or a USB-C flash drive plugged straight in, with the OTG wiring inside the adapter — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. On the M6, M6 Note and M6s, which take micro-USB, a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying the USB storage was added; tap it to open the drive. Method 3: through a computer (the reliable route)

If a PC or Mac is handy, this is the route to trust: plug the Meizu phone into the computer with its USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. Method 5: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Meizu offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — no Meizu-specific backup advice turned up in any source beyond a computer copy, Google Drive, or Google Photos — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open.   • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026 — and on the two models that list OTG No, and the six below Android 7.0, that route may not exist, so the honest answer is that a computer may be needed.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Meizu phone the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
