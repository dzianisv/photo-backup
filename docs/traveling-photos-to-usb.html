What traveling without Wi-Fi actually means for your photos

Traveling changes the backup job, because it takes away the two things a backup at home leans on — the cloud and the second copy — and it adds the one risk a backup at home rarely faces, which is losing the phone itself:

  • The phone is the only copy until the drive copy finishes. At home, the photos are on the phone and in the cloud, or on the phone and on a computer. On the road with no Wi-Fi, there is no cloud copy in progress and no computer to fall back on — the phone is the only copy, and a lost, stolen, or water-damaged phone takes the trip's photos with it. The drive copy is the second copy, and it only counts once it finishes and is checked.
  • No Wi-Fi means no upload at all. A cloud backup does not run slowly on bad Wi-Fi — with no Wi-Fi it does not run. Hotel Wi-Fi, hostel Wi-Fi, and cafe hotspots are the places travelers upload from, and all three are slow, metered, or absent on the days that matter — the days the photos are taken. The drive copy needs none of it: it is local, offline, and it runs at USB speed.
  • The trip ends, and the photos have to survive it. A trip ends with a flight home, a phone wiped for resale, a phone handed to a repair shop, or a phone lost in transit — and every one of those endings erases or removes the only copy if the drive copy never happened. The backup has to finish before the trip ends, not after it — after the trip, the phone may not be yours to copy from.
  • One copy in one bag is not two copies. A drive in the same bag as the phone is lost with the phone. The PetaPixel guide ends every trip with three separate physical copies — one card in the camera, one card on the person, one SSD in the checked bag — and the same rule holds here: the phone in your pocket, the drive in a different bag. Separation is the backup.
  • The copy has to be verified, not assumed. A copy that was interrupted, a drive that disconnected, a file that never wrote — none of them shows up until you open a photo on the drive and look at it. On the road, the check is the backup: open a few photos on the drive — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones — before the phone leaves your hands.
  • The last night is the worst night to start. A whole trip's library is tens of gigabytes, and a copy that big takes time, battery, and a charged phone. Starting on the last night means starting with the least battery, the least time, and the most to lose. Copy every few days, while the trip is still going — and the last-night copy is a top-up, not the whole library.
  • Deleting from the phone is not part of the trip. Freeing space by deleting photos from the phone before the drive copy is verified leaves the photos in neither place if the copy failed. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — deleting is your step, after the check, and on a trip the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.

None of this is fixed by uploading later — later is after the loss, and after the loss the phone is not yours to upload from. The fix is a copy that finishes while the trip is still going: oldest-first, verified on the drive, and kept in a different bag from the phone.

What you need

  • An Android phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk).
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone does not see the drive, try a different adapter. For a whole trip's library, a portable SSD holds more — but a portable SSD may need a powered hub, because the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Check first how much your photos take — Settings, then Storage, shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare. A trip adds photos every day, so check the size before you start, not halfway through.
  • An exFAT drive if you have the choice — a drive formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, so a very long video will be skipped and listed, not copied. Reformatting erases the drive, so copy existing files elsewhere first.
  • A charger or a power bank — a big library is a long copy, and the phone powers the USB drive while it copies. Charge first if you can, because one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — or use a powered USB-C hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • A powered USB-C hub, if you are backing up a camera card too — the hub is what lets the card reader and the SSD share the phone's one port, and it has to be externally powered to run the SSD. That is Method 4.
  • Wi-Fi, if you want to check the cloud copy in Method 3 — the drive copy itself needs none of it. SnapDisk has no internet permission at all.
  • Time to let the copy finish, and time to check it — the backup is only a backup once you have opened a few photos on the drive. Do not wait until the last night of the trip, and do not delete from the phone until the check is done.
  • What you should not buy: do not buy a drive with no space to grow into — the trip will keep filling it — and do not buy a cable and call it an adapter — a charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.

One drive, one job at a time. The phone's photos need only the drive; the charger needs only the port, before the drive goes in.

Method 1: SnapDisk — the phone, one tap

SnapDisk is the one-tap version, and it runs entirely on the phone — the photos never leave it until they are on the drive, and it needs no Wi-Fi at all. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which is minSdk 24 in the repo, and it is not on Google Play yet, so install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page before you travel. One honest limit first: the copy runs as a foreground service, which is the mechanism Android gives a copy that must keep running with the screen off — but the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no travel run has ever been tested, so screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified.

  1. Check how much the photos take. Open Settings, then Storage, and look at the photos number. That is the size the drive has to hold — and the reason to copy oldest-first, because the earliest photos of the trip are the ones a lost phone puts most at risk.
  2. Charge first if you can. A big library is a long copy, and the phone powers the USB drive while it copies — one USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both, so charge before the drive goes in, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging.
  3. Open SnapDisk. It opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos), To /disk/DCIM on the drive.
  4. Check From — and check it twice, because a phone's photos may not all sit in DCIM. If your photos live somewhere else — a messaging app's folder, a downloads folder, screenshots — tap the From picker and choose that folder. The picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it. Repeat the copy for each folder that holds photos you want to keep.
  5. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?" with Backup and Cancel.
  6. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally, with live progress — percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left — and Pause and Stop.
  7. Turn the screen off and let it run. The copy is a foreground service, built to keep running with the screen off. Wake the phone when you want to check progress — the notification shows percent, GB copied / total, speed, and time left — then turn the screen off again. If the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume — that is advice, not a verified fact, because battery-saver behaviour with SnapDisk is untested.
  8. Wait for the copy to finish — all of it. The backup has to complete before the phone is wiped, lost, or the trip ends, and a trip ending cannot wait for a backup. If the copy is interrupted, resume it — finished files stay on the drive, and resume picks up where it stopped.
  9. Open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones. If they open at full quality, the copy is good.
  10. Unplug the drive and put it in a different bag from the phone. A backup that travels in the same bag as the phone is lost with the phone — separation is the backup. Do not delete the photos from the phone yet: SnapDisk copies, it never deletes, and the phone stays a second copy until the drive copy is checked — deleting is your step, after the check, and on a trip the check is the drive in your hand, opened, with photos in it.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run — one tap — and running it again past the cap picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. A trip's library is often bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size, so every time you plug the drive in on the road, the new photos copy without a tap. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss — and it copies, it never deletes.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

Same phone, no app to install, and no Play Store or APK needed. This is the manual version of Method 1, and it is the route every phone-to-USB guide above describes — and the route to use when the phone cannot install anything, or when you are already on the road with no way to install:

  1. Plug the USB drive into the phone — directly, if it is a USB-C drive, or through an OTG adapter for a regular USB drive. A notification appears; tap it and choose Transfer photos or File Transfer if it asks.
  2. Open the Files app and find the photos — usually in DCIM, or in Pictures. Select the photos and videos — long-press one, then tap the rest — then tap Copy.
  3. Tap the USB drive in the Files app, open the folder you want them in, and tap Paste. Or drag and drop, if the Files app on your phone supports it.
  4. Repeat for every folder that holds photos — DCIM, Pictures, Downloads, and any messaging app's folder. The Files app copies one selection at a time, so a library spread across folders takes several passes, and a folder you forget is a folder a lost phone takes with it.
  5. Keep the phone unlocked while the copy runs. The screen stays on for the whole transfer — which is the manual copy's cost, and why the background copy is the better route when the library is big and the battery is the thing you are short of.
  6. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before doing anything else — older photos and a long video, not just the most recent ones.
  7. Unplug the drive and put it in a different bag from the phone — separation is the backup — and only then think about deleting anything from the phone, after the check.

Copy, not Move: the Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step, and if the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the trip is still going, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. What the manual copy does not give you is the one-tap backup: no oldest-first order, no progress bar with speed and ETA, no Pause and Stop, and no resume if the copy is interrupted — and on a library that can run to tens of gigabytes, the missing resume is the part you will feel first, because you have to repeat the whole selection next time, with the battery you have left.

Method 3: the cloud trap — check Google Photos before you trust it

This is the step most travelers skip — because the Photos app on the phone shows the photos, and showing them feels like backing them up. It is not. And on the road, the check has a catch of its own: with no Wi-Fi, the upload cannot run at all, so the check waits for Wi-Fi — the drive copy does not:

  1. Open the Google Photos app, tap your profile picture, then Photos settings, then Backup. Check that Backup is on — and check which account it is backing up to, because photos can upload to an account you do not intend to use when you get home.
  2. Check the backup quality. If it is set to storage saver, the cloud holds compressed copies, not the originals — fine for viewing, not the same as the files on the phone. If you want the originals, switch the quality and let the upload finish, or copy the originals to a USB drive with Method 1 instead.
  3. Check the device folders. In Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders — switch on every folder that holds photos you want to keep: WhatsApp images and video, screenshots, downloads, and any other app's folder. Google Photos can back up more than pictures taken with the camera, but only the folders you switch on.
  4. Check the upload actually finished — on another device, not this phone, and on Wi-Fi, not on the road. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone, sign in with the same account, and look for the photos there. Seeing them in the Photos app on this phone proves nothing, because the app shows on-device photos too. A first backup can take up to 24 hours on Wi-Fi while charging — so if the upload is still running, wait for it, or copy to USB with Method 1 and travel now.
  5. Check the storage. Photos and videos count against the shared 15 GB of free Google account storage across Gmail, Drive, and Photos. If the account is full, the upload stopped without telling you clearly, and the photos that never uploaded are the ones a lost phone takes with it.
  6. If anything is missing from the cloud, copy it to a USB drive first. The drive copy is the original file, byte for byte, and it does not depend on the Wi-Fi the trip took away — which is exactly why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round.

Two honest limits: the cloud check only covers Google Photos — if your photos live in Samsung Cloud, Huawei Cloud, or another maker's service, check that service the same way, on another device, when Wi-Fi returns — and a cloud backup is a copy that depends on Wi-Fi, an account, a password, and a subscription, while the drive copy depends on none of those. That is why the drive is Method 1 and the cloud is the check, not the other way round — and why, on a trip with no Wi-Fi, the drive copy is the only backup that exists at all.

Method 4: the camera-card bridge — when the photos live on a card

This is the route the travel guides describe, and it is the right route when the photos live on a camera's memory card rather than in the phone. The phone is the bridge: card in one side, SSD in the other, Files app in the middle:

  1. Power the hub first. Connect a USB-C hub with a built-in card reader to a charger or a power bank through its power input port — the hub must be externally powered to run the SSD, because the phone alone cannot supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. The shawnmoreton.com guide says the same, and the PetaPixel guide powers the hub the same way.
  2. Connect the hub to the phone, insert the SD or CFexpress card into the reader, and plug the portable SSD into one of the hub's ports. Both should appear in the phone's Files app within a few seconds.
  3. Open the Files app, navigate to the card, long-press to select, tap Copy — then navigate to the SSD, long-press on empty space, and tap Paste. The transfer runs in the background; the shawnmoreton.com guide says you can lock the screen while it works.
  4. Wait for the copy to finish, then confirm the files are on the SSD before doing anything else — open a few RAW or JPEG files at full size. Eject both drives through the Files app before physically removing them, and only then format the card in-camera when you are ready to shoot again.
  5. Keep the copies apart: one card in the camera, one card on you, one SSD in the checked bag — the PetaPixel guide's three-copy rule. A card and a drive in the same bag are lost together.

Two things this route is not: it is not a backup of the phone's own library — the card copy covers the camera's photos, and the phone's photos still need Method 1 — and it is not one-tap — the Files-app copy is manual select-copy-paste, with no progress detail, no Pause and Stop, and no resume. The hardware alternative is PurrfectBackup, the pocket-sized copier that copies a card to an SSD with one button and no phone at all — but it is pre-launch, not yet shipping, and it copies cards, not the phone's library, so it is the card route without the phone, not a replacement for Method 1.

What this does not do

  • Does not undo a loss, a theft, or a wipe. Once the phone is gone, the photos on it are gone — there is no un-lose, no recycle bin, and no recovery of what left with the phone. The backup has to finish before the phone is lost, not after it.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data. Back those up in Settings, Google, Backup, before the trip too — and export 2FA tokens and transit passes separately, because a lost phone takes those with everything else.
  • Does not back up photos that live only in the cloud. If your originals live only in Google Photos, they are not on the phone to copy — download them to the phone first, or export them from photos.google.com when Wi-Fi returns. And if they never uploaded at all, Method 3 is how you find out — before the trip ends, not after it.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not promise the copy continues under battery saver. The repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling, and no travel run has been tested — if the copy pauses under battery saver, turn battery saver off for the copy and tap Resume.
  • Does not run past Android's 6-hour dataSync budget without a pause. On Android 15 and later, a dataSync foreground service gets 6 hours in 24 hours — if the copy runs past that, Android pauses it, and opening the app resets the timer.
  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — minSdk 24 in the repo — so on a phone running older Android, use Method 2 instead: the Files-app copy has no version floor.
  • Does not copy files over 4 GB off a FAT32 drive. Drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup, per the repo README. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.
  • Does not charge the phone while the drive is in the port. One USB-C port holds the drive or the charger, not both — charge first, or use a powered hub with pass-through charging, which is standard USB-C behaviour but unverified with SnapDisk.
  • Does not power a portable SSD from the phone alone. A portable SSD may need a powered hub — the phone alone may not supply enough power for both the hub and the drive. A spinning hard drive may need its own power too.
  • Does not fix a phone that will not boot. If the phone will not turn on, no software route exists — check the cloud, then repair.
  • Does not work on iPhone. SnapDisk is Android only. On an iPhone the traveling route is the Files app with a USB-C or Lightning drive, or the camera-card bridge in Method 4 — and the same cloud trap applies: check that the photos actually uploaded before trusting it.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Waiting until the last night of the trip. A whole trip's library is tens of gigabytes, and a copy that big takes time and battery. Copy every few days, while the trip is still going — the last-night copy is a top-up, not the whole library.
  • Starting the backup with a dying battery and no charger. The phone powers the USB drive while it copies, and one port holds the drive or the charger, not both. Charge first, or use a powered hub — a flat battery mid-copy is the interruption you chose.
  • Trusting the cloud without checking it on another device. Seeing photos in the Photos app on the phone proves nothing — the app shows on-device photos too. Open photos.google.com on a computer or another phone and check that the photos are actually there, in the quality you want — when Wi-Fi returns.
  • Forgetting the device folders. Google Photos backs up the camera folder by default — WhatsApp images, screenshots, and downloads have to be switched on separately, in Photos settings, Backup, Back up device folders. A folder you never switched on is a folder a lost phone takes with it.
  • Keeping the only two copies in the same bag. A drive in the same bag as the phone is lost with the phone. Phone in your pocket, drive in a different bag — separation is the backup.
  • Moving instead of copying. The Files app's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone — and the trip is still going, so a failed Move leaves photos in neither place. Copy first, verify, then decide.
  • Deleting from the phone before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you have confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then decide — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. In the Files app, eject the drive before unplugging it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. Use exFAT — and check the drive's format before you are relying on it, not after the copy fails.
  • Reformatting the drive without copying its files off first. Reformatting erases the drive — copy existing files elsewhere before reformatting, and test the drive with the phone before the copy.
  • Buying a drive too small for the whole trip. Make sure it is big enough for the entire library, including videos — a trip adds photos every day, and a drive that filled up yesterday cannot hold today's photos. Check the size in Settings before you buy.
  • Buying a charging cable and calling it an adapter. A charging cable connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. What you need is a data-capable adapter or a USB-C drive.
  • Buying the wrong adapter for the port. USB-C and micro-USB do not fit each other. Check the phone's port before buying — and check the adapter supports data transfer, not just charging.
  • Expecting the free 5 GB cap to cover a whole trip in one run. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run — run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice — but a whole trip is bigger than 5 GB, so it takes several taps, and that is the case Pro is for.
  • Assuming the bridge guides cover the phone. The PetaPixel, desnoulez.fr, and shawnmoreton.com guides back up a camera card, not the phone's library — following one of them leaves the phone's own photos with no backup at all. The card route is Method 4; the phone's library is Method 1.

Rule of thumb: on the road there is no cloud to fall back on, so the copy comes first and the deleting comes never — copy the whole library oldest-first, check a few photos on the drive, keep the drive in a different bag from the phone, and do it every few days while the trip is still going, not on the last night; and check the cloud when Wi-Fi returns, because the Photos app showing a photo is not proof it uploaded.
