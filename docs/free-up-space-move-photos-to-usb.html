How to free up space by moving photos to a USB drive

Your phone is full because your photos are. Move them to a USB drive you can hold, check the copy, then delete the originals — and the storage warning goes away without losing a single picture.

November 15, 2026 · 4 min read

TL;DR: To free up space on Android by moving photos to a USB drive, copy the photos to the drive, open a few on the drive to check they arrived, then delete the originals from the phone. The easy way to do the copy is SnapDisk: plug in the drive, tap Backup in the notification, and your photos and videos copy oldest-first in the background, with live progress and Pause / Stop. The free version covers 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size. One honest note: SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is yours, after you verify the copy. If you'd rather do it by hand, the Files app's "Move to" copies and deletes in one step — just with no resume and no verification if you unplug mid-copy.

Why photos are what fills the phone

On most phones, photos and videos are the single biggest thing in storage — a camera roll grows every day and never shrinks on its own. The usual fix, a cloud "free up space" button, works by uploading every photo first and deleting the local copy after. That needs an account, an upload, and a subscription to keep working. Moving the photos to a USB drive is the same idea without the cloud: the photos leave the phone, but they land on hardware you hold, not on someone else's server.

The order matters more than the tool. A move is two separate acts — copy, then delete — and the only safe order is copy first, check the copy, then delete. Any method that deletes as it copies is gambling that nothing goes wrong mid-copy. This page does the copy and the delete as two steps on purpose.

What you need

  • An Android phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk).
  • A USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. Most modern Android phones support OTG; if the phone doesn't see the drive, try a different adapter.
  • Enough space on the drive for the photos you want to move. Check first how much your photos take — Settings → Storage shows it — and pick a drive with room to spare.

Method 1: SnapDisk (copy first, delete after)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else — and it does it entirely offline. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet), then:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?"
  2. Tap Backup. Photos and videos copy oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. By default it copies from /sdcard/DCIM to /disk/DCIM on the drive; both folders can be changed in Settings.
  3. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  4. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big camera roll partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.
  5. Check the copy on the drive. Open the Files app, browse to the USB drive, and open a few photos — the oldest and the newest. If they open, the copy is good.
  6. Delete the originals from the phone. In the Gallery or the Files app, select the photos you just copied and delete them. Only now is the space actually free — and only now is it safe, because the copies are verified on the drive.

If you unplug the drive mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup.

The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: by hand in the Files app (no app needed)

If you'd rather not install anything, Android's built-in Files app can move your photos to the USB drive — also fully offline:

  1. Plug in the USB drive. A notification or a "USB drive" entry should appear in the Files app under Storage devices.
  2. Open Files, browse to Internal storage → DCIM → Camera. That's where your phone's photos live. (Screenshots are in DCIM → Screenshots; other apps keep their own folders.)
  3. Select the photos, tap ⋮ → Move to, then choose the USB drive as the destination. "Move to" copies each file and deletes the original as it goes — that is what frees the space.
  4. Wait for the move to finish — and don't unplug the drive until the Files app says it's done.

This works fine for a one-off move of a few dozen photos, and it frees the space in a single step. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: "Move to" deletes as it copies, so a mid-move interruption can leave you with neither copy verified; there is no resume if you unplug, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time. For a full camera roll, the app's copy-first-then-delete flow is the safer option. If you want the copy step on its own, see How to back up photos to a USB drive without uploading them.

What to avoid

  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round.
  • "Move to" on the first try. It deletes the originals as it goes. If anything interrupts it — a loose cable, a full drive — the file in flight may be gone from both places. Copy first; move only once you trust the flow.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.
  • Freeing space by deleting the only copy. A photo that exists in one place is not backed up. If the drive is the only copy left, keep it somewhere safe — a drive in a drawer is one dropped drive away from losing everything.

Rule of thumb: the copy is only a backup once you've opened a photo on the drive. Copy, check, then delete — never the other way round.
