Method 1: SnapDisk — proven on nineteen

SnapDisk is the one-tap version. It needs Android 7.0 or later, which all twenty covered HMD phones clear — and the one-tap backup is proven on nineteen of them: each one clears the floor and lists USB OTG Yes. The Key is the one exception — its OTG support is contested — so run the mounting test on the Key first.

  1. Install SnapDisk v0.1.2 — it is not on Google Play yet, so install the APK from the GitHub release page. You do not need an account, and there are no ads.
  2. Plug the USB drive into the phone — straight in if it is a USB-C drive, or through a USB-C OTG adapter for a regular USB-A drive. Every covered HMD phone takes USB Type-C, so the adapter is the same on all twenty.
  3. Check the phone sees the drive before doing anything else. If nothing appears, the drive does not mount and neither this method nor Method 2 works on that phone — on the Key, this is the test that settles the contest.
  4. Open SnapDisk and tap Backup. SnapDisk copies photos and videos oldest-first from the phone to the drive, in the background, with live progress, Pause and Stop.
  5. Wait for the copy to finish, then open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

Free version: backs up manually, up to 5 GB per run. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase — auto-backup on connect and unlimited size. On the nineteen proven phones, SnapDisk installs and the one-tap backup works — they clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG Yes. On the Key, SnapDisk installs — it runs Android 14 Go — but the one-tap backup is unproven until a drive mounts, so plug the drive in first and check the phone sees it.

Method 2: file manager + OTG adapter

Same plug, no app to install. On the nineteen proven phones it works exactly as on any other Android phone with OTG support — once the drive mounts:

  1. Plug the USB drive into the phone through a USB-C OTG adapter — or straight in, if it is a USB-C drive.
  2. Check the phone sees the drive. If nothing appears, the drive does not mount and this method does not work on that phone.
  3. Open the phone's file manager and find the DCIM folder — that is where the camera photos live.
  4. Select the photos and videos, copy them to the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish.
  5. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

On the Key the mounting test comes first — its OTG is contested, phonebunch Not Supported against phonepik Yes — so plug the drive in and check before trusting it there. On the other nineteen the file-manager copy is proven alongside the one-tap backup.

Method 3: through a computer

The fallback that works on all twenty covered phones, including the one no phone-to-USB method reaches with certainty: the Key, if the drive does not mount on it.

  1. Plug the phone into the computer with a USB cable — a plain charging cable works here, because the computer is the other end.
  2. On the phone, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer.
  3. On the computer, open the phone's DCIM folder and copy the photos to the computer, then to the USB drive.
  4. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

This is the method HMD's own guides sit next to — HMD points to the Google backup for moving to a new phone, and the computer copy is the plain-files version of the same idea. It needs the PC in between, so it is the fallback, not the route, when the phone and the drive are all you have.

Method 4: microSD card reader route

If the phone has a card slot — and nineteen do, for certain — the card is the backup, and the reader is the bridge to the USB drive:

  1. Put a microSD card in the phone and copy the DCIM folder to the card with the file manager.
  2. Take the card out, put it in a card reader, plug the reader into the computer, and copy the photos from the card to the USB drive.
  3. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

A microSD card fits nineteen phones for certain, so the card-reader route is proven on nineteen. Ceilings are stated on seventeen, contested on three — the Aura, the Arc and the Vibe 5G — and unstated on two — the Skyline and the Aura 2. The XR21 has no card slot at all, so this method does not work on it. A card copy is a real local copy, but the card stays in the phone until you take it out, and opening it anywhere else needs the card reader — a USB drive plugs into any computer as it is.

Method 5: Google Photos, or the cloud

The cloud is the last resort, and the one HMD's own guides point to: transfer data to a new phone using your Google account, or restore from a Google backup, Settings > System > Backup. HMD offers no bundled cloud storage of its own. The cloud works on all twenty covered phones — every one runs Android, so Google Photos runs on all twenty.

  1. Open Google Photos, turn on backup, and wait for the upload to finish — this needs Wi-Fi, and a big library takes a while.
  2. On a computer, open Google Photos, download the photos, and copy them to the USB drive.
  3. Open a few photos on the drive before deleting anything from the phone.

Two warnings. First, Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost — check the setting before trusting a cloud copy. Second, a cloud backup is not a USB backup: your photos sit on Google's servers, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying for storage. If you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy — that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup.

What this does not do

  • Does not install below Android 7.0. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later, and no covered HMD phone runs below Android 7.0 as shipped — the lowest shipped version is Android 13, on the XR21, the Aura and the T21 — so SnapDisk installs on all twenty. No other HMD phone is below the floor.
  • Does not work if the phone does not host a drive. Nineteen covered HMD phones list USB OTG Yes, so on those nineteen the drive mounting is a formality — but the Key's OTG is contested, phonebunch Not Supported against phonepik Yes, so on the Key plug the drive in and check the phone sees it before trusting either the one-tap backup or the file-manager copy.
  • Does not delete photos. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Does not back up anything but photos and videos. SnapDisk copies image and video files only — no settings, no contacts, no messages, no app data.
  • Does not back up photos from apps that hide them. If an app keeps its photos in a private folder SnapDisk cannot read, those photos stay in the app.
  • Does not compress. SnapDisk copies the original files as they are — no recompression, no resizing, no quality loss.

What to avoid

  • Buying an adapter you don't need — or the wrong one. All twenty phones take USB Type-C, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into every one of them, and a micro-USB OTG adapter is the wrong adapter for every one of them. A plain USB charging cable is not an OTG adapter: it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction.
  • Mistaking a cloud backup for a photo copy. Google Drive and Google Photos back your phone up to Google's servers — if you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy, that is Method 1 or 2, not a cloud backup. HMD has no bundled cloud of its own, and HMD's own guides point to the Google backup — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Mistaking a PC tool for a phone-to-USB copy. A computer copy with a USB cable moves files to a computer — but it is a copy to a computer, and the backup lands on the PC. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route, not a desktop copy.
  • Mistaking a spec table or a catalogue page for a phone-to-USB guide. The HMD spec pages are real HMD pages — but they only list an Android version, a chipset, or a USB connector type. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the phone. HMD's own user guides are the closest thing to HMD backup advice, and they point to the Google backup.
  • Mistaking OTG support on one model for OTG support on the other. Nineteen HMD phones list USB OTG Yes — but the Key is contested, phonebunch Not Supported against phonepik Yes — so do not assume the Key hosts a drive just because it clears Android 7.0, and do not assume a phone that clears Android 7.0 hosts a drive either.   • Mistaking a microSD card copy for a USB backup.   • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying.   • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
