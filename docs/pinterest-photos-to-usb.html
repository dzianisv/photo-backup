Save Pinterest photos to a USB drive

The wall: Save is not Download — Pinterest photos are not on your phone until you download them

Start with the two buttons Pinterest gives you, because they do two different things and the whole job turns on telling them apart — and because the obvious guide gets it wrong by treating them as one. Pinterest's Save button stores a Pin on one of your boards, so you can find it inside Pinterest later. It does not put a file on your phone. Download image is the other action: it creates a local image file on your device. A photo you saved to a board is still inside Pinterest — it is a bookmark to the pin, not a picture in a folder — and no backup app, file manager, or USB drive can reach it, because there is nothing to reach. That is the wall, and it is a quieter one than in the Messenger case: Messenger at least shows you the photo in a chat and the save is a visible step inside it. Pinterest's wall is a word. A board full of saved pins looks like a collection you own, and it is not — it is a collection Pinterest holds for you, and the day the pin is deleted, the board goes private, or the account is gone, the collection goes with it. If you have never downloaded a pin, there is no Pinterest folder on your phone at all, and pointing SnapDisk at a folder that does not exist copies nothing.

The step that puts the photos on the phone is the built-in download, one pin at a time. Open the pin, tap the three-dot menu — its position varies by version, top corner or underneath the image — and tap Download image. Pinterest asks permission to save to your gallery the first time; allow it, or the download cannot write anything. The downloaded pin lands in your gallery, and that is the whole difference between a Pinterest photo you downloaded and one you only saved to a board — the downloaded one is a file, the saved one is a bookmark — and the rest of this article is built around that difference: the work is downloading the pins you want to keep, and once they are downloaded the backup is the one-tap job SnapDisk already does.

Two scope facts, because they decide what this article can and cannot promise. First, the built-in download is one pin at a time, and there is no save-all button for a board: you download pins one by one, from the board or from the home feed. A board that has collected hundreds of pins over a year is a long evening of tapping, and this article does not pretend otherwise — the bulk routes are the PC extensions in Method 3, which download to a computer, not to the phone, and the downloader apps in the same section, which save to the gallery and still leave the USB leg to SnapDisk. Second, not every pin can be downloaded at all: Pinterest's own help says you can download some Pins, and the Download image option is missing on some of them. Pinterest's own advice then is to try downloading from the home feed instead — tap the three-dot menu at the bottom-right of the pin there — or to open the pin in a desktop or mobile browser instead of the app. And videos, Stories, and Carousel Pins cannot be downloaded through the built-in feature at all: Pinterest's help says it is not possible to download video Pins, including your own video Pins, and the independent guides agree the built-in download covers image Pins only. A board of video pins has no phone route in this article, and a screenshot is the only copy left then — a picture of the screen, not the photo, lower quality, carrying the app chrome around it.

And a word on whose photos these are, because Pinterest photos are usually someone else's as much as yours. The pins this article covers are the ones other people made — a recipe photograph, a pattern, a room you want to copy, a picture a seller posted for a listing, the images that pile up on a board over months. Downloading them to your own drive for your own records is the job. They are also someone else's photos in a plain sense: a photographer's work, a maker's design, a shop's product shot. Downloading a file does not grant permission to reuse it — the guides say so plainly, and Pinterest's own help says the same — so re-posting a pin as if it were yours, or sharing it onward as your own work, is a different act, and the same courtesy applies here as anywhere: the pin's creator is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else. This article's methods copy the file to your drive; what you do with it afterwards is yours to decide, and asking is the honest step before anything leaves your phone. Only download content you have permission to keep — and this article makes no watermark-removal claim anywhere: SnapDisk copies the file exactly as Pinterest saved it, watermark and all.

Where downloaded pins land — the guides disagree

This section exists because the folder is the one fact the sources do not agree on, and a guide that picks one of them as certain would be guessing. MakeUseOf, the dedicated save guide, says the downloaded image saves under “Pins” in the phone's gallery: tap Allow when Pinterest asks for gallery permission, and the image is saved under Pins. Pinterest's own help page says the image is saved in the Gallery folder on your device — no album name, no folder path. The newer independent guides hedge further: one says a downloaded file usually appears in Gallery or Files, and to check Gallery, Google Photos, Files, Downloads, and any Pinterest-specific album; another says Pinterest app downloads go to your gallery, browser downloads go to the Downloads folder, and in Google Photos to look under Collections, then On this device, where pictures saved by other apps sit in a separate device folder. Those are three different answers — a named album, a named folder, and a list of places to look — and they disagree because the folder really does vary: by device, by Android version, by Pinterest version, and by gallery app. So this article does not state one path as the path. It gives the check order instead, and the check order is the same on every phone.

Check the gallery first. Open your gallery app and look for a “Pins” album. If it is there, the download works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see the pins, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If the album is not there yet, no pin has been downloaded: download one as in Method 1 and look again. Then check Downloads: if you downloaded the pin from a browser instead of the app — Pinterest's own fallback when the option is missing in the app — the file goes to the browser's Downloads folder, not straight into the gallery. Then check DCIM and Pictures, in that order: a pin saved by the app can land in either, depending on the phone. In Google Photos, look under Collections, then On this device — pictures saved by other apps sit in a separate device folder there, and a pin that is not with your newest photos may be sitting in it. If the pin is in none of those places, check that Pinterest has permission to save photos — without it, the app cannot write to the gallery at all — and download one pin again, watching for the confirmation message at the top of the screen that says the image has been saved.

One more scope fact for this section, because it decides what the drive copy holds: the downloaded file is the pin as Pinterest serves it, not necessarily the original upload. Pinterest's own help says it does not offer a separate higher-quality or original-resolution download option, and one guide adds that if a download looks blurry, the original lives on the pin's destination website, not in the app. SnapDisk copies the file exactly as Pinterest saved it — byte-for-byte, no conversion, no quality change — but it cannot improve a file Pinterest already compressed. If the pin you want is a photographer's full-resolution image, open the pin's destination website and look for the original there; the app download is the served copy, and this article says so rather than promising the original.

What you need

An Android phone — Android 7.0 or later, which is SnapDisk's minimum version — with the Pinterest app installed and signed in, plus one USB drive your phone can read: a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. SnapDisk itself is installed from the v0.1.2 APK — it is not on Google Play yet — so install it by allowing “install unknown apps” for the browser or Files app you download it with, which is standard Android. You need nothing else for the main route: no file manager with hidden files, no extra app, no account. The download step is inside Pinterest itself, and the backup is SnapDisk's default run. Check the drive's space first: Pinterest photos are small — a pin is a few megabytes at most — so space is almost never the problem here, but look anyway, because a whole-library default run copies everything, not just Pinterest. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit, and exFAT is the format to use for a photo drive.

Before any method, settle the one question that decides whether the phone route works at all: does your phone see the USB drive? Plug the drive in and open Settings > Connected devices or Settings > Storage — the drive should appear there. That check is the test, and it takes ten seconds. If the drive does not appear, try a different adapter first — cheap OTG adapters are the usual failure — then a different drive, and a powered hub if the drive is a spinning hard drive rather than a flash drive. And one warning up front, because it applies to every route below: do not delete anything from the phone until the USB copy is done and verified. The pins on the board are Pinterest's copy, and the pins in the gallery are yours — but until the drive copy is checked, the gallery is the only copy outside Pinterest, and deleting from the gallery before the check is how a photo ends up nowhere at all.

Method 1: Download the pins, then back up with SnapDisk

This is the route to use, because it is the only one that joins the whole job — the Pinterest photo, out of the pin and into the gallery, onto a USB drive plugged into the phone, no computer — using only the apps already on the phone plus SnapDisk. It has two halves, and they have to happen in order: the download, which puts the pins on the phone as files, and the SnapDisk copy, which puts them on the drive. The first half is the work. The second half is the backup. Skipping the first half is the mistake this article exists to prevent: running SnapDisk's default backup with nothing downloaded copies every photo in the gallery — and zero Pinterest photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no Pinterest photos to copy.

  1. Download the pins from inside the app. Open the pin, tap the three-dot menu — its position varies by version, top corner or underneath the image — and tap Download image. Allow gallery permission when Pinterest asks, the first time. You can also download from the home feed: tap the three-dot menu at the bottom-right of the pin and choose Download image. There is no save-all button — every pin is one tap — so a board with a hundred pins is a long job, and it is worth doing in sittings rather than all at once. Download the ones that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting. If Download image is missing on a pin, try it from the home feed instead, or open the pin in a mobile browser — Pinterest's own advice for the missing option — and download it there; a browser download goes to the Downloads folder, so check there, not the gallery.
  2. Check the Pins album appeared. Open your gallery app, go to Albums, and look for “Pins”. If it is there, the download works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see the pins, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If the album is not there, work through the check order in the section above: Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, then Google Photos > Collections > On this device. If the pin is in none of those places after a download, check that Pinterest has storage permission — Settings > Apps > Pinterest > Permissions, Files and media, Allow access to media only — and download one pin again, watching for the confirmation message at the top of the screen.
  3. Plug in the drive and run SnapDisk. Plug the USB drive into the phone. SnapDisk offers the backup in a notification — tap Backup — or open the app and tap Start. The run copies oldest-first, verifies every file by size after copying, and shows progress with Pause and Stop in the notification. The free run covers up to 5 GB, and Pinterest photos are small, so one run is almost always enough for the Pinterest half — though a whole-library run copies everything else too, and that is the point: the downloaded pins come along with the rest, with no folder to pick and no step to remember. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  4. Skip the videos, Stories, and Carousel Pins — or screenshot them. The built-in download will not give you a video Pin, a Story, or a Carousel Pin, and no step in this article changes that: Pinterest's help says video Pins cannot be downloaded, including your own, and the independent guides agree the built-in download covers image Pins only. If a video pin matters, a screenshot is the only copy left on the phone — and a screenshot is a picture of the screen, not the photo: it is lower quality, it carries the app chrome around it, and for a video it is one frame, not the video. Do not plan a backup around downloading what Pinterest will not let you download.

Two honest limits. First, the download is not retroactive in the other direction either: downloading a pin today does not put the pins you only saved to a board yesterday onto the phone — a saved pin is a bookmark, and it stays a bookmark until you download it. That is step 1's job, and it is a manual job: one tap per pin, or the bulk routes in Method 3 for the ones you are willing to take to a computer. Second, the 5 GB free cap is per run — a Pinterest album will never reach it, but a whole-library default run might, and running it again picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Third, SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the pins from the gallery after the check is always your step, never the app's — and deleting them from the board is Pinterest's, not SnapDisk's, and it does not free the drive copy. The drive copy is the backup; the gallery copy is the original until you decide otherwise.

Copying only some Pinterest photos? If you would rather copy just a few pins than the whole album, the From folder picker still takes a folder, not single files — so copy the ones you want into their own folder first (in the file manager, or by downloading them to the gallery and selecting them there), then point the picker at that folder. The picker route keeps only recognised photo and video extensions. If the drive is unplugged mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

And the check that matters for this article: after the run, compare the count against the album. A green backup that copied the whole library has still copied zero Pinterest photos if nothing was ever downloaded from the app — which is exactly what happens when the pins were only saved to a board. Open the drive and look for the Pins folder by name: the default run mirrors the source tree, so the album should be there as its own folder. If it is missing, the download is the suspect, not the run — download the pins from the app first, then run again. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — the only files it ever deletes are its own half-finished .part files — so deleting the originals after the check is always your step, never the app's.

Method 2: Point the From picker at the folder that holds the downloaded pins

If you would rather copy just the Pinterest photos and nothing else — and the pins are already downloaded — point SnapDisk's From folder picker at the folder that holds them. Open SnapDisk, tap the From folder picker, and navigate to the folder you found them in, using the check order in the folder section above: the gallery's Pins album first, then Downloads, DCIM, or Pictures — and tap Backup. To get everything in Pictures at once, pick the parent Pictures folder instead: SnapDisk walks the folder recursively, so one run copies the Pins album and any other album that lives under Pictures together. The same 5 GB free cap applies, and the same oldest-first order, size verification, Pause and Stop.

The honest limit on this route is that it is the code-supported route, not the test-proven one — and this article says so rather than claiming a test that never ran — and the folder itself is the second honest limit. The picker is the system's folder picker, and the code has no Pinterest path handling anywhere — zero matches for “pinterest” in the whole source tree — so the navigation is yours, step by step, through folders whose names are ordinary. But the exact folder name is disputed between sources — MakeUseOf says the gallery album is “Pins”, Pinterest's help says the Gallery folder, and the other guides list Downloads and Pictures as candidates — so this article does not state one folder path as the path, and no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet. Neither candidate name starts with a dot, so neither is hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and the folder sits at the top of internal storage, not under Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks the Telegram folder does not apply here at all. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the parent Pictures folder reaches every album under it. But the end-to-end pick is untested, and this article marks it as such. If the picker will not show the folder on your phone, fall back to Method 1: download the pins to the gallery first, then run the default backup, which is the route that is proven to work. And one more scope fact for this route: the folder walk keeps only recognised photo and video extensions — a document in the folder is simply skipped, and a board is never copied, because a board is not a photo or a video. For a whole board, use Method 3 — this article is only about the photo and video files.

Method 3: Bulk board download on a PC — and why it is not a phone-to-USB job

Pinterest has no bulk download of its own, and the bulk routes that exist all run off the phone — and they are worth naming honestly, because they are the first results for every board-download search — and none of them is this job. On a PC, Chrome extensions such as PinSave and Image Downloader download a whole board in one click: install the extension, open the board, select the images or select all, and the files land in the browser's Downloads folder. MultCloud offers the same shape through its cloud service: paste the board URL, fetch the images, download them all — to the PC, or to a cloud drive. On the phone, downloader apps such as PSaver and Downloader for Pinterest save pins to the gallery, one by one or in batches. This article names all four without endorsing any of them: they are third-party tools with their own terms and their own permissions, and none of them was tested this run.

But none of them is a phone-to-USB backup, and the difference is where the files land. The PC extensions put the board on a computer: the files are in the browser's Downloads folder, and they still need a second copy — computer to USB drive — before they are a backup you can open anywhere. The MultCloud route puts the board in a cloud account first, which is the opposite direction from a plain copy you hold. The downloader apps put the pins in the gallery, which is the same place Method 1 puts them — and then the USB leg is still SnapDisk's job, exactly as in Method 1. So use the bulk routes for what they are: the way to get a whole board off Pinterest in one sitting, for the day you need the whole board at once. For plain photo files on a drive you can open anywhere, download the pins to the gallery first — Method 1 — and copy them to a USB drive with SnapDisk, no computer. The two routes are not rivals; they are different shapes, and this article is about the plain-files one. And the same permission rule applies to all of them: only download content you have permission to keep, and do not use a downloader to strip a watermark or re-post someone else's work as your own.

Can SnapDisk read the Pinterest folder directly?

This is the question the whole article turns on, and it deserves a straight answer in three parts — what the code proves it can do, what is untested, and what would prove the rest. The first part is proven, from the code at the current base and from how Android's media index works — and it is the same shape as the Messenger and Viber cases. SnapDisk's default run reads the phone's media index — it queries the MediaStore images and videos tables, oldest-first by date taken, with no folder filter. A Pinterest photo downloaded to the gallery is an ordinary indexed file: it shows in the gallery as a “Pins” album, and gallery apps read the media index. The album's existence in the gallery is the evidence the folder is indexed — if Pinterest placed a .nomedia file in the folder, the media scanner would skip it and the album would not appear in the gallery at all; it does appear, in every guide, so there is no such file. The query is in PhotoQuery.kt, the no-folder-filter shape is the code's own design — the query call passes no selection at all — and the gallery evidence is the platform fact that closes the loop. So the default run sees downloaded Pinterest photos, and it sees them with no step beyond the download. That is not an assumption: it is the code's query shape plus the gallery evidence, stated as such. And the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only — the query reads every indexed image and video on the phone, whatever folder it sits in, so the Pins album is covered the same way DCIM is.

The second part is untested, and this article marks it as such rather than guessing in either direction: whether SnapDisk's From folder picker reaches the folder that holds the downloaded pins end to end on a real phone. The picker is the system's folder picker — ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree, in MainActivity.kt — and it opens with no folder pre-selected: there is no initial-folder hint anywhere in the code, and no Pinterest path handling anywhere in the app — zero matches for “pinterest” in the whole source tree. The navigation chain is ordinary folders — the gallery album, Downloads, DCIM, or Pictures — none of them dot-prefixed, so none of them is hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and the folder sits at the top of internal storage, outside Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks the Telegram folder does not apply here. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the parent Pictures folder should reach every album under it. But the exact folder name is disputed between sources, and no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first.

The third part is what the code does prove about the rest of the job, so the limits above are not mistaken for a wider doubt. The manifest requests the media-read permissions (READ_MEDIA_IMAGES and READ_MEDIA_VIDEO, plus the older READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE capped at Android 12), the notification permission, and foreground-service permissions — and no INTERNET permission at all, zero occurrences, so the photos physically cannot leave the device. It does not request all-files access — no MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE anywhere — which is the permission a file manager uses to browse another app's folders freely, and the absence is the reason SnapDisk cannot simply walk into the Pinterest folder the way a file manager can; the folder picker is the route instead, and it needs your tap. The backup itself is the verified core: oldest-first by date taken with a date-modified fallback (PhotoQuery.kt), every file size-verified after copying (BackupEngine.kt), Pause and Stop in the notification (NotificationHelper.kt, BackupService.kt), files of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive skipped with their reason instead of stalling the job (BackupService.kt), and the free run capped at 5 GB with Pro a one-time $9.99 purchase (ProPrefs.kt). None of that changes the picker verdict — it bounds it. The backup engine is proven; the end-to-end pick of this exact folder is not yet tested.

Photos you pinned vs photos you uploaded

The route is the same for both, but the starting point is not, and it is worth saying which one you are saving before you start. Photos you pinned — the case this article is really about — are the ones the download governs: with a download they land in the gallery, and without it they are not on the phone at all, and the methods above are written for them. Photos you uploaded are different, because you usually have a better original: a photo you uploaded to Pinterest is almost certainly still in your camera roll, in DCIM/Camera, at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file that SnapDisk's default run already covers — and the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only, so it is covered either way. Back that up instead. The copy on the pin is a version Pinterest made for serving — smaller, compressed — and the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping. That matters more than it sounds, because the photos you upload are often the ones you care about most: a picture of the finished work for a client, a photo of the goods before dispatch, the shot you took to show someone what something looks like. The original is the one they will ask for again. The Pinterest route for a photo you uploaded is only needed when the original is gone — you deleted it from the camera roll, or it was a photo you took inside Pinterest's camera and never saved — and then the pin copy is the only one left: download it from the pin the same way as a pinned photo, and the default run picks it up like any other indexed photo, or point the From picker at the folder and copy it directly.

One related corner is worth a line, because Pinterest users hit it and it looks like a shortcut that is not one: Pinterest keeps its working data under Android/data, and a guide may tell you to dig in Android/data for a cached pin. That is a different thing from downloaded photos — the cached copies are fragments keyed to the installation, not your photos in any usable sense — and Android/data is guarded by scoped storage, so a file manager may not even show it. Do not go digging in Android/data for your Pinterest photos: they are not there. The pins are in the gallery once downloaded, or nowhere at all when they are not, and those are the only two places this article's methods touch.

Checking the drive after the copy

The check has two halves, and they have to happen in order: first that the download actually produced a file in the gallery, then that the drive holds it. After the SnapDisk run finishes, open the drive on a computer or on the phone itself and count: the drive should hold the Pinterest photos you downloaded, in a Pins folder of their own — the default run mirrors the source tree, so the album should be there by name — and a few opened at random should render fully, videos included. Open a Pinterest photo itself — not just any photo on the drive — because a run that covered the rest of the library would still look complete on a random check while the one album you cared about was missing. If the count is short, the run is not the first suspect — the download is. Check the gallery: is the “Pins” album actually there, and does it hold the photos you expected? If the album is empty, the pins were never downloaded — download the ones that matter from the app, and run again. If the album holds them and the drive does not, the run is the suspect, and the run needs repeating, not a different folder.

If the drive's free space ran out mid-run, the run stops with the full error shown; free space by moving the drive's contents to a bigger drive first, or back up to a second drive. If the run stopped at the 5 GB free cap — unlikely for a Pinterest album alone, possible on a whole-library default run — run it again and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. And the check that matters most for a photo that only existed inside Pinterest: confirm the drive copy before you delete anything from the phone. The copy in the gallery is an ordinary file and will survive; the copy on the board depends on Pinterest keeping it, and a deleted pin or a replaced phone ends that. The drive is a plain folder of plain files. Anyone who picks up the drive can open them. That is the price of a copy you can open anywhere, and it is worth stating before the photos leave the phone.

What to avoid

  • Assuming Save puts a photo on your phone. It does not — Save pins the image to a board inside Pinterest. A photo you only saved to a board lives inside Pinterest, not in any folder. A one-tap backup copies zero Pinterest photos until you download them from the pin first.
  • Assuming the download covers a whole board. It does not — the built-in download is one pin at a time, and there is no save-all button. A board with a hundred pins is a long job of one tap each; the bulk routes run on a PC, not on the phone.
  • Assuming videos, Stories, and Carousel Pins can be downloaded. They cannot — Pinterest's own help says video Pins cannot be downloaded, including your own, and the guides agree the built-in download covers image Pins only. A screenshot is the only copy left then, and it is a picture of the screen, not the photo.
  • Assuming every image Pin can be downloaded. It cannot — Pinterest says you can download some Pins, and the option is missing on some of them. Try the home feed or a browser instead, per Pinterest's own advice, before giving up on the pin.
  • Assuming one folder path for downloaded pins. UNVERIFIED — the guides disagree: MakeUseOf says a “Pins” album in the gallery, Pinterest's help says the Gallery folder, and the newer guides list Downloads, DCIM, and Pictures. Check the gallery first, then Downloads, DCIM, and Pictures — do not plan a backup around a single path no source agrees on.
  • Assuming the downloaded file is the original upload. It is not necessarily — Pinterest offers no higher-quality or original-resolution download option, so the file is the pin as Pinterest serves it. If you need the original, open the pin's destination website and look for it there.
  • Assuming the default SnapDisk run covers pins you only saved to a board. It cannot — proven from the code and the platform: the default run reads the media index, and a pin that was never downloaded never enters the index. A green backup copies zero Pinterest photos from boards you never downloaded from.
  • Assuming the From picker route is test-proven. Untested — no SnapDisk test has picked the folder that holds the downloaded pins yet, and the exact folder name is disputed between sources. The code supports it and the folder names are ordinary, but do not plan a backup around it as the only route; Method 1's download-to-gallery-first workflow is the proven one.
  • Assuming the pin copy is the original. A photo you uploaded is usually still in DCIM/Camera at full quality. The pin copy is a smaller, compressed version Pinterest made for serving; back the original up, and use the pin copy only when the original is gone.
  • Looking in Android/data for your Pinterest photos. That folder holds Pinterest's working data — cached fragments, not your photos — and scoped storage guards it. The pins are in the gallery once downloaded, or nowhere at all when they are not.
  • Using a PC bulk-board extension and thinking the USB job is done. The extension puts the board on a computer, not on a drive — the files land in the browser's Downloads folder and still need a second copy to reach a USB drive. The USB leg is still SnapDisk's job — the extension replaces nothing in this article.
  • Using a cloud bulk-board service and thinking the copy is yours. MultCloud's route puts the board in a cloud account first, which is the opposite direction from a plain copy you hold. The drive copy is the one you can open anywhere; the cloud copy is a different shape.
  • Assuming a downloader app backs up to USB. It does not — PSaver and Downloader for Pinterest save pins to the gallery, and stop there. The USB leg is still SnapDisk's job, exactly as in Method 1 — and this article names them without endorsing them, for content you have permission to keep only.
  • Downloading someone else's pin to re-post it as your own. Downloading a file does not grant permission to reuse it. The pin's creator is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else — and this article makes no watermark-removal claim anywhere.
  • Assuming screen-off continuation under battery saver. UNVERIFIED — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for a library with long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, and a library with long 4K clips will hold one. SnapDisk skips those files and lists them — use exFAT.
  • Assuming the free 5 GB cap covers the whole library. It is per run. A library over 5 GB needs several free runs — run it again and it picks up where it stopped — or one $9.99 Pro purchase.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. An unplug mid-copy leaves a half-copied library that looks finished until you count it.
  • Checking the drive by opening any photo. Open a Pinterest photo itself — not just any photo on the drive. A random check passes even when the one album you cared about was never copied, because the photos that did copy render fine.

Rule of thumb: Download first, then back it up — Save pins the photo to a board inside Pinterest, Download image is the step that puts a file on your phone, and the gallery is the bridge to the drive. The download is one tap per pin; the backup after it is one tap for all of them.
