Back up Huawei photos to a USB drive (no computer)

Huawei phones have their own built-in backup app, but it writes an encrypted archive — not plain photos you can open anywhere. A USB-C flash drive plugs straight in, and the stock route is the Files app. Here is the manual way, and the one-tap version.

November 16, 2026 · 4 min read

TL;DR: On a Huawei P, Mate, or nova phone, plug a USB drive in, tap Backup in SnapDisk, and your whole photo library copies oldest-first to the drive in the background — with real progress (percent, GB copied / total, speed, time left), Pause and Stop, and resume where it stopped if you unplug. No computer, no upload, no account. The copies are the original files: SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte to the drive, never recompresses or resizes, and checks the size of every file after copying. The honest contrast, up front: Huawei Backup works — it is built into the phone and it writes to a USB drive — but it creates an encrypted backup archive that only a Huawei phone can restore, with a password you must not lose; it is not a folder of plain photos you can open on a computer. The Files app also works, but it is manual select-copy-paste with no progress detail, no resume, and no verification; and cloud backup (Huawei Cloud on newer phones, Google Photos on older ones) is a cloud copy, not a USB copy. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — deleting from the phone afterwards is your step, only after verifying a few photos on the drive. Free version: 5 GB per run; run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. What the search results actually offer

Search "back up photos from Huawei to USB flash drive without computer" and every top result teaches the same manual job or the same encrypted archive, in different packaging:

  • Huawei OTG tutorial (Huawei OTG (Use & Tutorial)) — Huawei's own guide: plug the drive in, open the Files manager, select the photos, Copy, then Paste to the drive. Manual select-copy-paste, start to finish — and it suggests Copy/Paste through the file manager with no progress detail, no resume, and no verification.
  • Huawei Backup (Back Up with an External Storage Device) — Huawei's own support page: connect the phone to a USB drive via an OTG cable, go to Settings, System & updates, Backup & restore, Data backup, External storage, touch USB storage, touch BACK UP, select the data, and set a password. The data is encrypted and backed up, and the password is required to restore it — and the restore runs only through Huawei Backup on a Huawei phone. It backs up the whole device's data, not a folder of plain photo files you can open on any computer.
  • AnExplorer (How to Transfer Files from Android to USB Drive (OTG, No Root)) — connect the drive with an OTG adapter, open AnExplorer, long-press to select files in DCIM/Camera, Copy, Paste to the drive. A thorough manual guide, and the only one of the five that shows a progress bar with speed and time remaining — but it is still a manual copy: you pick the files, and there is no resume or verification.
  • MobiKin (5 Ways to Transfer Files from Android to USB Flash Drive) — PC drag-and-drop, a PC tool (MobiKin Assistant for Android), OTG manual copy, Files by Google, or AirDroid. The PC ways need a computer; the phone-only ways are the same manual select-copy-paste.
  • FoneLab (How to Transfer Photos from Android to Flash Drive in 3 Easy Ways) — OTG manual copy in the Files app (select photos, Move or Copy to the flash drive), a PC tool (FoneLab HyperTrans, phone and drive both plugged into the computer), or Samsung Smart Switch. The OTG way is the same manual file-manager copy, the PC way needs a computer, and Smart Switch is for Samsung phones, not Huawei.
  • Cloud routes — on newer Huawei phones (Mate 30 and later) Google Photos is not preinstalled at all — those phones ship without Google services, so the cloud route is Huawei Cloud, which backs up to Huawei's servers, not to a drive you hold. On older Huawei phones with Google, Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos — that is where quality is actually lost, not on a USB copy. And a cloud backup stops working the day you stop paying for storage.

None of them offers an automated photo-library backup: nothing that takes the entire camera roll, copies it oldest-first in the background as plain files you can open anywhere, shows progress, pauses and resumes, and verifies every file. That is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

What you need

A Huawei P, Mate, or nova phone (Android 7.0 or later for SnapDisk); a USB drive your phone can read — a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into a modern Huawei phone, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter; and enough space on the drive for the photos you want to keep. Three Huawei specifics: first, there is no OTG switch to enable. Huawei's own OTG tutorial just says plug the drive in and open the Files manager — unlike OnePlus or Xiaomi there is no "OTG connection" toggle hiding in Settings. If the phone shows no reaction at all, check the USB notification: if it says the phone is charging a connected device, tap it and switch to file transfer. Second, the stock file manager on Huawei phones is Files, and the stock gallery is Gallery — and expandable storage varies by model: many recent Huawei flagships take Huawei's own NM (Nano Memory) card — the same size as a nano-SIM, in 64, 128, or 256 GB — instead of a microSD card, older models take a regular microSD card, and some have no card slot at all, so check your own model's specs. If your phone has no card slot, a USB drive is the plug-in storage you get. Third, newer Huawei phones (Mate 30 and later) ship without Google services — no Google Play Store, no Google Photos preinstalled — so on those phones SnapDisk installs from the v0.1.2 APK on the GitHub release page, not from Play, and the cloud comparison is Huawei Cloud, not Google Photos. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 can't hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit.

Method 1: SnapDisk (one tap, background copy)

SnapDisk is a free Android app that does exactly this job and nothing else. Install it from the v0.1.2 APK (it's not on Google Play yet — and on a Huawei phone without Google Play, the APK from the GitHub release page is the way in anyway), then:

  1. Open SnapDisk and check the folders. The app opens to a settings screen with a From folder and a To folder, each with a folder picker. The defaults are already right for most people: From /sdcard/DCIM (your camera photos — Huawei camera photos live in DCIM/Camera), To /disk/DCIM on the drive. If your photos live somewhere else, tap the From picker and choose that folder — the picker is the Android system folder picker, and the app keeps access to it.
  2. Plug in the USB drive. No switch to enable first — Huawei mounts the drive on its own. SnapDisk offers a backup in a notification: "External storage is connected. Backup photos?"
  3. Tap Backup. Your whole library copies oldest-first — your earliest photos first — in the background, while you use the phone normally. The copy mirrors the source folder structure on the drive, so DCIM stays DCIM.
  4. Watch the progress notification — real percent, copied / total size, speed, and time left — with Pause and Stop. You can pause and resume any time.
  5. Wait for "Backup complete." Every file is size-verified after copying, so the counts are proof, not a promise. Files that already copied are never copied twice — so if the free 5 GB per-run limit stops a big library partway, just run it again and it picks up where it left off.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the phone to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: Files (the stock route, no app needed)

Huawei phones ship with the Files app as the stock file manager, and it can copy your photos to the USB drive: 1. Plug in the USB drive — a notification should appear; tap it to open the drive. 2. Open Files, tap the USB drive to confirm it mounted. 3. Go back to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. 4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or tap Select all). 5. Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until Files says it's done. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos. This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason SnapDisk exists: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. For a library that grows every day, the app's oldest-first background copy is the calmer option.

Method 3: Huawei Backup (the built-in archive)

If you want Huawei's own route, the Backup app is already on the phone: connect the USB drive with an OTG cable, go to Settings, System & updates, Backup & restore, Data backup, External storage, touch USB storage, touch BACK UP, select the data you want, and follow the prompts to set a password. The data is encrypted and backed up to the drive, and the password is required to restore it — through Huawei Backup, on a Huawei phone. This works fine as a device backup. Its limits, for photos specifically: the backup is an archive, not a folder of photos — you cannot plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures; you can only restore them through Huawei Backup, and only onto a Huawei phone; and if you lose the password, the backup is unreadable. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

Method 4: through a computer (if you have one)

If a PC or Mac is handy, the old route still works: plug the Huawei phone into the computer with a USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer (by default a Huawei phone charges only) — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive, or use Huawei's own HiSuite on the PC to bulk-export the photos first. This works fine, and it is the only route above that needs a second device in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the automatic copy is not oversold:

  • Photos and videos only. SnapDisk copies image and video files. Documents, voice notes, and anything that isn't a photo or video is skipped — Huawei Backup is the tool if you want contacts, messages, and settings in the same archive, not just photos.
  • Only the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you point the From folder at them, or at a parent folder that contains them.
  • Free 5 GB per run. A library bigger than 5 GB stops at the cap; run it again and it picks up where it stopped. Pro ($9.99, one-time) removes the limit and starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are skipped. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive are listed with their reason instead of failing the backup. Reformat the drive to exFAT if you need them.
  • It never deletes. SnapDisk only copies. Freeing space on the phone is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.

What to avoid

  • Hunting for an OTG switch that isn't there. Huawei has no OTG toggle — that is a OnePlus/Xiaomi thing. If your Huawei phone ignores a drive, the switch is not the problem: check the cable or adapter, check the USB notification, and check the drive is formatted exFAT or FAT32 — stock Android cannot mount NTFS drives.
  • Mistaking an encrypted archive for a photo copy. Huawei Backup writes a backup file to the drive, not your photos as files. If you want to plug the drive into a computer and open the pictures, you need a plain copy — that is Method 1 or 2, not Huawei Backup. And if you do use Huawei Backup, keep the password somewhere safe: without it the archive cannot be restored.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC, or HiSuite, works — but both need the PC in between. If the phone and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 16, 2026.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On newer Huawei phones the cloud route is Huawei Cloud, not Google Photos — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the phone — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the phone. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
