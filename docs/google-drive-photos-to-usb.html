Save Google Drive photos to USB from Android

Your photos in Google Drive are files in the cloud, not files on your phone. Download them first — the Drive app writes a real file into Downloads — then copy that file to a USB drive, no computer.

November 25, 2026 · 13 min read

TL;DR: A photo in Google Drive is not on your phone yet. Drive is a cloud file store: you can see the photo, open it, share it, and mark it available offline — and none of that writes a file you can copy to a USB drive. The file appears only when you download it. In the Drive app, tap the three-dot More menu next to the photo and choose Download — Google's own help page gives exactly those two steps — and the file lands in the phone's Downloads folder as an ordinary .jpg or .png. That is the whole first half of the job, and it is the half every other guide skips: the generic Android-to-USB guides start from photos already on the phone, and the Drive guides that do exist route through a computer — download on the PC, then drag to the drive — which is the opposite of what you asked for. From Downloads the backup is the easy half: SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the downloaded Drive photos copy along with everything else, no computer. The honest limits, up front: Make available offline is not a download — it keeps the file inside the Drive app, and a file the Drive app holds is not a file SnapDisk or a file manager can see; a downloaded photo often has no original taken date, so it copies in download-date order rather than the date the photo was taken; selecting several photos at once may hand you one ZIP file instead of separate photos, and SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives, so unzip first; there is no download-a-folder button in the Drive Android app, so a folder is emptied one select-all pass per folder, and a folder with subfolders takes one pass per subfolder; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. As of November 25, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated Google-Drive-photos-to-USB how-to with no computer: the results are generic Android-to-USB guides where Drive is a phone-to-Drive-to-computer-to-USB leg, Google Photos guides that cover a different store entirely, and save guides that stop at the phone.

The confusion first: Google Drive is not Google Photos

Google sells you two places to keep photos, with nearly the same name, and the whole job depends on knowing which one yours are in. Google Drive is a file store. You put files in folders — documents, PDFs, spreadsheets, and photos as files — and you open them from the Drive app, a browser, or any signed-in device. Google Photos is a photo library. You back up your camera roll there, and the photos live in a library organised by date, album, and face, with editing and search built in. They are not two views of the same thing. They are two different stores that happen to draw from the same 15 GB of Google account storage, shared with Gmail as well.

The distinction matters because the download step is different for each, and the guides that confuse them will send you down the wrong menu. Uploading a photo to Drive does not put it in Google Photos. Backing up in Photos does not put a file in Drive. A photo you dragged into a Drive folder in your browser is a Drive file, and it stays a Drive file until you download it. A photo your phone backed up automatically is a Photos library photo, and the Drive app will never show it as a file in a folder, because it was never put in a folder. If you open the Drive app and cannot find a photo you are sure you have, that is the first thing to check: it may be in Photos, not Drive, and this article's steps do not apply to it. The Google Photos route — download from the Photos app, then copy to USB — is a different job, covered by the live2tech guide and the tecnobits guide, and it is not this article. This article covers photos stored as files in Google Drive only.

There is a second confusion hiding inside the first. Google Drive on the web and Google Drive on Android do not offer the same download. On a computer, you right-click a file and choose Download, and the browser saves it to your Downloads folder; select several files and Drive zips them into one archive first. On Android, there is no right-click, and the menu is the three-dot More button next to the file's name. Google's own help page for downloading a file on Android gives the whole procedure in two steps: open the Google Drive app, then next to the file's name tap More and then Download. That is the official route, and it is the route this article uses. Everything else in this article — the folder it lands in, the second save route, the bulk workaround — is built on top of those two steps, and every one of them is checked against a live source, not assumed.

Why does any of this need an article at all? Because the obvious thing — plug the drive into the phone and copy the photos — fails silently when the photos are still in the cloud. A USB drive plugged into an Android phone can only receive files that exist on the phone. The file manager can see Downloads, DCIM, Pictures, and every folder on internal storage; it cannot see inside your Google account. SnapDisk can only copy files the phone's media index holds or a folder you point it at contains; it cannot reach into Drive either — the app has no INTERNET permission at all, which is a deliberate design choice, not a missing feature, and it means no cloud account of any kind is ever in scope for it. So the job is always two jobs: first get the files out of the cloud and onto the phone, then get them off the phone and onto the drive. The first job is a download. The second job is a copy. Guides that describe only one of the two are not wrong about the half they describe; they are useless for the job as a whole, because the half they skip is the half that stops you.

One more thing to settle before the steps: the photos you download are yours in the plainest sense. A .jpg downloaded from Drive is the same file you uploaded — same pixels, same EXIF data, same resolution — because Drive stores files, it does not recompress your photos the way a backup service with a storage-saver setting might. Copy the file and everything worth keeping lives inside the file itself, byte-for-byte. That is the honest centre of this article: a Drive photo is an ordinary image file once it is saved, and the only trap is the save — the difference between a file on your phone and a file in your account, and the difference between the two menu items that look like they do the same thing and do not.

The wall: Download writes a file, Make available offline does not

The Drive app offers two menu items that sound like they save a file, and only one of them does. Tap the three-dot More menu next to a photo and you will see both: Download, and Make available offline. They are not two names for the same action. They are opposite actions with opposite results, and picking the wrong one is the single most common way this job fails — silently, because nothing tells you it failed. You tap the item, the app confirms something, and you go to copy the photo to the drive and find nothing there.

Download writes a real file. Google's help page says so in the plainest terms it has: open the Drive app, next to the file's name tap More, then Download. Three independent guides agree on where the file goes: technologydots says the download is saved to the phone's storage and tells you to open the Files app and check Downloads or recent files; multcloud says the same, with the warning that the destination depends on how you downloaded and that the Files app is the place to check; Android Authority says the file may ask where to save, and if you download something and do not know where to access it, read the guide on finding downloaded files on Android. The file is an ordinary file in shared storage. Any app can reach it: the Files app can see it, the gallery can index it, and SnapDisk can copy it. That is what "a real file" means, and it is the whole point of this step.

Make available offline does not write a file. It keeps a copy inside the Drive app so you can open the photo without a connection. Google's own offline help page describes it exactly that way: open Drive, next to the file tap More, tap Make available offline — and then, to find the file again, open the Drive app and tap Menu, then Offline. The file lives in Drive's private cache, not in shared storage. Multcloud says it directly: a file marked available offline "stays accessible through Google Drive and may not appear as a regular file in Downloads." It will not appear in the Files app. It will not appear in the gallery. SnapDisk will not see it, because SnapDisk reads the media index and the folders you point it at, and a file inside Drive's private storage is in neither. If you tapped Make available offline and then wondered why the backup found nothing, this is why: you saved the photo for Drive, not for the phone.

There is one more way the Download option can disappear entirely, and it is worth knowing before you blame the app. Google's help page carries a tip: when Data Saver is turned on in your network settings, the download option is not available. Technologydots repeats it: if Download does not show up at all, check your network settings, because Google says the option is not available when Data Saver is turned on. If the menu shows Make available offline but no Download, turn Data Saver off first — Settings, Network, Data Saver — and open the menu again. It is a small thing, and it is the kind of small thing that costs an evening if nobody tells you.

So the rule for the whole article, stated once and used everywhere: Download means a file in Downloads; Make available offline means a file in Drive. If you are ever unsure which one you tapped, open the Files app and look in Downloads. If the photo is there, you downloaded it. If it is not, you did not — whatever the app seemed to confirm.

What you need. Four things, none of them exotic. An Android phone with the Google Drive app installed and signed in to the Google account that holds the photos — the account matters, because Drive shows you one account's files at a time, and a photo in a different account's Drive is invisible until you switch accounts in the app. Enough free space on the phone to hold the downloads: the photos have to land on the phone before they can be copied to the drive, so if the phone is nearly full, download in batches and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next. A USB drive the phone can read and write — USB-C straight in, or USB-A through an OTG adapter that carries data, not just power; a charging-only adapter will power the drive and transfer nothing. And the drive formatted in a filesystem the phone can write: exFAT is the safe choice, because FAT32 cannot hold a single file of 4 GB or more, and SnapDisk skips such a file with its reason instead of failing the whole backup.

Check the drive before you start, not after the first batch fails. Plug it in, open Files by Google, and look under Storage devices: if the drive appears, the phone sees it. Copy one small photo to it by hand — select, Copy to, choose the drive — and open it from the drive to confirm it reads back. That one-minute check rules out the three most common hardware failures at once: a charge-only adapter, a drive the phone cannot mount, and a drive formatted in a filesystem the phone cannot write. The rest of this article assumes the drive works, because the download steps are the same either way, and a broken drive deserves its own diagnosis, not a footnote in a download guide.

One more check, on the Drive side: open the Drive app and confirm the photos are actually there. Search for one by name. If the app shows it, it is a Drive file and the steps below apply. If the app does not show it, check the other account — tap the profile picture in the Drive app and switch accounts — and then check Google Photos, because the photo may be a library photo, not a Drive file, and the Photos download is a different menu in a different app. Do not download first and diagnose later: a download of the wrong photo is a waste of the phone's storage and your time, and a download of a photo that was never in Drive is impossible, which is a confusing way to discover the mistake.

Method 1: Download one photo, then back it up with SnapDisk

This is the core route, and it is short enough to do from memory once you have done it once. Open the Google Drive app. Find the photo — browse the folder, or search for it by name. Next to the photo's name, tap the three-dot More menu. Tap Download. A notification tells you when the download has finished; wait for it, because a photo that is still downloading is a half-written file, and a half-written file is not a backup of anything. Then open the Files app, look in Downloads, and confirm the photo is there: the file name should match the one in Drive, and the size should be plausible for a photo, not a few kilobytes.

That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because the Downloads folder is where the whole second half of the job starts. A photo in Downloads is a real file in shared storage, and everything downstream — the gallery, the file manager, SnapDisk — reads from shared storage. You do not need to move the photo out of Downloads, rename it, or put it in DCIM. Leave it where Drive put it. The default run, described in the next section but one, reads the media index with no folder filter, and Downloads is indexed like any other folder. Moving the file is an extra step that can only go wrong: a move that is interrupted leaves the file in neither place, and a rename breaks the link between the file on the drive and the file in Drive if you ever need to match them up again.

There is a second save route in the same menu, and it is worth knowing because it lands the photo somewhere else. Tap More next to the photo, then Send a copy, then Save image — Android Authority and technologydots both describe this route — and the photo lands in your camera roll instead of in Downloads. That is a real file too, and it is a real file in the folder the gallery watches most closely, which makes it the better route if you want the photo to show up in your gallery immediately. But it is a per-photo route, and it is a share-sheet route: the menu you get depends on the apps installed on the phone, and the wording varies by version. Download is the route Google documents, and it is the route this article recommends as the default, because it is the same two taps for every file type, it lands in a folder you can find in any file manager, and it does not depend on the share sheet. Use Send a copy when you specifically want the photo in the camera roll; use Download for everything else.

Once the photo is in Downloads, the backup is one tap. Plug in the USB drive. SnapDisk shows a notification offering Backup — tap it, and the run starts. The app copies oldest-first, in the background, with a progress notification that shows the percentage, the gigabytes copied against the total, the speed, and the time left, and Pause and Stop on the notification itself. You do not pick the photo, the folder, or the file: the default run copies the whole library, and the downloaded Drive photo copies along with everything else. The free version caps each run at 5 GB; if the download batch is bigger than that, run it again past the cap and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup when a disk is connected and unlimited backup size. When the run finishes, open the drive in the Files app and check a few photos on the drive itself — open them from the drive, not from the phone — before you delete anything from the phone. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the originals afterwards is your step, only after verifying the copy.

A note on scale, because it decides whether this method is the right one. Method 1 is one photo at a time, and it is the right method for a handful of photos — the ten you actually want, the folder of twenty from a trip, the scans someone sent you. It is the wrong method for a thousand photos, because a thousand two-tap downloads is an evening of tapping, and an evening of tapping is a job that will not get done. For a folder full of photos, use Method 2, which empties a whole folder in one select-all pass. For a whole Drive account, use Takeout on a computer — Google Takeout packs an entire account into archive files you copy to the drive — and accept that the whole-account job is the one job in this article that genuinely needs a computer. The phone route is for the photos you actually want, not for the archive you might want one day.

Method 2: A whole Drive folder — the select-all workaround (and the ZIP catch)

The Drive Android app has no download-a-folder button. That is not a guess: a Google Drive community thread from 2023 asks exactly this question — is it possible to download a folder from Drive on Android — and the answer is that there is no way to select a folder and download all its contents in the app. The workaround the thread gives is the one this article uses, and it is worth quoting in full because it is the only bulk route the app offers: open the folder, press and hold any file until the top bar shows one file selected, tap the button at the top that selects all files in the folder, tap the three-dot menu in the top right, and tap Download. Every file in the folder downloads. It is four taps for a whole folder instead of two taps per photo, and it is the difference between a feasible job and a tedious one.

There are two catches, and both are worth knowing before you tap Download on fifty files. The first is the ZIP catch. If you select several photos at once, Drive may hand you a single ZIP file instead of separate photos. Gbtimes states it directly: select multiple photos, tap Download, and Google Drive will automatically compress the selected photos into a single ZIP file. That behaviour is documented on the desktop, and several guides report the same ZIP on mobile; this article states it as reported, not as tested on every phone, because the app's behaviour varies by version and by file type. If your download arrives as one .zip in Downloads, do not panic and do not back it up as it is: SnapDisk copies photos, not ZIP archives — .zip is not in the extension list the app copies — so open Files by Google, tap the ZIP, and extract it. Files extracts to a folder in Downloads, and from there the photos are ordinary files again, and the backup is the same as any other saved photo. The extraction takes a minute and costs nothing, but only if you know to do it; a ZIP sitting in Downloads looks like a completed download, and it is not one yet.

The second catch is subfolders. The select-all button selects the files in the folder you are looking at, and only those files. A folder that contains subfolders is not emptied in one pass: the files inside each subfolder need their own select-all pass, inside that subfolder. For a folder with two subfolders, that is three passes; for a folder with twenty, it is twenty-one. At that point the honest advice changes: if the folder tree is deep, the phone route is the wrong tool, and Takeout on a computer — which packs the whole tree, subfolders included, into archives — is the right one. Do not spend an evening tapping through subfolders to prove a point. The phone route is for flat folders and handfuls; the computer route is for trees and archives. Knowing which job you have is the skill, and the skill is worth more than any single workaround.

One more detail on the select-all pass: it selects files, and the Drive app's idea of a file includes things that are not photos. If the folder holds a mix — photos, a PDF, a spreadsheet, a video — the select-all pass downloads all of them, and the download lands in Downloads as a mix. That is fine for the backup: SnapDisk copies the photos and videos and skips the rest, because its extension list keeps only image and video files, and a PDF in Downloads is simply not a photo. But it is not fine for the phone's storage if the folder is large, because every non-photo file you download still takes up space until you delete it. If the folder is mixed and large, download the photos in a separate pass: long-press one photo, tap the other photos one by one, then Download. It is slower, and it is the pass that respects the phone's storage.

Method 3: Point the From picker at Downloads

The default run copies the whole library, and for most people that is the right backup: the downloaded Drive photos copy along with the camera roll, and one tap covers everything. But sometimes the whole library is not what you want. Sometimes the Drive photos are the only ones that matter — the rest of the library is already backed up, or the rest of the library is three years of screenshots you do not want on the drive — and in that case the From picker is the tool. Open SnapDisk, open settings, tap the From folder, and pick Downloads. The app then backs up exactly that folder, and nothing else.

The picker route is code-supported, and it is worth saying exactly what that means, because this article does not claim a test it has not run. The picker is Android's folder picker — ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree, with no folder pre-selected — and the code that lists the folder walks the folder tree directly, recursing into subfolders, and keeps only files whose names end in an image or video extension. That walk does not depend on the media index at all: a photo the index has not picked up yet still copies, because the picker reads the folder, not the index. The extension list it uses is the same list the default run uses, and it includes .jpg, .jpeg, .png, .heic, .heif, .webp, .gif, .bmp, .dng, .raw, .avif, and .jxl for images, and .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .webm, .3gp, .m4v, and .avi for video. A Drive photo is a .jpg or a .png in the ordinary case, and both are in the list.

What this article does not claim is that a SnapDisk test has picked the Downloads folder end to end on a real phone. No SnapDisk test has run a Drive backup end to end on a real phone at all — the app is pre-launch, the backup has been proven on the emulator, and the real-device proof on the owner's Pixel 9a is still an open gate. So the From-picker route is marked code-supported but untested on this exact folder, in the same form the series has used since WL-246: the code path exists, the folder is an ordinary folder in shared storage outside Android/data, and the navigation chain is ordinary folders, none dot-prefixed — but the end-to-end run on this folder has not happened, and the article says so instead of asserting it. If you use this route, verify the copy on the drive before deleting anything, exactly as you would for the default run. The verification is not a formality; it is the test.

There is also a manual route that needs no app at all, and it is worth keeping in the article because it is the fallback when everything else fails. Plug in the drive, open Files by Google, go to Downloads, select the photos, tap Copy to, choose the drive, and tap Copy here. It works, and it works for any file, including the ZIP from Method 2 once you have extracted it — though for the ZIP itself, extract first, because copying a ZIP to the drive backs up an archive, not photos you can open. The manual route has no progress detail, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, and no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole selection next time photos pile up. That is the honest contrast: the manual copy is the route that always works, and SnapDisk is the route that works without the repetition. Use the manual copy to check the drive works; use SnapDisk for the backup itself.

Does SnapDisk's default run cover a downloaded Drive photo?

Yes — with one condition, and the condition is the whole point of this section. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter, so a downloaded photo in the Downloads folder copies with the rest of the library. The code is short enough to quote in full, and this article quotes it rather than paraphrasing it, because the claim is only as strong as the code behind it. The default run calls queryMediaStoreOldestFirst, which queries two tables — MediaStore.Images and MediaStore.Video, the external-content URIs — and merges the results, then sorts them oldest-first. The query itself passes no selection at all: the contentResolver.query call takes the projection, then null, null, then the sort order. Null selection means no filter. No folder filter, no file-name filter, no source filter. Every image and every video the media index holds is in the result, whatever folder it sits in — DCIM, Pictures, Download, or anywhere else in shared storage.

The condition is that the photo has to be in the media index at all. A photo the gallery shows is a photo the index holds, and a photo the index holds is a photo the default run sees. That chain is the test you can run yourself: download the photo, open the gallery, and look for it. If it is in the gallery — in the Download album, or in the camera roll if you used Send a copy — it is indexed, and the default run covers it. If it is not in the gallery, open the Files app and look in Downloads: if the file is there but the gallery does not show it, the index has not picked it up yet, and the fix is to wait a moment or open the file once, which prompts the media scanner; or use the From picker route from Method 3, which reads the folder directly and does not depend on the index at all. Either way, the check is the gallery, and the gallery is the answer to the question this section asks.

What the default run does not do is reach into Google Drive. That is not a limitation to work around; it is the design. The app holds no INTERNET permission — zero occurrences in the manifest — and no Google Play Services dependency, so it cannot open a network connection and cannot ask Drive for anything. A photo still in the cloud is invisible to it, in exactly the same way a photo still in the cloud is invisible to the file manager. The download is not an optional first step you can skip by pointing the app at Drive; it is the step that moves the photo from a place the app cannot reach into a place it can. Anyone who tells you an app can back up Drive photos without downloading them first is selling you a different app, or a misunderstanding.

The default run also does not care where the photo came from. It does not know, and it does not need to know, whether a .jpg in Downloads came from Drive, from a browser, from WhatsApp, or from a camera. It reads the index, it sorts oldest-first, it copies the file byte-for-byte, and it verifies the size of every file after copying — a copy result is only recorded as copied after the verification passes. The source of the photo is irrelevant to the copy, and that irrelevance is a feature: it means the Drive route, the WhatsApp route, the Reddit route, and the camera-roll route are all the same route once the file is on the phone, and the only part that differs is the download that puts it there.

The date problem: a downloaded photo loses its taken date

There is one honest limit in the default run that this article has to state plainly, because it changes the order your photos arrive in on the drive. A photo downloaded from Drive often has no original taken date in the metadata the media index reads — and when the taken date is missing, SnapDisk orders the photo by its download date instead. The photo still copies, byte-for-byte, with every pixel intact. What changes is where it sits in the oldest-first order: a photo taken in 2019 and downloaded today copies as if it were taken today, at the end of the run rather than near the beginning. If the order matters to you — if you want the drive to read like a timeline — that is worth knowing before you start, not after you finish.

The code is explicit about the fallback, and it is worth quoting because it is the difference between a guess and a fact. The query projects both DATE_TAKEN and DATE_MODIFIED, and the sort order is a CASE expression: when DATE_TAKEN is null, use DATE_MODIFIED times a thousand; otherwise use DATE_TAKEN — ascending, with the row id as the tiebreak. The comment above it says it in one line: missing DATE_TAKEN falls back to DATE_MODIFIED. DATE_MODIFIED for a downloaded file is the download time, or close to it. So the run is oldest-first by effective date, and the effective date of a downloaded photo is, in the ordinary case, the day you downloaded it. That is not a bug. It is the only sane fallback when the taken date is not there — and it is the reason the article says "oldest-first by the date the phone knows", not "oldest-first by the date the photo was taken", when the photos came from a download.

Whether the taken date survives at all depends on the file. A .jpg downloaded from Drive keeps its EXIF data, including the taken date, in the ordinary case — Drive stores files, it does not strip them — so a photo uploaded from a camera with its EXIF intact usually downloads with its EXIF intact, and the media index reads the taken date from it. But "usually" is doing work in that sentence, and the cases where it fails are common enough to name. A photo that was uploaded to Drive from Google Photos, rather than from a camera, may carry the upload date rather than the taken date. A photo that was edited in an app that rewrites EXIF may carry the edit date. A screenshot has no taken date at all in the photographic sense — its DATE_TAKEN is the moment of the screenshot, which is the date you want anyway. And a photo downloaded through a browser, rather than through the Drive app, may be re-written by the browser's download handling in ways that drop or alter the metadata. The practical rule is simple: download one photo, check where it lands in the gallery's date order, and you will know what the rest will do. If it lands on its taken date, the batch will too. If it lands on today, the batch will too, and you should decide whether that matters before you download a thousand of them.

There is a second, subtler version of the same problem, and it concerns the file name rather than the date. Drive lets two files in different folders share a name — "IMG_0001.jpg" in the 2019 folder and "IMG_0001.jpg" in the 2021 folder — and when you download both to the same Downloads folder, the second download either overwrites the first or arrives as "IMG_0001 (1).jpg", depending on the app version and the phone. Either way, something is lost: an overwrite loses a photo, and a rename breaks the match between the file on the drive and the file in Drive. The defence is to download one folder at a time and copy each batch to the drive before downloading the next, or to rename the folders in Drive first so the names are unique. It is a dull precaution, and it is the precaution that saves a photo. The backup verifies the size of every file it copies, so a photo that was overwritten before the backup started is a photo the backup cannot know about — the verification proves the copy matches the file on the phone, not that the file on the phone is the photo you meant to download.

If the order on the drive matters more than anything else — if you are building an archive, not just a copy — the From picker route from Method 3 gives you more control than the default run, because you choose the folder, and the folder's contents copy in their own oldest-first order, separate from the rest of the library. Download the 2019 folder, back up Downloads, clear Downloads, download the 2021 folder, back up Downloads again: the drive ends up with the folders in the order you chose, even if the dates inside them are download dates. It is slower, and it is the route for an archive. The default run is the route for a backup. Know which one you are doing.

Photos you uploaded vs photos Google Photos backed up

This section is the untangling, stated as a table and then as a rule, because it is the section readers will come back to when they are stuck. There are two ways a photo ends up in Google's cloud, and they look identical in the Photos app but are different in every way that matters for this job. The first is upload: you, or someone, put a file in a Drive folder — dragged it in a browser, tapped Upload in the Drive app, or saved it there from another app. The photo is a file in a folder, and the Drive app shows it in that folder, and the download steps in this article apply. The second is backup: your phone's Google Photos backup uploaded the camera roll automatically, and the photo lives in your Photos library. The photo is not a file in a folder, and the Drive app does not show it, and the download steps in this article do not apply — the Photos download is a different menu, in the Photos app, and it lands in a different place.

The test is one tap. Open the Drive app and search for the photo by name. If it appears, it is a Drive file: download it from the three-dot menu, and it lands in Downloads. If it does not appear, open Google Photos and search there. If it appears in Photos, it is a library photo: open it in the Photos app, tap More, tap Download — live2tech documents this exact route — and the photo downloads to the phone, from where the USB copy is the same as in this article. If it appears in neither, it is in a different Google account: switch accounts in the Drive app and search again, then switch accounts in Photos and search again. And if it appears in both — which happens when a photo was both uploaded to Drive and backed up in Photos — download it from one place only, because downloading it from both gives you two copies of the same photo with different dates, and the backup will copy both.

The storage is shared, and that shared storage is the reason the two stores get confused. A Google account gets 15 GB shared across Drive, Gmail, and Google Photos, and a photo counts against that 15 GB whether it sits in Drive or in Photos. So the quota does not tell you where a photo is, and the storage screen does not either — it tells you how much you have used, not where you used it. The only reliable test is the search test above: look in Drive, then look in Photos, and believe what you see, not what you remember uploading. People remember uploading a photo to "Google" and forget which Google they uploaded it to, and the confusion is honest — the two apps share a name, a logo family, and an account. The article's job is to make the distinction visible, once, so the reader does not have to discover it by downloading the wrong photo twice.

There is a third case worth naming, because it is the one that produces the angriest support threads. Someone shared a Drive folder with you — a family album, a work folder, a wedding photographer's delivery — and the photos are in "Shared with me", not in "My Drive". Those photos are still Drive files, and the download steps still apply: open the shared folder, tap More, tap Download, and the file lands in Downloads like any other. But they are not your files, and that matters in two ways. First, the owner can remove your access, and a photo you have not downloaded yet is a photo you may lose — so download shared photos promptly, and do not assume they will be there next month. Second, the download counts against your storage while it sits on your phone, and it counts against nothing in Drive, because the file lives in the owner's quota. The USB copy is yours once it is on the drive, and it stays yours even if the share is revoked — which is, after all, the point of a backup.

Checking the drive after the copy

A backup you have not checked is a hope, not a backup. The check takes two minutes, and it is the same check the series has used since the first article, because it is the only check that proves what matters. Plug the drive into the phone — or into a computer, if one is handy — open it in the file manager, and open three photos from the drive itself, not from the phone. Pick one from the beginning of the run, one from the middle, and one from the end: the oldest, a middle one, and the most recent download. If all three open, full-size, from the drive, the copy worked. If one does not open, or opens as a thumbnail, or opens at the wrong size, something went wrong with that file, and the fix is to run the backup again — SnapDisk skips files already copied at the right size and copies the ones that are missing or wrong, so a second run repairs the first without starting over.

Check the count as well as the content. The Files app shows the number of files in a folder and the folder's total size; compare those against Downloads on the phone before you delete anything. The numbers will not match exactly — the drive holds the photos, and Downloads holds the photos plus whatever else was already there — but the photo count should match the number you downloaded, and the total size should be close. If the drive holds far fewer photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the ZIP from Method 2: the ZIP copied as one file, or did not copy at all, because SnapDisk does not copy archives. Extract it, run the backup again, and check the count a second time. If the drive holds far more photos than you downloaded, the likely cause is the default run: it copied the whole library, not just the Drive batch, which is what it is supposed to do, and the extra photos are your camera roll, correctly backed up. That is not an error. It is the default run doing its job, and the article says so because the first time it happens it looks like one.

Check the dates, too, with the expectation set in the date section: a downloaded photo may sit in download-date order, not taken-date order, and that is expected, not a failure. What is a failure is a photo with a date of 1970, or a photo with no date at all in the file manager's list: that usually means the metadata did not survive the download, and the photo itself is still fine — the pixels are all there — but the date is gone, and it will not come back. If the date matters, re-download that photo through the Drive app rather than the browser, and check whether the date survives the second time; if it does not, the metadata was lost at upload, not at download, and no download route will recover it.

Finally, eject the drive before you unplug it. Android's Files app offers an eject or unmount control for the connected drive, and the drive's own notification offers the same. It takes one tap, and it is the difference between a finished write and a corrupted one: unplugging mid-write is the single most common cause of a drive that reads fine on the phone and fails on the computer, or the other way round. SnapDisk's verification — the size check on every file after copying — proves the copy matched the file on the phone at the moment it was written; it cannot prove the drive's filesystem survived being yanked out during the last write. Eject, wait for the confirmation, then unplug. Then put the drive somewhere safe, and label it with the date: a drive you cannot find is not a backup either.

What to avoid

Do not tap Make available offline and call it a download. It is the trap this article is built around, and it is worth repeating in this section because it is the mistake readers make after reading the steps: the two menu items sit next to each other, the offline one sounds more thorough, and the confirmation it gives sounds like a download. It is not. A file available offline lives inside the Drive app, and nothing outside the Drive app can see it. If you have already tapped it, nothing is lost — tap More again and tap Download, and the real file appears in Downloads.

Do not download the whole Drive account to the phone. The phone does not have room for it, and the download will fail partway through with a storage error, leaving a half-downloaded set that is worse than nothing, because you will not know which photos arrived and which did not. Download in batches — one folder at a time, or fifty photos at a time — copy each batch to the drive, and delete the batch from Downloads before starting the next. The batch size is set by the phone's free space, not by your patience: leave at least a gigabyte free at all times, because Android itself needs room to work, and a phone with zero free space stops downloading, stops copying, and stops telling you why.

Do not use Move instead of Copy, in the file manager or anywhere else. Move deletes the original as it goes, which means a failure halfway through the move loses the photos that were already moved and the photos that were not yet moved in different ways, and neither is recoverable from the other. Copy leaves the originals on the phone until you have verified the drive, and the verification is the step that makes the whole job safe. SnapDisk copies and never deletes, for exactly this reason: the only .delete() calls in the code are on SnapDisk's own half-finished .part files, the temporary files of a copy that was interrupted, and they exist so a failed copy does not leave a corrupt file pretending to be a photo.

Do not back up the ZIP. If a multi-select download arrives as one archive, extract it first. A ZIP on the drive is not a backup of photos; it is a backup of a container, and a container you cannot open on the drive is a container you have to bring back to the phone to open, which defeats the point of putting it on the drive. Extract in Files, check that the photos came out, delete the ZIP from Downloads, and then run the backup. The extra minute is the difference between a drive full of photos and a drive full of archives.

Do not trust the cloud as the only copy. Drive is a store, not a backup: files in Drive can be deleted, accounts can be closed, shares can be revoked, and storage quotas can be exceeded, and any one of those turns a photo in Drive into a photo that is gone. The USB drive is the second copy, and the second copy is the one that survives the account. That is the whole argument for this article in one paragraph: the cloud is convenient, the drive is yours, and a photo with one copy is a photo at risk. Download it, copy it, check it, and then — only then — delete the original from the phone if you need the space.

Do not assume the screen-off run finished. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. The Android 15 six-hour dataSync budget is handled: the backup service pauses cleanly with a message when the budget is reached, and the run resumes from where it stopped. But a phone that fell asleep under battery saver is a different case, and this article does not claim it works, because no test has proven it. Plug the phone in, keep the screen on, and watch the progress notification reach 100%. A watched run is a finished run.
