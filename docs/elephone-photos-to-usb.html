Method 1: SnapDisk on the four (the one-tap route)

On the four phones that clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG, the automated route exists. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — the U Pro runs 8.0, the P11 runs 8.0, the P11 3D runs 9.0 and the U2 runs 9.0 — and the one-tap backup needs the phone to host a drive, which all four list. The other four phones that clear 7.0 — the U and P8 3D with OTG half-stated, and the P8 and P8 Max with no OTG stated — have the drive mounting as the test:

  1. Install SnapDisk. Until the Play listing is live, install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page. It installs on Android 7.0 and later, so it installs on the eight phones that clear 7.0 — the P8, P8 3D, P8 Max, U, P11, U Pro, P11 3D and U2 — and the one-tap backup to a drive works on the four that list USB OTG, the U Pro, P11, P11 3D and U2. On the other four, SnapDisk installs but the one-tap route is unproven until a drive mounts. It does not install on the other seven: they run Android 6.0 or older, below the minimum, so Method 3 or 4 is the route on those.
  2. Plug in the USB drive with the right adapter. The U Pro, P11, P11 3D and U2 take USB Type-C, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into those four, or a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. On the U, P8 3D, P8 and P8 Max the adapter is micro-USB: the U takes USB Type-C too, so a USB-C drive plugs straight into it as well, while the P8 3D, P8 and P8 Max take micro-USB and need a micro-USB OTG adapter.
  3. Look for the mount notification, then tap Backup in the notification. If the drive mounts, SnapDisk offers the backup when the drive connects, then copies the whole camera roll oldest-first in the background, showing progress, speed and ETA, with Pause and Stop. If nothing mounts on the U, P8 3D, P8 or P8 Max, the phone does not host the drive — the sources are half-stated or silent on all four, so a no-mount settles it for your unit, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size.
  4. Check the copy, then delete from the phone if you need the space. SnapDisk verifies every file's size after copying, but open a few photos on the drive yourself before deleting anything.

This is the only method in this article that takes the whole library in one tap, with progress, pause, resume and verification. Its limits: it works only on phones that run Android 7.0 and host a drive — and on four of the eight that clear 7.0 the sources do not agree, or do not say, that the phone hosts one. On the seven below Android 7.0, only Methods 3 and 4 exist.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: the file manager and an OTG adapter (works wherever the drive mounts)

This is the stock route, and the only phone route besides SnapDisk. It works if — and only if — your phone hosts the drive. On the U Pro, P11, P11 3D and U2 it should mount; on the U, P8 3D, P8 and P8 Max it is the test; and it is also the phone route on the seven below-7.0 phones, five of which list OTG Yes — the P3000S, P9000 and P20 — while the P6000, P7000 and P8000 have no OTG stated and the P9000 Lite failed OTG in a hands-on test — so on the P3000S, P9000 and P20 the drive should mount too, with no SnapDisk involved:

  1. Plug in the USB drive with an OTG adapter. On the P9000, P9000 Lite, P20, P11, P11 3D, U, U Pro and U2 that is a USB-C OTG adapter, or a USB-C flash drive plugged straight in, with the OTG wiring inside the adapter — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. On the P3000S, P6000, P7000, P8000, P8, P8 3D and P8 Max, which take micro-USB, a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter. A notification should appear saying the USB storage was added; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive — and if the drive never shows up, the phone does not host it, and Method 3 or 4 is the route.
  2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. The pre-installed file manager on an Elephone phone is called Files, or Files by Google, per devicesfaq — use it, or the built-in file manager, whatever it is called on your unit.
  3. Go to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. On these phones the photos are the 13 MP shots from the rear cameras on the P3000S and P6000, the 16 MP shots on the P7000, the 13 MP shots on the P8000, P9000 and P8 3D per phonecorridor's key specs — 13 MP per its table too — the 12 MP shots on the P20, the 21 MP shots on the P8, the 16 MP shots on the P8 Max and P11, the 16 MP + 2 MP shots on the P11 3D per findpare — 13 MP + 2 MP per droidafrica's text — the 13 MP + 13 MP dual shots on the U and U Pro, and the 16 MP + 5 MP + 2 MP triple shots on the U2 per smartprix — 12 MP + 5 MP + 5 MP per wovow — plus whatever image files sit in the folders you pick.
  4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos.

This works fine for a one-off copy, if the drive mounts at all. Its limits are the reason a backup app exists on the four phones: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. And on the U, P8 3D, P8 and P8 Max the drive mounting is the test, since their OTG is half-stated or unstated.

Method 3: through a computer (the reliable route)

If a PC or Mac is handy, this is the route to trust: plug the Elephone phone into the computer with its USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. This works on every covered model, with no Android-version question and no OTG question at all: the computer reads the phone as storage, and the drive plugs into the computer. It is also the only phone-to-drive route for the seven models below Android 7.0, which run Android 6.0 or older and cannot run SnapDisk at all, and for the four where the drive mounting is the test, if the drive never mounts — and it is the route devicesfaq names for Elephone backup, calling the computer copy the most economical method. Its only cost is the second device in between: if the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route on the four, or Method 4 on fourteen of the fifteen.

Fourteen covered Elephone models take a microSD card — up to 128 GB on the P3000S, P8, P8 3D, P8 Max and U per most sources, up to 64 GB on the P6000 and P7000, up to 128 GB on the P8000, up to 256 GB on the P9000 per gizchina and techadvisor — up to 128 GB per techbloat — up to 128 GB on the P9000 Lite, up to 256 GB on the P11 per naijatechgist and philnews — up to 512 GB per smartprix — up to 1 TB on the U and U Pro per phonearena, up to 256 GB on the U2 per whatmobile, and a hybrid slot with an unstated ceiling on the P11 3D — and the card-reader route needs no OTG, no computer connection to the phone, and no app: 1. Copy the DCIM folder from the card to the drive. 4. Put the card back and power the phone on. This is the same route the old phone manuals describe — remove the memory card, place it in a card reader, and let the reader transfer the pictures — and it works identically for a phone card. Three warnings: if the card holds photos the phone has not yet written to it, or photos stored on the phone's internal storage rather than the card, those photos are not on the card — check where your photos live first, in the camera app's storage setting or in the file manager. The ceilings are disputed on five models — the P9000's slot is up to 256 GB per gizchina and techadvisor and up to 128 GB per techbloat, the P11's is up to 256 GB per naijatechgist and philnews and up to 512 GB per smartprix, the U's is up to 128 GB per gizmochina and smartprix and up to 1 TB per phonearena, the P8 3D's is up to 128 GB in phonecorridor's key specs and up to 64 GB in its own table — so check the card slot on your unit before buying a card larger than 64 GB for the P8 3D, larger than 128 GB for the P9000 or the U, or larger than 256 GB for the P11. Unlike Coolpad, where thirteen of fifteen take a card, fourteen covered Elephone phones clearly take one — so this route exists on fourteen, with the P20 unproven.

Method 5: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Elephone offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — no Elephone-specific backup advice turned up in any source beyond a computer copy, Google Drive, or Google Photos — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 5 GB of free storage by default since May 2026, with the full 15 GB available only after verifying a phone number on the account. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1, 2, 3 or 4 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The gadgets360, smartprix and findpare listings are real spec pages — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port type. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026 — and on the seven models below Android 7.0, and the four where the drive mounting is the test, that route may not exist, so the honest answer is that a computer may be needed.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On an Elephone phone the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
