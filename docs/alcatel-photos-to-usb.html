Method 1: SnapDisk on the seven (the one-tap route)

On the seven phones that clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG, the automated route exists. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — the A7 runs 7.0, the Idol 5 and Idol 5S run 7.1, the 1X and 3 (2018) run 8.1 and 8.0, the 5 runs 8.0 and the 7 runs 8.1 — and the one-tap backup needs the phone to host a drive, which all seven list. The other eight phones that clear 7.0 — the 5V, 3L (2019) and 3X (2020) with OTG disputed, the 1S (2019), 1V (2019) and 3 (2019) with OTG unstated, and the 1 and 1SE with OTG No — have the drive mounting as the test, or no phone route at all:

  1. Install SnapDisk. Until the Play listing is live, install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page. It installs on Android 7.0 and later, so it installs on the fifteen phones that clear 7.0 — the A7, Idol 5, Idol 5S, 1, 1X, 3 (2018), 5, 5V, 7, 1S, 1V, 3 (2019), 3L, 3X and 1SE — and the one-tap backup to a drive works on the seven that list USB OTG, the A7, Idol 5, Idol 5S, 1X, 3 (2018), 5 and 7. On the other eight, SnapDisk installs but the one-tap route is unproven or absent until a drive mounts. Kimovil lists the 1S with Google Mobile Services, and the 1S and 3L manuals list Google Play as the app store, so once the Play listing is live the Play install is the route on all covered models. It does not install on the other six: they run Android 6.0, below the minimum, so Method 3 or 4 is the route on those.
  2. Plug in the USB drive with the right adapter. The Idol 5S, 5 and 7 take USB Type-C, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into those three, or a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. The other twelve phones that clear 7.0 — the A7, Idol 5, 1, 1X, 3 (2018), 5V, 1S, 1V, 3 (2019), 3L, 3X and 1SE — take micro-USB, so a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter on any of them.
  3. Look for the mount notification, then tap Backup in the notification. If the drive mounts, SnapDisk offers the backup when the drive connects, then copies the whole camera roll oldest-first in the background, showing progress, speed and ETA, with Pause and Stop. If nothing mounts on the 5V, 3L (2019) or 3X (2020), the phone does not host the drive — the sources dispute OTG on all three, so a no-mount settles it for your unit, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. If nothing mounts on the 1S (2019), 1V (2019) or 3 (2019), the same applies — no source states OTG for any of the three, so a no-mount settles it for your unit. On the 1 and 1SE the drive should not mount at all — both list OTG No — so there is no phone route on those two, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size.
  4. Check the copy, then delete from the phone if you need the space. SnapDisk verifies every file's size after copying, but open a few photos on the drive yourself before deleting anything.

This is the only method in this article that takes the whole library in one tap, with progress, pause, resume and verification. Its limits: it works only on phones that run Android 7.0 and host a drive — and on six of the fifteen that clear 7.0 the sources do not agree, or do not say, that the phone hosts one, and on two more the sources agree it does not. On the six below Android 7.0, only Methods 2, 3 and 4 exist, since none of them can run SnapDisk.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 5: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Alcatel offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — no Alcatel-specific backup advice turned up in any source beyond a computer copy, Google Drive, or Google Photos — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 5 GB of free storage by default since May 2026, with the full 15 GB available only after verifying a phone number on the account. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1, 2, 3 or 4 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What this does not do

  • It does not back up your settings or your contacts. SnapDisk copies photos, videos, and any files you pick — the files themselves, exactly as they are. App settings, call logs, texts, and your accounts stay with your phone and your Google account. For those, Android's built-in backup is the tool — it backs up personal settings to Google's servers with a Google account — not SnapDisk.
  • It does not delete photos from your phone after copying. Deleting is always your choice, after you check the copy on the drive. SnapDisk never deletes originals — it only copies.
  • It does not work if your phone does not host a drive. On the 1 and the 1SE the sources agree the phone does not support OTG, so no backup app of any kind can copy your photos straight to a USB drive on those two. If the drive never mounts, that settles it for your unit — use Method 3 or 4 instead.
  • It does not back up photos from apps that hide them. Some apps keep photos in private folders SnapDisk cannot read. Those stay on the phone — check each app's own export or share option.
  • It does not compress your photos. It copies the original files byte-for-byte, so the copy is the same size as the original. On the A7, with its 16 MP camera, and the Idol 4S, with its 16 MP camera, that is real storage on the drive — bring a drive with room.

What to avoid

  • Don't move files instead of copying them. The file manager's Move deletes the original as it goes. Copy first, check the copy on the drive, and only then delete the originals from the phone if you need the space.
  • Don't unplug the drive until the copy is done. If the copy is still running — SnapDisk shows progress, speed and ETA — unplugging leaves half-copied files. Wait for it to finish, then eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it.
  • Don't buy an adapter before checking your phone's port. Twelve of the fifteen phones that clear Android 7.0 take micro-USB; only the Idol 5S, 5 and 7 take USB Type-C. A USB-C adapter does not fit a micro-USB port — and on the 3 (2018) the sources disagree about the connector itself, GSMArena's spec page saying microUSB and its MWC 2018 hands-on saying USB Type-C — so check your unit's port before buying anything.
  • Don't assume the drive will mount. On the 5V, 3L (2019) and 3X (2020) the sources disagree about OTG — GSMArena's USB row lists only microUSB 2.0 on the 5V, whatmobile lists OTG Yes; mobilegizmo lists USB OTG Support Yes on the 3L, manuals.plus lists it as not supporting OTG; smartprix lists USB on-the-go on the 3X, GSMArena's spec listing shows only microUSB 2.0 — so on those three the drive mounting is the test, not a given. On the 1S (2019), 1V (2019) and 3 (2019) no source states OTG at all, so the same applies there. And on the 1 and 1SE the sources agree it does not mount, so there is no phone route on those two at all — use Method 3 or 4.
  • Don't let one folder hold every photo. SnapDisk copies oldest-first, and the copy is the same size as the original, so a full camera roll on a phone with a 16 MP camera — the A7 or the Idol 4S — fills a small drive fast. Bring a drive with room, or back up in batches and check the copy before deleting anything.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a copy. A backup finds your photos, copies them all, and tells you when it's done. On the seven covered Alcatel phones that clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG — the A7, Idol 5, Idol 5S, 1X, 3 (2018), 5 and 7 — that's SnapDisk, in one tap. On the other eight that clear Android 7.0, the drive mounting is the test, or there is no phone route at all, and on the six below Android 7.0, the computer copy or the card-reader route is the backup.
