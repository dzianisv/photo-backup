Method 1: SnapDisk on the four (the one-tap route)

On the four phones that clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG, the automated route exists. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — the Jumbo, Jumbo 2 and Frames S7 run 7.0 exactly and the S9 Plus runs 9.0 — and all four list OTG:

  1. Install SnapDisk. Until the Play listing is live, install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page. It installs on Android 7.0 and later, so it installs on the four. It does not install on the other ten: they run Android 4.1 to 4.4.2, below the minimum, so Method 3 or 4 is the route on those.
  2. Plug in the USB drive with a micro-USB OTG adapter. All four take micro-USB, so a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. A USB-C flash drive does not plug straight into any of them.
  3. Tap Backup in the notification. SnapDisk offers the backup when the drive connects, then copies the whole camera roll oldest-first in the background, showing progress, speed and ETA, with Pause and Stop. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size.
  4. Check the copy, then delete from the phone if you need the space. SnapDisk verifies every file's size after copying, but open a few photos on the drive yourself before deleting anything.

This is the only method in this article that takes the whole library in one tap, with progress, pause, resume and verification. Its limits: it works only on phones that run Android 7.0 and host a drive — and ten covered Karbonn phones do neither. On those ten, Method 3 or Method 4 is the route.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: the file manager and an OTG adapter (works on the four)

This is the stock route, and the only phone route on the four besides SnapDisk. It works if — and only if — your phone hosts the drive, and OTG is listed on all four:

  1. Plug in the USB drive with a micro-USB OTG adapter. All four take micro-USB, so a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. A notification should appear saying the USB storage was added; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive.
  2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. No source found names the stock file manager on a Karbonn phone, so use Files by Google or the built-in file manager, whatever it is called on your unit.
  3. Go to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. On these phones the photos are the 13 MP shots from the rear camera on the Jumbo, Jumbo 2 and Frames S7 — the S9 Plus's cameras are not stated in the listings used here — plus whatever image files sit in the folders you pick.
  4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos.

This works fine for a one-off copy. Its limits are the reason a backup app exists on the four phones: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. And on the other ten it does not work at all as a phone route — they run Android 4.1 to 4.4.2, so even the Hexa, which lists OTG Supported, cannot run a modern file-manager backup flow to a drive without an app that installs on it.

Method 3: through a computer (the reliable route)

If a PC or Mac is handy, this is the route to trust: plug the Karbonn phone into the computer with its USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. This works on every covered model, with no Android-version question and no OTG question at all: the computer reads the phone as storage, and the drive plugs into the computer. It is also the only phone-to-drive route for the ten 2013–2015 models, which run Android 4.1 to 4.4.2 and cannot run SnapDisk at all. Its only cost is the second device in between: if the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route on the four, or Method 4 on the thirteen that take a card.

Method 4: the microSD card reader route (works on thirteen)

Thirteen of the fourteen covered Karbonn models take a microSD card — up to 128 GB on the Frames S7 and the S9 Plus, up to 64 GB on the Jumbo and the Jumbo 2, up to 32 GB on the rest that take one — and the card-reader route needs no OTG, no computer connection to the phone, and no app: 1. Power the phone off and remove the microSD card. 2. Put the card in a USB card reader and plug the reader into a computer, or into a USB drive with a built-in card slot. 3. Copy the DCIM folder from the card to the drive. 4. Put the card back and power the phone on. This is the same route the old phone manuals describe — remove the memory card, place it in a card reader, and let the reader transfer the pictures — and it works identically for a phone card. Two warnings: if the card holds photos the phone has not yet written to it, or photos stored on the phone's internal storage rather than the card, those photos are not on the card — check where your photos live first, in the camera app's storage setting or in the file manager. And the ceilings are disputed on two models — the Mach One's storage is 8 GB per gadgets360 and 16 GB per the Economic Times review, and the Mach Two's slot is up to 32 GB per gadgets360, the Times of India and phonebunch but even held a 64 GB card per the Mobile Indian's review — so check the card slot on your unit before buying a card larger than 32 GB for either of those two. The Hexa is the exception: it takes no microSD card at all, so this route does not exist on it — the computer copy in Method 3 is its only route.

Method 5: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Karbonn offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — no Karbonn-specific backup advice turned up in any source beyond a computer copy, Google Drive, or Google Photos — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 5 GB of free storage by default since May 2026, with the full 15 GB available only after verifying a phone number on the account. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1, 2, 3 or 4 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

What this does not do

A few honest limits, so the manual copy is not oversold:

  • No automated backup exists for a Karbonn phone below Android 7.0 — and ten covered phones are below it. The S5, S5 Plus, S5i, S7, S9, Titanium X, Hexa, Octane Plus, Mach One and Mach Two run Android 4.1 to 4.4.2, so SnapDisk cannot install on any of them, and neither can Photos to USB, VaultDrive or FotoSafe in any version that runs on a modern phone — the computer copy in Method 3 and the card-reader route in Method 4 are the routes on those ten.
  • The Hexa has no card-reader route either. It takes no microSD card at all, per phonebunch, so the computer copy in Method 3 is its only route — even though it lists OTG Supported, which changes nothing without an app that installs on Android 4.4.
  • Photos and videos only, from the folder you pick. By default that's DCIM — your camera photos. Photos in other folders (Downloads, app folders) only copy if you select them too, and photos stored on the phone's internal storage rather than the microSD card are not on the card for the Method 4 route.
  • Files over 4 GB on FAT32 are a problem. Very long videos on a FAT32 drive will not copy — copy those to a computer instead, or reformat the drive to exFAT, noting that Android 13 and later read exFAT natively, and no covered Karbonn phone runs Android 13, so FAT32 is the safer format for the phone route on all four.
  • It never deletes. The file manager's Copy only copies. Freeing space on the device is copy, check the copy on the drive, then delete the originals yourself — never the other way round.
  • The S9's OTG is disputed, not Yes. Phonebunch lists USB On-the-go and OTG Supported, but gadgets360 says USB OTG No. The dispute changes nothing for the backup — the S9 runs Android 4.2 and cannot run SnapDisk either way — but it is named here so the two listings are not mistaken for agreement.
  • The S9 Plus launch is two dates in the sources found. 20 January 2020 per one gadgetsnow comparison, 22 October 2020 per another gadgetsnow comparison. Either way it runs Android 9.0 and lists OTG, so the backup route is the same.
  • The Mach One's internal storage is two numbers in the sources found. 8 GB per gadgets360, 16 GB per the Economic Times review. Either way it runs Android 4.4 and cannot run SnapDisk, so the storage dispute changes nothing about the backup.
  • The Mach Two's microSD ceiling is two numbers in the sources found. Up to 32 GB per gadgets360, the Times of India and phonebunch, while the Mobile Indian's review says its slot even held a 64 GB card. Check the card slot on your unit before buying a card larger than 32 GB for that model.
  • The Jumbo 2's launch is two dates in the sources found. 1 February 2018 per gadgets360's comparison tables, announced 16 February 2018 per androidos.in and the Financial Express. Either way it runs Android 7.0 and lists OTG, so the backup route is the same.
  • The Mach Two's promised Lollipop upgrade does not change the backup. Pkgsm lists it as upgradable to 5.0, and Karbonn promised the update — but even Android 5.0 is below SnapDisk's 7.0 minimum, so the computer copy in Method 3 stays the route on it.
  • Two spec pages describe second phones that do not match the launch record. Phonepik lists a Titanium Jumbo released 15 June 2023 on Android 14 with a 6.67-inch display, and a Titanium S9 Plus released 15 June 2023 on Android 14 — against the October 2017 Jumbo launch on Android 7.0 with a 5-inch display and the 2020 S9 Plus launch on Android 9.0 that every other source lists. Neither listing is used as a source in this guide.
  • VaultDrive cannot run on three of the four. It needs Android 8 or later per its own site, so the Jumbo, Jumbo 2 and Frames S7 on Android 7.0 cannot run it — only the S9 Plus on Android 9.0 can — and AppBrain lists 12.1 or later, which no covered Karbonn phone clears, since the newest covered model runs Android 9.0.

  • No source found names the stock file manager on a Karbonn phone. Method 2 uses Files by Google or the built-in file manager, whatever it is called on your unit.
  • No source found states the S9 Plus's cameras, battery, processor or RAM in the listings used here. The gadgetsnow comparison used here gives its Android version, OTG, microSD ceiling, storage and port — the cameras and battery are not stated there, so they are not stated here either.

Ten covered Karbonn phones run below it — the S5, S5 Plus, S5i, S7, S9, Titanium X, Hexa, Octane Plus, Mach One and Mach Two — so check Settings > About phone if you are unsure: below Android 7.0, SnapDisk cannot install, and the computer copy in Method 3 is the route.

What to avoid

  • Buying a USB-C OTG adapter for a Karbonn Titanium phone. All fourteen covered models take micro-USB — there is no USB-C model in this guide — so a USB-C adapter does not fit any of them. Buy a micro-USB OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it.
  • Assuming the Hexa's OTG listing means a phone route exists on it. Phonebunch lists OTG Supported on the Hexa — but it runs Android 4.4, so SnapDisk cannot install on it, and it takes no microSD card either, so the computer copy in Method 3 is its only route.
  • Assuming the Mach Two's promised Lollipop upgrade fixes the backup. Karbonn promised Android 5.0 for the Mach Two, and pkgsm lists it as upgradable to 5.0 — but 5.0 is still below SnapDisk's 7.0 minimum, so the upgrade changes nothing about the backup.
  • Assuming the S9's OTG listing is settled. Phonebunch says OTG Supported and gadgets360 says OTG No. Either way the S9 runs Android 4.2 and cannot run SnapDisk, so the dispute is not a reason to buy an adapter for it.
  • Assuming a 2023 phonepik listing is the same phone. Phonepik lists a Titanium Jumbo and a Titanium S9 Plus released 15 June 2023 on Android 14 — but the Jumbo launched in October 2017 on Android 7.0 and the S9 Plus launched in 2020 on Android 9.0, per every other source. The 2023 listings are not used as sources here, and an Android 14 phone would not be a 2017 Jumbo.
  • Assuming VaultDrive or FotoSafe runs on an Android 7.0 Karbonn. VaultDrive needs Android 8 or later per its own site, and FotoSafe needs Android 9 — so neither runs on the Jumbo, Jumbo 2 or Frames S7, which run Android 7.0 exactly. Only the S9 Plus clears both floors.
  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The gadgets360 and phonebunch listings are real spec pages — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port type. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026 — and on the ten Android 4 models, that route does not exist, so the honest answer is that a computer is needed.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Karbonn phone the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
