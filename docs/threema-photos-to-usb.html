Back up Threema photos to a USB drive

Threema photos stay inside the app until you save them — auto-save is off by default, so turn it on once and the photos land in Pictures/Threema by themselves. Then the backup is a one-tap job.

November 25, 2026 · 13 min read

TL;DR: Threema keeps the photos you receive inside the app, in encrypted private storage — not in any folder on your phone — until you save them. Nothing in Threema saves them for you by default: the auto-save switch, Settings, Media, Save media, ships turned off, and Threema's own open-source code carries that default in plain text. So a photo you only viewed in a chat is not on your phone at all, and no backup app, file manager, or USB drive can reach it, because there is nothing to reach. Turn the switch on once and the job changes shape: every photo and video you receive from then on lands in your gallery by itself — photos in Pictures/Threema, videos in Movies/Threema, on Android 10 and newer — and from there the backup is the easy half. SnapDisk's default run reads Android's media index with no folder filter and copies the whole library — not DCIM only — so plug in the drive, tap Backup, and the saved Threema photos copy along with everything else, no computer. For the photos already in your chats, save them by hand: open a photo and tap the save icon, or long-press one message, tap the others, and save the selection together — Threema's save takes a list, so a whole chat's photos are one selection, not one tap per photo the way Kik's are. The honest limits, up front: the auto-save switch only covers photos that arrive after you turn it on, so the old chats still need the manual save; Threema deletes messages and media older than a year by default, so a photo left unsaved in an old chat is on a clock, not just in a queue; the encrypted Data Backup file is a restore file, not viewable photos — it can itself be copied to USB as a file, but a drive holding only that file holds no photos you can open; and screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it. As of November 25, 2026, no top search result offers a dedicated Threema-photos-to-USB how-to: Threema's own backup manual covers only the encrypted Data Backup file and Threema Safe, RESQ Toolbox is a PC program, and the rest are generic OTG copy guides that never name Threema at all.

The wall: Threema saves nothing by itself — auto-save is off by default, and a photo you only viewed lives in encrypted storage

Start with the fact that decides the whole job, because Threema's wall is quieter than Kik's and easier to miss. Kik at least makes the wall obvious: it never saves anything, ever, and the save is one tap per photo. Threema looks friendlier — it has an auto-save switch, and the switch has a plain name, Save media, sitting in Settings under Media — but the switch ships turned off. That is not an inference from a guide: Threema's own open-source code carries the default in the settings file, a switch whose default value is false. Threema's own Work guides name the switch too — "Automatically Save Media" in the First Steps guide, "Auto-save to gallery" in the app-configuration docs, where it is defined as whether media can be auto-saved to the device's local gallery or camera roll — and the independent guides agree it is a switch you turn on, not a behaviour you get: one writes that Threema can save incoming media to the phone folder if you turn that switch on, another that the setting will make them automatically save all media to your gallery. Until you turn it on, a photo you received sits in Threema's private app storage, encrypted on the device with AES-256 — the same storage the 2019 independent audit describes as app files neither world-readable nor world-writable, all encrypted — and no other app can see it. Threema's own design makes that airtight by intent: messages are deleted from its servers immediately after delivery, so there is no server copy to fall back on either.

That is the wall, and it is a total one in the same way Kik's is: a chat full of photos you looked at and never saved is a chat full of photos that do not exist anywhere outside Threema, and running SnapDisk's default backup on that phone copies every photo in the gallery — and zero Threema photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no Threema photos to copy. The difference from Kik is what happens once you notice. Kik gives you one tool for the backlog: one tap per photo, no switch, no selection. Threema gives you two, and the second one is the reason this article's first method is a switch, not a tapping session: turn Save media on and every photo and video that arrives from then on saves itself. The backlog still needs the manual save, and the manual save is the better half of Kik's deal too — Threema's save action takes a list of selected messages, so you long-press one photo, tap the others, and save the selection together, instead of opening each photo one at a time. A chat that has collected hundreds of photos over months is still a job, but it is a job of selections, not of single taps, and it is worth doing in sittings rather than all at once. Save the ones that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting, with one clock running that Kik does not have — see the next paragraph.

Two scope facts, because they decide what this article can and cannot promise. First, Threema deletes old media by itself: by default, all messages and media older than a year are automatically deleted, and the setting lives in Settings, Storage management, where you can choose a shorter interval or delete everything immediately. That is the urgency in this article, and it is different from Kik's: with Kik the risk is logging out or deleting the app; with Threema the risk is also time — a photo left unsaved in a chat older than a year is deleted by the app's own housekeeping, and no backup of any kind brings it back. The save is not housekeeping; it is the only copy that survives the year. Second, the save itself has a gate that is easy to miss: Threema can be configured to block saving and sharing media outside the app entirely — the app-configuration setting for external saving and sharing, which on Android applies to images, videos, and voice messages — and a work or managed Threema install may have that switch locked off by an administrator. If the save icon is missing or greyed out and the setting will not turn on, that is the likely cause, and no step in this article gets past an administrator's lock: the photos stay inside Threema until the policy changes. On a personal install the switch is yours, and it is off only because nobody turned it on.

And a word on whose photos these are, because Threema photos are usually your own conversations, not someone else's catalogue — and because Threema's users chose it for privacy, which deserves a straight answer. The photos this article covers are the ones people sent you — a picture a friend took, a photo from a group chat, an image someone shared that you want to keep — and the ones you sent, which you usually still have as originals. Saving them to your own drive for your own records is the job. They are also someone else's photos in a plain sense: a friend's picture of their own life, a photo someone sent you in confidence, quite possibly sent to you on Threema precisely because it does not leave the app by default. Saving a file does not grant permission to reuse it — so re-posting a photo someone sent you in a private chat, or sharing it onward as if it were yours, is a different act, and the same courtesy applies here as anywhere: the person who sent it is the person to ask before the photo leaves your phone for anywhere else. This article's methods copy the file to your drive; what you do with it afterwards is yours to decide, and asking is the honest step before anything leaves your phone. Only save photos you have permission to keep — and this article makes no claim about recovering photos from a chat you have already deleted, because Threema keeps no server copy to recover from, and its own storage management may already have deleted them.

Where saved Threema photos land — Pictures/Threema on modern Android, a legacy folder on old phones

This section exists because the folder is the one fact the guides do not answer at all — and because Threema is the one app in this series where the answer comes from the app's own source code instead of a guide. Threema for Android is open source, and its save-to-gallery code is public: when you save a photo, Threema's FileService writes it through Android's MediaStore, and the folder is set in code, not left to chance. On Android 10 and newer, an image goes to the Pictures directory with a Threema subfolder — Internal storage/Pictures/Threema — and a video goes to the Movies directory the same way — Internal storage/Movies/Threema. The code says so in two places that agree: the save function picks DIRECTORY_PICTURES for images and DIRECTORY_MOVIES for videos, then appends the app's media path, and the consumer app's build file sets that media path to the single word "Threema". Audio goes to Music/Threema, PDFs to Documents/Threema, and anything else to Downloads/Threema. That is the folder, exact, for every Threema photo saved on a modern phone — and it is a folder no guide in the search results names, because the guides only say "the gallery" or "the phone folder" and stop there.

On an older phone the folder is different, and that is the disagreement this article has to state honestly — not between guides, but between Android versions. Before Android 10, Threema wrote the files itself instead of going through the MediaStore, and the path was a folder at the top of internal storage: /Threema/Threema Pictures for photos and /Threema/Threema Videos for videos. The same code file carries both paths — the modern MediaStore branch and the legacy branch — so a phone on Android 9 or older saves to the legacy folder, and a phone on Android 10 or newer saves to Pictures/Threema. The generic storage guides add a third, vaguer answer that is still worth a line: Motorola's own help says photos saved from messages or photo-sharing apps land in Pictures/[app] — which is the same shape as Pictures/Threema, stated without the name. So this article does not state one path as the path for every phone. It gives the check order instead, and the check order is the same on every phone.

Check the gallery first. Open your gallery app and look for a "Threema" album. If it is there, the save works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see the photos, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If the album is not there yet, no photo has been saved: turn on auto-save as in Method 1, or save one photo, and look again. Then check Pictures/Threema in the file manager: Internal Storage, Pictures, Threema — the modern location for photos. Then check Movies/Threema for videos: Internal Storage, Movies, Threema — the modern location for videos, and the one people miss when they look for a video in the photos folder. Then check the legacy folder on an old phone: Internal Storage, Threema, Threema Pictures — and Threema Videos for videos. If the photo is in none of those places, check that the save actually happened: open the photo in the chat and look for the save confirmation, and check that auto-save is really on — Settings, Media, Save media — because the switch is off by default and a photo received before you turned it on was never saved.

One more scope fact for this section, because it decides what the drive copy holds: the saved file is the photo as Threema delivered it in the chat, and Threema gives the sender a choice about that. By default Threema sends images at Average quality — compressed, the same way every messenger does — but the sender can choose Original instead, in Settings under the image-size option, and then the photo arrives at full resolution. SnapDisk copies the saved file byte-for-byte, no conversion, no quality change — but it cannot recover quality Threema already removed, and it cannot upgrade a photo the sender sent compressed. If the photo you want is one you sent, the original is usually still in your camera roll in DCIM/Camera at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file the default run already covers; back that up instead. The Threema route for a photo you sent is only needed when the original is gone. And the photos you took inside Threema's own camera are a separate case again: they are sent from the chat the same way, and saving them follows the same save step — the camera does not put them in the gallery by itself.

What you need

An Android phone — Android 7.0 or later, which is SnapDisk's minimum version — with the Threema app installed and signed in, plus one USB drive your phone can read: a USB-C flash drive, or a regular USB drive with a USB-C OTG adapter. SnapDisk itself is installed from the v0.1.2 APK — it is not on Google Play yet — so install it by allowing "install unknown apps" for the browser or Files app you download it with, which is standard Android. You need nothing else for the main route: no file manager, no extra app, no account. The save step is inside Threema itself, and the backup is SnapDisk's default run. Check the drive's space first: Threema photos are small — a chat photo is a few megabytes at most, and Threema caps a single file at 50 MB — so space is almost never the problem here, but look anyway, because a whole-library default run copies everything, not just Threema. One format note: drives formatted FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB. That only matters for very long videos — SnapDisk skips those files and lists them instead of failing the whole backup. A drive formatted exFAT has no such limit, and exFAT is the format to use for a photo drive.

Before any method, settle the one question that decides whether the phone route works at all: does your phone see the USB drive? Plug the drive in and open Settings > Connected devices or Settings > Storage — the drive should appear there. That check is the test, and it takes ten seconds. If the drive does not appear, try a different adapter first — cheap OTG adapters are the usual failure — then a different drive, and a powered hub if the drive is a spinning hard drive rather than a flash drive. And one warning up front, because it applies to every route below: do not delete anything from the phone until the USB copy is done and verified. The photos in the chat are Threema's copy, and the photos in the gallery are yours — but until the drive copy is checked, the gallery is the only copy outside Threema, and deleting from the gallery before the check is how a photo ends up nowhere at all. And do not delete the Threema app, or let the one-year auto-delete run, until the save and the copy are both done: Threema keeps no server copy, and the in-chat photos are gone when the app's housekeeping deletes them.

Method 1: Turn on auto-save, save the backlog, then back up with SnapDisk

This is the route to use, because it is the only one that joins the whole job — the Threema photo, out of the chat and into the gallery, onto a USB drive plugged into the phone, no computer — using only the apps already on the phone plus SnapDisk. It has two halves, and they have to happen in order: the save, which puts the photos on the phone as files, and the SnapDisk copy, which puts them on the drive. The first half is the work, and with Threema it is two jobs, not one: the switch for everything that arrives from now on, and the manual save for everything already in the chats. Skipping the first half is the mistake this article exists to prevent: running SnapDisk's default backup with nothing saved copies every photo in the gallery — and zero Threema photos, because from the media index's point of view there are no Threema photos to copy.

  1. Turn on auto-save for everything from now on. In Threema, open Settings, go to Media, and turn on Save media — Threema's own guides call it "Automatically Save Media". That one switch is the whole future half of the job: every photo and video you receive from then on is saved to the gallery by itself, photos to Pictures/Threema and videos to Movies/Threema, with no tapping at all. Check it worked: ask someone to send you a photo, or wait for the next one, and look for the Threema album in your gallery. If the switch will not turn on, or the setting is greyed out, the likely cause is the managed-install lock in the wall section above — an administrator can disable saving media outside the app — and no step here gets past that. On a personal install the switch is yours.
  2. Save the photos already in your chats. Auto-save only covers photos that arrive after you turn it on, so the backlog needs the manual save. Open a chat, long-press one photo, then tap the others to add them to the selection — Threema's save takes a list of selected messages, so a whole chat's photos are one selection, not one tap per photo — and tap the save icon to write them all to the gallery at once. The other shape of the same tap is the single photo: open it full-screen and tap the save or download icon. Either way the result is the same — files in Pictures/Threema, videos in Movies/Threema — and either way it is worth doing in sittings rather than all at once. Save the ones that matter first; the rest can wait for the next sitting, but remember the clock: Threema deletes messages and media older than a year by default, so the oldest chats are the ones to save first. If a video will not save, screen-record it instead: open the video full-screen, start the phone's built-in screen recorder, play the video with the sound on, stop the recording, and trim the start and end in the gallery — a recording is a copy of the screen, not the video file, so it is lower quality and carries the app chrome around it, and Threema can block screenshots in a conversation when configured to do so, which may block the recording too.
  3. Check the Threema album appeared. Open your gallery app, go to Albums, and look for "Threema". If it is there, the save works — and this is also the check that proves the default run will see the photos, because the gallery and SnapDisk read the same media index. If the album is not there, work through the check order in the section above: Pictures/Threema, then Movies/Threema for videos, then the legacy Threema/Threema Pictures folder on an old phone. If the files are in the folder but not in the gallery, the phone's media scanner has not picked them up yet — a restart forces a rescan. If the Threema folder does not exist at all, the save is the suspect: check that auto-save is really on, or save one photo by hand and watch for the confirmation that it saved.
  4. Plug in the drive and run SnapDisk. Plug the USB drive into the phone. SnapDisk offers the backup in a notification — tap Backup — or open the app and tap Start. The run copies oldest-first, verifies every file by size after copying, and shows progress with Pause and Stop in the notification. The free run covers up to 5 GB, and Threema photos are small, so one run is almost always enough for the Threema half — though a whole-library run copies everything else too, and that is the point: the saved Threema photos come along with the rest, with no folder to pick and no step to remember. Screen-off continuation under battery saver is unverified — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling — so keep the phone awake for the run or check it.
  5. Do not plan around the photos you never saved. A photo you only viewed in the chat is still inside Threema, and no step in this article changes that: auto-save only covers what arrives after you turn it on, there is no save-all for a chat, and there is no export that writes the chat's photos out as files. If a photo matters, save it from the chat now, while the chat is still on this phone and the year has not run out — Threema's storage management deletes old media by default, and Threema keeps no server copy to recover from. A screenshot is the only copy left for a photo the built-in save will not give you — and a screenshot is a picture of the screen, not the photo: it is lower quality, it carries the app chrome around it, it lands in the Screenshots folder, not with the saved photos, and Threema can block it in a conversation when configured to do so. Do not plan a backup around saving what Threema will not let you save.

Two honest limits. First, the save is not retroactive in the other direction either: turning on auto-save today does not put the photos from a chat you already deleted onto the phone — a deleted chat is gone, and Threema keeps no server copy to recover from. That is step 2's job, and it is a manual job: one selection per chat, or the bulk route in Method 3 for the ones you are willing to take to a computer. Second, the 5 GB free cap is per run — a Threema album will never reach it, but a whole-library default run might, and running it again picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. Third, SnapDisk copies, it never deletes: deleting the photos from the gallery after the check is always your step, never the app's — and deleting them from the chat is Threema's, not SnapDisk's, and it does not free the drive copy. The drive copy is the backup; the gallery copy is the original until you decide otherwise.

Copying only some Threema photos? If you would rather copy just a few photos than the whole album, the From folder picker still takes a folder, not single files — so copy the ones you want into their own folder first (in the file manager, or by saving them to the gallery and selecting them there), then point the picker at that folder. The picker route keeps only recognised photo and video extensions. If the drive is unplugged mid-copy, the backup pauses with the full error shown and resumes where it stopped when the drive is back. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

And the check that matters for this article: after the run, compare the count against the album. A green backup that copied the whole library has still copied zero Threema photos if nothing was ever saved from the chat — which is exactly what happens when auto-save was never turned on and the backlog was never saved. Open the drive and look for the Threema folder by name: the default run mirrors the source tree, so the album should be there as its own folder. If it is missing, the save is the suspect, not the run — turn on auto-save and save the photos from the chat first, then run again. SnapDisk copies, it never deletes — the only files it ever deletes are its own half-finished .part files — so deleting the originals after the check is always your step, never the app's.

Method 2: Point the From picker at the Threema folder

If you would rather copy just the Threema photos and nothing else — and the photos are already saved — point SnapDisk's From folder picker at the folder that holds them. Open SnapDisk, tap the From folder picker, and navigate to the folder you found them in, using the check order in the folder section above: the gallery's Threema album first, then Pictures/Threema for photos, then Movies/Threema for videos — and tap Backup. To get everything in Pictures at once, pick the parent Pictures folder instead: SnapDisk walks the folder recursively, so one run copies the Threema folder and any other album that lives under Pictures together. The same 5 GB free cap applies, and the same oldest-first order, size verification, Pause and Stop.

The honest limit on this route is that it is the code-supported route, not the test-proven one — and this article says so rather than claiming a test that never ran — and the folder itself is the second honest limit. The picker is the system's folder picker, and the code has no Threema path handling anywhere — zero matches for "threema" in the whole source tree — so the navigation is yours, step by step, through folders whose names are ordinary. But the exact folder depends on the Android version — Pictures/Threema on Android 10 and newer, Threema/Threema Pictures on Android 9 and older — so this article does not state one folder path as the path, and no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet. Neither candidate name starts with a dot, so neither is hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and the folder sits in shared storage, not under Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks the Telegram folder does not apply here at all. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the parent Pictures folder reaches every album under it. But the end-to-end pick is untested, and this article marks it as such. If the picker will not show the folder on your phone, fall back to Method 1: save the photos to the gallery first, then run the default backup, which is the route that is proven to work. And one more scope fact for this route: the folder walk keeps only recognised photo and video extensions — a document in the folder is simply skipped, and a chat is never copied, because a chat is not a photo or a video. For a whole chat, use Method 3 — this article is only about the photo and video files.

One Threema-specific reason Method 2 matters more here than in the other messaging-app articles: the split between photos and videos. The default run reads the media index, and it reads both the images table and the videos table, so it covers Pictures/Threema and Movies/Threema together with no step beyond the save. The picker route does not: if you point it at Pictures/Threema you get the photos and not the videos, and the videos need a second pick at Movies/Threema — or one pick at the parent, which does not exist, because the two folders sit under different parents. That makes the picker the route for just the photos, and the default run the route for the whole Threema set. This is a code-grounded difference, not a guess: the default path queries both MediaStore tables, while the picker path walks one folder tree — and the split is Threema's own save code, which sends images and videos to different top-level directories. The pick itself is still untested end to end on a real phone, and this article marks it as such — but the reason the default run covers both halves where the picker covers one is the code's own two paths, stated as such.

Method 3: The encrypted Data Backup file — a copy of everything, and why it is not a photo backup

Threema has a bulk route of its own, and it is worth untangling carefully, because it is the first result for every Threema-backup search — and it is not this job. Threema's Data Backup, made in the app under Backups, Data Backup, writes a single password-protected encrypted file to a folder you choose on the device, and it holds your chats, groups, contacts, ID, and media files if you turn on "Include large media files" — Threema's own FAQ recommends creating a second backup without large media as well, because restoring a backup with large media can currently cause problems depending on the size. Threema Safe is different again and smaller: an anonymous encrypted backup of your ID, contacts, groups, and some settings, stored on Threema's server or a server of your choice — and it holds no chat histories and no media files at all. This article names both without endorsing either of them as a photo backup, because neither of them is one.

The difference is what the file is. The Data Backup file is one encrypted archive, not a folder of photos: you cannot open it and look at the pictures, and the pictures inside it are not files you can copy, share, or print. The file opens only by restoring it into Threema, and it restores only on the same operating system — Android to Android — so a backup made on an Android phone rebuilds nothing on an iPhone. That is a restore file, and it is a good one for what it is: the way to move a whole Threema life to a new phone, chats and all. But a drive holding only that file holds no photos you can open anywhere, and a person who copies it to USB thinking the photos are backed up has backed up a locked box, not the photos. The file itself can be copied to USB as a file — it is an ordinary file once created, so copy it to the drive with a file manager and keep the password with it, separately from the drive, because without the password the file is unreadable even to you — but that copy is the second backup, not the first. For photos you can open anywhere, save them to the gallery first — Method 1 — and copy them to a USB drive with SnapDisk, no computer. The two routes are not rivals; they are different shapes, and this article is about the plain-files one.

The PC route that exists alongside Threema's own backup is the RESQ Toolbox, and it is worth naming honestly for the same reason: it is the tool the search results offer when they offer anything Threema-specific at all. RESQ Toolbox is a free Windows app that backs up an Android phone's internal storage over a USB cable to a PC — photos, videos, music, downloads, documents, and the WhatsApp backup, plus Signal and Threema backups — and it needs USB debugging switched on in the phone's developer options. This article names it without endorsing it: it is a third-party tool with its own terms and its own permissions, and it was not tested this run. But it is not a phone-to-USB backup either, and the difference is where the files land. The Toolbox puts the phone's data on a computer: the files are in a backup on that computer, and they still need a second copy — computer to USB drive — before they are a backup you can open anywhere. So use the PC route for what it is: the way to get a whole phone's contents, including the Threema backup file, onto a computer in one sitting. For plain photo files on a drive you can open anywhere, save the photos to the gallery first — Method 1 — and copy them to a USB drive with SnapDisk, no computer. And the same permission rule applies to all three routes: only save photos you have permission to keep, and do not use a backup tool to pull someone else's photos out of a chat you were never meant to keep.

Can SnapDisk read the Threema folder directly?

This is the question the whole article turns on, and it deserves a straight answer in three parts — what the code proves it can do, what is untested, and what would prove the rest. The first part is proven, from the code at the current base and from how Android's media index works — and it is the same shape as the Kik and Viber cases, with one Threema-specific condition. SnapDisk's default run reads the phone's media index — it queries the MediaStore images and videos tables, oldest-first by date taken, with no folder filter. A Threema photo saved to the gallery is an ordinary indexed file: it shows in the gallery as a "Threema" album, and gallery apps read the media index. The album's existence in the gallery is the evidence the folder is indexed — and the folder itself is proven by Threema's own code, which writes saved images to Pictures/Threema and saved videos to Movies/Threema through the MediaStore, so the files arrive already indexed. The query is in PhotoQuery.kt, the no-folder-filter shape is the code's own design — the query call passes no selection at all — and the gallery evidence is the platform fact that closes the loop. So the default run sees saved Threema photos that show in the gallery, and it sees them with no step beyond the save. That is not an assumption: it is the code's query shape plus the gallery evidence, stated as such. And the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only — the query reads every indexed image and video on the phone, whatever folder it sits in, so the Threema album is covered the same way DCIM is, and the videos in Movies/Threema are covered the same way the photos are.

The condition is the auto-save switch, and it is the part that makes Threema different from Kik in this series: the default run's coverage is conditional on the photos having been saved, and with Threema the save is a switch that ships off. If auto-save was never turned on and the backlog was never saved by hand, the photos are still inside Threema's encrypted private storage, and the default run cannot see them — not because the code is wrong, but because the index it reads has nothing to return. Threema's own code carries the default in plain text, and the fix is its own: Settings, Media, turn on Save media, then save the backlog by hand. Until that is done, the default run copies zero Threema photos while the chats sit full of them. This article states the condition in the TL;DR, in the wall section, in Method 1 steps 1 and 2, and here — because a guide that promised the default run covers every Threema photo, without the condition, would be wrong on exactly the phones where the save looks broken. The second condition is the one-year clock: Threema deletes old media by default, so a photo in a chat older than a year may already be gone, and no backup of any kind recovers it.

The second part is untested, and this article marks it as such rather than guessing in either direction: whether SnapDisk's From folder picker reaches the Threema folder end to end on a real phone. The picker is the system's folder picker — ActivityResultContracts.OpenDocumentTree, in MainActivity.kt — and it opens with no folder pre-selected: there is no initial-folder hint anywhere in the code, and no Threema path handling anywhere in the app — zero matches for "threema" in the whole source tree. The navigation chain is ordinary folders — the gallery album, Pictures/Threema, Movies/Threema, or the legacy Threema/Threema Pictures — none of them dot-prefixed, so none of them is hidden from the picker the way a dot-folder would be, and the folder sits in shared storage, outside Android/data, so the scoped-storage wall that blocks the Telegram folder does not apply here. And the folder walk recurses into every subfolder it can list, so picking the parent Pictures folder should reach every album under it. But the exact folder depends on the Android version, and no SnapDisk test has picked this exact folder yet, so the end-to-end pick is untested — do not plan a backup around it as the only route, and do not assume it fails either. The joined workflow in Method 1 is the route that is proven to work, and it is the route this article gives first. The picker has one Threema-specific limit the default run does not, stated in Method 2: it walks one folder tree, so Pictures/Threema gives the photos and Movies/Threema needs a second pick — but that limit is itself untested end to end, and this article marks it as such too.

The third part is what the code does prove about the rest of the job, so the limits above are not mistaken for a wider doubt. The manifest requests the media-read permissions (READ_MEDIA_IMAGES and READ_MEDIA_VIDEO, plus the older READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE capped at Android 12), the notification permission, and foreground-service permissions — and no INTERNET permission at all, zero occurrences, so the photos physically cannot leave the device. It does not request all-files access — no MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE anywhere — which is the permission a file manager uses to browse another app's folders freely, and the absence is the reason SnapDisk cannot simply walk into Threema's private storage the way a file manager can; the folder picker is the route instead, and it needs your tap. The backup itself is the verified core: oldest-first by date taken with a date-modified fallback (PhotoQuery.kt), every file size-verified after copying (BackupEngine.kt), Pause and Stop in the notification (NotificationHelper.kt, BackupService.kt), files of 4 GB or more on a FAT32 drive skipped with their reason instead of stalling the job (BackupService.kt), and the free run capped at 5 GB with Pro a one-time $9.99 purchase (ProPrefs.kt). None of that changes the picker verdict — it bounds it. The backup engine is proven; the end-to-end pick of this exact folder is not yet tested.

Photos you sent vs photos you received

The route is the same for both, but the starting point is not, and it is worth saying which one you are saving before you start. Photos you received — the case this article is really about — are the ones the save governs: with the save they land in the gallery, and without it they are not on the phone at all, and the methods above are written for them. Photos you sent are different, because you usually have a better original: a photo you sent on Threema is almost certainly still in your camera roll, in DCIM/Camera, at full quality, as an ordinary indexed file that SnapDisk's default run already covers — and the default run copies the whole library, not DCIM only, so it is covered either way. Back that up instead. The copy in the chat is a version Threema made for sending — smaller and compressed, unless the sender chose Original quality in the image-size setting — and the original on your camera roll is the one worth keeping. That matters more than it sounds, because the photos you send are often the ones you care about most: a picture of the finished work for a client, a photo of the goods before dispatch, the shot you took to show someone what something looks like. The original is the one they will ask for again. The Threema route for a photo you sent is only needed when the original is gone — you deleted it from the camera roll, or it was a photo you took inside Threema's camera and never saved — and then the chat copy is the only one left: save it from the chat the same way as a received photo, and the default run picks it up like any other indexed photo, or point the From picker at the folder and copy it directly.

One related corner is worth a line, because Threema users hit it and it looks like a shortcut that is not one: Threema keeps its working data under Android/data, and a guide may tell you to dig in Android/data for a cached photo. That is a different thing from saved photos — the cached copies are fragments keyed to the installation, encrypted with the app's own keys, not your photos in any usable sense — and Android/data is guarded by scoped storage, so a file manager may not even show it. Do not go digging in Android/data for your Threema photos: they are not there. The photos are in the gallery once saved, or nowhere at all when they are not, and those are the only two places this article's methods touch. And one more Threema-specific corner: a photo sent as a file, rather than as a photo, is a different thing again — Threema lets you send files in any format up to 50 MB, and a photo sent that way saves to Downloads/Threema, not Pictures/Threema, and SnapDisk's folder walk skips it only if it is not a recognised photo extension — a JPEG sent as a file is still a JPEG, and the picker walk keeps it, while the default run sees it only if the media scanner indexed it, which it does for a file in Downloads. If a photo you are missing was sent as a file, check Downloads/Threema first.

Checking the drive after the copy

The check has two halves, and they have to happen in order: first that the save actually produced a file in the gallery, then that the drive holds it. After the SnapDisk run finishes, open the drive on a computer or on the phone itself and count: the drive should hold the Threema photos you saved, in a Threema folder of their own — the default run mirrors the source tree, so the album should be there by name — and a few opened at random should render fully, videos included. Open a Threema photo itself — not just any photo on the drive — because a run that covered the rest of the library would still look complete on a random check while the one album you cared about was missing. If the count is short, the run is not the first suspect — the save is. Check the gallery: is the "Threema" album actually there, and does it hold the photos you expected? If the album is empty, the photos were never saved — turn on auto-save, save the ones that matter from the chat, and run again. If the album holds them and the drive does not, the run is the suspect, and the run needs repeating, not a different folder. If the files are in the Threema folder but not in the gallery, the media scanner is the suspect: restart the phone to force a rescan, and run again — or pick the folder directly with the From picker, which does not depend on the gallery.

If the drive's free space ran out mid-run, the run stops with the full error shown; free space by moving the drive's contents to a bigger drive first, or back up to a second drive. If the run stopped at the 5 GB free cap — unlikely for a Threema album alone, possible on a whole-library default run — run it again and it picks up where it stopped, because files already copied are never copied twice. And the check that matters most for a photo that only existed inside Threema: confirm the drive copy before you delete anything from the phone. The copy in the gallery is an ordinary file and will survive; the copy in the chat depends on Threema keeping it, and the one-year auto-delete ends that. The drive is a plain folder of plain files. Anyone who picks up the drive can open them. That is the price of a copy you can open anywhere, and it is worth stating before the photos leave the phone — especially for Threema, where the photos were inside an encrypted app precisely because someone wanted them to stay private.

What to avoid

  • Assuming Threema saves the photos you receive. It does not — not by default. The Save media switch ships turned off, and a photo you only viewed lives in Threema's encrypted private storage, not in any folder. A one-tap backup copies zero Threema photos until you turn the switch on or save them from the chat first.
  • Assuming auto-save covers the photos already in your chats. It does not — the switch only covers photos that arrive after you turn it on. The backlog needs the manual save: long-press one photo, tap the others, save the selection together.
  • Assuming a deleted chat can be recovered. It cannot — Threema deletes messages from its servers immediately after delivery, and it keeps no server copy to recover from. Save the photos that matter while the chat is still on this phone.
  • Assuming old photos wait for you. They do not — Threema deletes messages and media older than a year by default. A photo left unsaved in an old chat is on a clock, not just in a queue; save the oldest chats first.
  • Assuming the Data Backup file is a photo backup. It is not — it is a single encrypted restore file that opens only by restoring it into Threema, on the same operating system. A drive holding only that file holds no photos you can open. Save the photos to the gallery first, and keep the backup file as the second, encrypted copy.
  • Assuming Threema Safe holds your photos. It does not — Threema Safe backs up your ID, contacts, groups, and some settings, and it holds no chat histories and no media files at all. It rebuilds the account, not the photos.
  • Assuming one folder path for saved Threema photos. UNVERIFIED as a single path — the folder depends on the Android version and the file type: Pictures/Threema for photos and Movies/Threema for videos on Android 10 and newer, Threema/Threema Pictures and Threema/Threema Videos on Android 9 and older, Downloads/Threema for a photo sent as a file. Check the gallery first, then Pictures/Threema, then Movies/Threema, then the legacy folder — do not plan a backup around a single path.
  • Assuming the default SnapDisk run covers photos you only viewed. It cannot — proven from the code and the platform: the default run reads the media index, and a photo that was never saved never enters the index. A green backup copies zero Threema photos from chats you never saved from.
  • Assuming the default run covers videos if you only picked the photos folder. It does not — that is the picker route, not the default run. Pointing the From picker at Pictures/Threema copies the photos and not the videos, which live in Movies/Threema and need a second pick. The default run covers both, because it reads both MediaStore tables.
  • Assuming the From picker route is test-proven. Untested — no SnapDisk test has picked the Threema folder yet, and the exact folder depends on the Android version. The code supports it and the folder names are ordinary, but do not plan a backup around it as the only route; Method 1's save-to-gallery-first workflow is the proven one.
  • Assuming the chat copy is the original. A photo you sent is usually still in DCIM/Camera at full quality. The chat copy is a smaller, compressed version Threema made for sending, unless the sender chose Original quality; back the original up, and use the chat copy only when the original is gone.
  • Looking in Android/data for your Threema photos. That folder holds Threema's working data — encrypted fragments keyed to the installation, not your photos — and scoped storage guards it. The photos are in the gallery once saved, or nowhere at all when they are not.
  • Using a PC backup tool and thinking the USB job is done. The tool puts the Threema data on a computer, not on a drive — the files land in a backup on that computer and still need a second copy to reach a USB drive. The USB leg is still SnapDisk's job — the tool replaces nothing in this article.
  • Using a screenshot and thinking it is the photo. It is not — a screenshot is a picture of the screen, lower quality, carrying the app chrome around it, and it lands in the Screenshots folder, not with the saved photos. Threema can also block screenshots in a conversation when configured to do so. Save the photo itself when the save works, and screenshot only when it does not.
  • Assuming screen-off continuation under battery saver. UNVERIFIED — the repo holds no wake lock and no battery-saver handling. Keep the phone awake for the run, or check it.
  • Using a FAT32 drive for a library with long videos. FAT32 cannot hold a single file over 4 GB, and a library with long 4K clips will hold one. SnapDisk skips those files and lists them — use exFAT.
  • Assuming the free 5 GB cap covers the whole library. It is per run. A library over 5 GB needs several free runs — run it again and it picks up where it stopped — or one $9.99 Pro purchase.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this. An unplug mid-copy leaves a half-copied library that looks finished until you count it.
  • Checking the drive by opening any photo. Open a Threema photo itself — not just any photo on the drive. A random check passes even when the one album you cared about was never copied, because the photos that did copy render fine.

Rule of thumb: Turn the switch on, then back it up — Threema saves nothing by itself, auto-save is off by default, and the gallery is the bridge to the drive. The switch is one tap for everything from now on; the backlog is one selection per chat; the backup after it is one tap for all of them.
