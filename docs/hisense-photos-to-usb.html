Method 1: SnapDisk on the two (the one-tap route)

On the two phones that clear Android 7.0 and list USB OTG, the automated route exists. SnapDisk needs Android 7.0 or later — the A2 Pro runs 7.1 and the A6 runs 8.0 — and the one-tap backup needs the phone to host a drive, which both list. The other six phones that clear 7.0 — the C30 Rock with OTG half-stated, and the C30 Lite, A5, A5 Pro CC, A6L and A7 with no OTG stated — have the drive mounting as the test:

  1. Install SnapDisk. Until the Play listing is live, install the v0.1.2 APK from the GitHub release page. It installs on Android 7.0 and later, so it installs on the eight phones that clear 7.0 — the C30 Rock, C30 Lite, A2 Pro, A5, A5 Pro CC, A6, A6L and A7 — and the one-tap backup to a drive works on the two that list USB OTG, the A2 Pro and A6. On the other six, SnapDisk installs but the one-tap route is unproven until a drive mounts. Four of the ten — the A5, A5 Pro CC, A6L and A7 — ship with no Google Play at all, so on those the APK sideload stays the install route even once the Play listing is live. It does not install on the other two: they run Android 5.0 and 6.0, below the minimum, so Method 3 or 4 is the route on those.
  2. Plug in the USB drive with the right adapter. The A2 Pro and A6 take USB Type-C, so a USB-C flash drive plugs straight into those two, or a regular USB drive needs a USB-C OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. On the C30 Rock, C30 Lite and A5 the adapter is micro-USB: those three take micro-USB and need a micro-USB OTG adapter. On the A5 Pro CC, A6L and A7 a USB-C drive plugs straight in too. On the C20 no source states the connector, so check your unit's port before buying an adapter for it.
  3. Look for the mount notification, then tap Backup in the notification. If the drive mounts, SnapDisk offers the backup when the drive connects, then copies the whole camera roll oldest-first in the background, showing progress, speed and ETA, with Pause and Stop. If nothing mounts on the C30 Rock, C30 Lite, A5, A5 Pro CC, A6L or A7, the phone does not host the drive — the sources are half-stated or silent on all six, so a no-mount settles it for your unit, and Method 3 or 4 is the route. The free version backs up manually up to 5 GB per run; Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase for auto-backup on connect and unlimited size.
  4. Check the copy, then delete from the phone if you need the space. SnapDisk verifies every file's size after copying, but open a few photos on the drive yourself before deleting anything.

This is the only method in this article that takes the whole library in one tap, with progress, pause, resume and verification. Its limits: it works only on phones that run Android 7.0 and host a drive — and on six of the eight that clear 7.0 the sources do not agree, or do not say, that the phone hosts one. On the two below Android 7.0, only Methods 2 and 3 exist, since both list OTG Yes but neither can run SnapDisk.

The copies are the original files. SnapDisk streams each file byte-for-byte from the device to the drive — the code reads the file and writes the same bytes out, with no decode, no recompression, and no resize — and then checks that the copy on the drive is the same size as the original. Other apps copy originals byte-for-byte too; what SnapDisk adds is the automated whole-library run: oldest-first order, progress with Pause and Stop, resume where it stopped, and skip-if-already-copied on the next run. If a file is corrupt or too big for FAT32, it's retried once, then skipped with its reason listed — it never stalls the whole backup. The free version backs up manually, up to 5 GB per backup run, with no ads, no account, and no cloud. SnapDisk Pro is a one-time $9.99 purchase (no subscription) that starts the backup automatically when you plug in a disk and removes the size limit. The app has no internet permission, so your photos physically cannot leave your device.

Method 2: the file manager and an OTG adapter (works wherever the drive mounts)

This is the stock route, and the only phone route besides SnapDisk. It works if — and only if — your phone hosts the drive. On the A2 Pro and A6 it should mount; on the C30 Rock, C30 Lite, A5, A5 Pro CC, A6L and A7 it is the test; and it is also the phone route on the two below-7.0 phones, both of which list OTG Yes — the C20 and the A2 — so on those two the drive should mount too, with no SnapDisk involved:

  1. Plug in the USB drive with an OTG adapter. On the A2, A2 Pro, A5 Pro CC, A6, A6L and A7 that is a USB-C OTG adapter, or a USB-C flash drive plugged straight in, with the OTG wiring inside the adapter — a plain sync cable is the wrong cable, it connects the phone to a computer, which is the opposite direction. On the C30 Rock, C30 Lite and A5, which take micro-USB, a regular USB drive needs a micro-USB OTG adapter. On the C20 no source states the connector, so check your unit's port first. A notification should appear saying the USB storage was added; tap it to open the drive. If nothing appears, check Settings > Storage for the drive — and if the drive never shows up, the phone does not host it, and Method 3 or 4 is the route.
  2. Open the file manager and confirm the USB drive shows up under storage devices. The pre-installed file manager on a Hisense phone is called Files, or Files by Google, per devicesfaq — use it, or the built-in file manager, whatever it is called on your unit.
  3. Go to Internal storage, DCIM, Camera, where your camera photos live. On these phones the photos are the 13 MP shots from the rear camera on the C20, the 16 MP shots on the C30 Rock, the 16 MP shots on the A2, the 12 MP shots on the A2 Pro, the 13 MP shots on the A5, the 24 MP shots on the A6 per chinacoupon — a single source — and the 16 MP shots on the A7 per ereaderx — 13 MP per liliputing's A7 CC piece — plus whatever image files sit in the folders you pick. The C30 Lite's, A5 Pro CC's and A6L's cameras are unstated in the sources found.
  4. Select the photos (long-press the first, tap the rest, or select all). Tap Copy, then select the USB drive, and wait for the copy to finish — don't unplug the drive until the file manager says it's done. Eject the drive from the notification or from Settings > Storage before unplugging it. Use Copy, not Move: Move deletes the originals as it goes, so a bumped cable halfway through loses photos.

This works fine for a one-off copy, if the drive mounts at all. Its limits are the reason a backup app exists on the two phones: you pick files one by one, there is no progress detail beyond a spinner, no resume if you unplug mid-copy, no verification that every file actually arrived, and you have to repeat the whole manual selection next time photos pile up. And on the C30 Rock, C30 Lite, A5, A5 Pro CC, A6L and A7 the drive mounting is the test, since their OTG is half-stated or unstated.

Method 3: through a computer (the reliable route)

If a PC or Mac is handy, this is the route to trust: plug the Hisense phone into the computer with its USB cable, tap the USB notification and choose File transfer — and drag the DCIM folder onto the USB drive. This works on every covered model, with no Android-version question and no OTG question at all: the computer reads the phone as storage, and the drive plugs into the computer. It is also the only phone-to-drive route for the two models below Android 7.0, which run Android 5.0 and 6.0 and cannot run SnapDisk at all, and for the six where the drive mounting is the test, if the drive never mounts — and it is the route devicesfaq names for Hisense backup, calling the computer copy the most economical method. Its only cost is the second device in between: if the phone and the drive are all you have, Method 1 or 2 is the route on the two, or Method 4 on five of the ten.

Method 4: the microSD card reader route (works on five)

Five covered Hisense models take a microSD card — up to 64 GB on the A5, a shared SIM slot on the A6 with a ceiling of up to 128 GB per phoneinfo and up to 256 GB per goodereader's A6L piece, up to 256 GB on the A6L and the A7, and the A2 Pro, whose card is implied by devicesfaq's advice to pass media files to a memory card, with no ceiling stated — and the card-reader route needs no OTG, no computer connection to the phone, and no app: 1. Power the phone off and remove the microSD card. 2. Put the card in a USB card reader and plug the reader into a computer, or into a USB drive with a built-in card slot. 3. Copy the DCIM folder from the card to the drive. 4. Put the card back and power the phone on. This is the same route the old phone manuals describe — remove the memory card, place it in a card reader, and let the reader transfer the pictures — and it works identically for a phone card. Three warnings: if the card holds photos the phone has not yet written to it, or photos stored on the phone's internal storage rather than the card, those photos are not on the card — check where your photos live first, in the camera app's storage setting or in the file manager. The ceilings are disputed on one model — the A6's slot is up to 128 GB per phoneinfo and up to 256 GB per goodereader's A6L piece — so check the card slot on your unit before buying a card larger than 128 GB for that model. And the A2 Pro's card is implied, not stated — devicesfaq tells its owners to pass media files to a memory card, but no source states the slot or its ceiling — so check your unit's SIM tray for a card slot before counting on it there. Unlike Elephone, where fourteen of fifteen take a card, five covered Hisense phones clearly or impliedly take one — so this route exists on five, with the other five unproven.

Method 5: Google Photos, Google Drive, or Google backup — not phone-to-USB plain files

Hisense offers no backup tool to a USB drive at all — no Hisense-specific backup advice turned up in any source beyond a computer copy, Google Drive, or Google Photos — so the stock options are Google's, and none of them writes your photos as plain files to a USB drive automatically. 1. Google Photos is Google's own photo backup app: it automatically backs up photos to your Google account. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and photos sync through Google Photos instead; and its "Storage saver" setting compresses photos (to a maximum of 16 megapixels, videos to 1080p). 2. Google Drive is the cloud backup Android names by default — it backs your data up to Google's cloud. It is a real backup — but it is a cloud backup, not a USB copy, and Google accounts only offer 5 GB of free storage by default since May 2026, with the full 15 GB available only after verifying a phone number on the account. 3. Google backup is what Android itself names: personal settings back up to Google's servers with a Google account, and are restored to a new phone when you sign in with that account. It is a real backup — but it backs up settings, not a folder of photo files on a drive you hold. None of them is a one-tap way to copy your camera roll to a drive as plain files. All of them work fine for what they are. Their limit, for photos specifically: none of them gives you a folder of plain photo files on a USB drive you can plug into any computer and open. If what you want is a copy of your photos you can open anywhere, Method 1, 2, 3 or 4 is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026.

Two covered Hisense phones run below it — the C20 on 5.0, 5.1 per versus.com, and the A2 on 6.0 — so check Settings > About phone if you are unsure: below Android 7.0, SnapDisk cannot install, and the computer copy in Method 3 is the route.

What to avoid

  • Buying a USB-C OTG adapter for a micro-USB Hisense phone. Three covered models take micro-USB — the C30 Rock, C30 Lite and A5 — so a USB-C adapter does not fit any of them. Buy a micro-USB OTG adapter, with the OTG wiring inside it, unless your unit is one of the six USB-C models — and on the C20, check your unit's port first, since no source states its connector.
  • Assuming the C30 Rock's, C30 Lite's, A5's, A5 Pro CC's, A6L's or A7's OTG listing is settled. Mobilexfiles lists OTG only in the C30 Rock's secret-code hardware-test menu, phonesdata lists only USB Yes for the C30 Lite, and no source states OTG for the A5, A5 Pro CC, A6L or A7 at all. Plug a drive in and look for the mount notification before counting on the one-tap route on any of the six.
  • Assuming the A2 Pro's card slot is settled. Devicesfaq tells A2 Pro owners to pass media files to a memory card, but no source states the slot or its ceiling directly, and phonesdata's card-slot field is blank. Check your unit's SIM tray for a card slot before counting on the card-reader route on it.
  • Assuming the C20, C30 Rock, C30 Lite, A2 or A5 Pro CC takes a microSD card. No source found states a card slot for any of the five. Either way the C20 and A2 run below Android 7.0 and cannot run SnapDisk, so the card route does not exist on them until a slot is confirmed — the computer copy in Method 3 is the route, plus Method 2 since both list OTG Yes — and on the C30 Rock, C30 Lite and A5 Pro CC the computer copy is the route until a slot is confirmed, plus Method 2 if the drive mounts.
  • Assuming the A6's Android 9.0, 6.53-inch display or 3800 mAh battery is settled. Goodereader's A6 review lists Android 9.0, a 6.53-inch IPS display and a 3800 mAh battery, but it describes the A6L's hardware — phonesdata lists Android 8.0 with a 6.01-inch AMOLED display, and UL benchmarks and chinacoupon list a 3300 mAh battery. Either way it runs Android 8.0 or 8.1 and lists OTG Yes, so the mix-up changes nothing about the backup.
  • Assuming the A6's 256 GB ceiling is settled. Phoneinfo says up to 128 GB, goodereader's A6L piece says up to 256 GB. Check the card slot on your unit before buying a card larger than 128 GB for that model.
  • Assuming the A2 Pro runs a Snapdragon 660 with 6 GB of RAM. Namu.wiki's Yotaphone page describes the A2 Pro that way, but notebookcheck, gizmochina and phonesdata all say Snapdragon 625 with 4 GB — the 660 with 6 GB reads like the Yota 3+. Either way it runs Android 7.1 and lists OTG Yes, so the mix-up changes nothing about the backup.
  • Assuming the A5 runs a Snapdragon 660. Projects-raspberry and electronics-lab both say Qualcomm 660 in one paragraph and Snapdragon 439 everywhere else on the same pages, and MobileRead, devicesfaq and goodereader all say Snapdragon 439. Either way it runs Android 9.0 and SnapDisk installs on it, so the mix-up changes nothing about the backup.
  • Assuming the A7's 16 MP rear camera is settled. Ereaderx says 16 MP, liliputing's A7 CC piece says 13 MP. Either way it runs Android 10 and SnapDisk installs on it, so the mix-up changes nothing about the backup.
  • Assuming VaultDrive or FotoSafe runs on an Android 7.0 or 7.1 Hisense. VaultDrive needs Android 8 or later per its own site, and FotoSafe needs Android 9 with OTG — so neither runs on the C30 Rock, C30 Lite or A2 Pro, and FotoSafe runs on none of the ten with its OTG condition proven, since the four that clear Android 9 list no OTG.
  • Mistaking a spec sheet or a product page for a phone-to-USB guide. The phonesdata, notebookcheck and goodereader listings are real spec pages — but they only list an Android version, a processor, a camera, or a USB port type. None of them describes copying your camera roll straight to a USB drive from the device.
  • Mistaking a drive-locked app for a general backup. Photo Backup Stick DC is a real one-tap backup app — but it only works with genuine Photo Backup Stick OTG drives. If the drive you already own is any other brand, that app cannot use it: SnapDisk, Photos to USB, and FotoSafe work with any drive.
  • PC-only tools when you have no computer. Drag-and-drop through a Windows PC works — but it needs the PC in between. If the device and the drive are all you have, a phone-to-USB copy is the route — that is the gap this article fills, as of November 19, 2026 — and on the two models below Android 7.0, and the six where the drive mounting is the test, that route may not exist, so the honest answer is that a computer may be needed.
  • Cloud backup dressed as USB backup. If the app needs an account and an upload before your photos are safe, that's a cloud subscription with a USB step. Your photos sit on someone else's server, and the backup stops working the day you stop paying. On a Hisense phone the cloud routes are Google Photos and Google Drive — and Google Photos' "Storage saver" setting compresses photos, which is the one place quality actually gets lost.
  • Deleting before verifying. A backup is only a backup once you've confirmed the copy. Copy, open a few photos on the drive, then delete from the device — never the other way round. SnapDisk only copies — it never deletes — so the delete step is always yours, and always after the check.
  • Moving instead of copying. The file manager's Move option copies and then deletes the originals in one step. If the copy fails halfway, photos are already gone from the device. Copy first, verify, then delete.
  • Unplugging mid-copy. Pulling a USB drive out while files are still writing can corrupt the file in flight. Wait for the copy to finish, or pause it first — SnapDisk's Stop button exists for exactly this.

Rule of thumb: if you have to pick the files yourself, it isn't a backup — it's a chore you will skip. Automate the copy, check it on the drive, then delete from the phone.
